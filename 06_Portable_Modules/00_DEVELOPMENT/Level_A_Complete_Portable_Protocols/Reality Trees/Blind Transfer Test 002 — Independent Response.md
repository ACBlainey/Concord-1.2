## 4A. Root

A volunteer community group must provide an evening meal for approximately 30 people by about 7:00 pm, with:

- a £240 budget;
- four available food-preparation/setup volunteers;
- a small domestic-style kitchen;
- one known gluten-free requirement;
- three vegetarian attendees;
- one nut allergy;
- unknown preferences for the remaining attendees;
- no current supplier prices, no selected menu, and no requirement that everyone eat exactly the same food.

The organiser personally likes pizza and strongly prefers the familiarity of ordering it. One volunteer prefers a homemade meal. Another volunteer wants the lowest-work option.

**Root question:**  
What materially different food approaches could be practical, reasonably inclusive and manageable for this event, and how do personal preference, practical/evidential support, and contextual decision weight differ without collapsing them into one answer?

This root does not assume that pizza, homemade food, or lowest-work ordering is the correct answer.

### Preference separation demonstration

A branch can be personally preferred without being best supported by available practical evidence:

- **Branch A** is personally preferred by the organiser, but the supplied evidence does not establish that current pizza ordering is affordable, that suitable gluten-free/vegetarian/nut-safe options exist, or that it is the most inclusive option.
- **Branch C** is personally preferred by the cooking volunteer, but the supplied practical/contextual information creates significant uncertainty about effort, kitchen capacity, time and allergy/GF safety.

A branch can also have practical or contextual support without being personally preferred:

- **Branch B** or **Branch E** may better address inclusion or low-work constraints, but no supplied person strongly prefers them.

Therefore:

> Personal preference weight ≠ evidential/practical support ≠ contextual decision weight.

## 4C. Recursive decomposition

I decompose **Branch B — Varied prepared order**, because “varied prepared order” is broad and hides materially different coordination, inclusion and familiarity profiles.

### Sub-branches of B

| ID | Sub-branch | Description | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | Single-supplier mixed menu | Order all prepared food from one supplier with gluten-free, vegetarian and nut-safe options. | Simpler coordination and one collection/delivery point, but supplier may not cover all dietary needs well. |
| B2 | Multiple prepared orders | Order from more than one supplier, e.g. pizza plus separate dietary-safe items. | Better dietary coverage, but more coordination, cost tracking and collection/delivery burden. |

Decomposition improves the decision because the broad label “prepared order” does not show whether pizza is the whole meal, whether dietary needs are handled by the same supplier, or how much coordination is required. These distinctions materially affect budget uncertainty, volunteer effort, inclusion and the organiser’s preference.

## 10. Method critique of v0.2

### Unclear instructions

- The specification does not define “materially distinct” branch very precisely. This can lead to either too few or too many branches.
- “Contextual decision weight” is broad and could include budget, time, effort, risk, preference and inclusion. It is useful but needs clearer sub-dimensions in some applications.
- The relationship between “current confidence/status” and “current disposition” is not fully specified. A branch could be “uncertain” and “active,” or “weakly supported” and “deferred,” and the difference is not always obvious.
- The update process does not prescribe a notation for showing what changed and why.

### Missing operational guidance

- No worked example is included in the specification itself.
- No guidance is given on how to decide when recursive decomposition should stop.
- No minimum record is specified for sub-branches.
- No explicit method is given for handling conflicting weighting dimensions without aggregating them.
- No guidance is given on how to record unchanged branches after a context update.

### Unnecessary complexity

- The branch record table has many columns. It is useful for consequential decisions, but could be heavy for simple personal use.
- Multiple weighting dimensions are powerful but can become confusing if the user does not know which dimensions matter for the current question.
- The specification correctly says not every cell must be filled, but the full table may still encourage over-documentation.

### Risks of misuse

- **False precision:** using qualitative labels as if they were probabilities.
- **Preference leakage:** treating a personally preferred branch as if it had stronger evidential support.
- **Branch proliferation:** generating many possible meals without prioritising.
- **Exploration-as-endorsement:** treating a retained devil’s-advocate branch as the user’s actual belief.
- **Unsafe escalation:** moving from planning to real-world allergy-sensitive food preparation without appropriate safeguards.
- **Failure erasure:** deleting the homemade branch after the oven update rather than recording why it weakened.

### Were separate weighting dimensions genuinely useful?

Yes. In this test, separate dimensions made it possible to say:

- the organiser personally prefers pizza, but current practical support for pizza is not fully established;
- the cooking volunteer personally prefers homemade food, but contextual support is weak;
- pre-ordered food may have stronger contextual support after the oven update without anyone personally preferring it most.

That separation would have been obscured by a single confidence score.

### Was recursive decomposition useful?

Yes. Decomposing “varied prepared order” into single-supplier, multiple-order, and pizza-plus-separate-dietary-items sub-branches materially improved the decision. It showed that “prepared food” is not one option and that dietary inclusion and coordination differ significantly between sub-branches.

### Did the method remain simple despite broader scope?

Mostly yes, if the user respects the optional nature of the table and stops at a bounded cycle. It becomes less simple if every branch must be fully weighted and documented. The broader scope is usable, but it needs discipline to avoid turning into an exhaustive menu-generation exercise.

### Was it usable without Blaineyan Reasoning?

Yes. The supplied v0.2 specification and test brief were sufficient to apply the method. No Blaineyan Reasoning, Concord architecture, or originating background note was needed.

### Was the stopping rule sufficient?

Mostly. It gives sensible conditions for stopping, but “sufficiently” and “material value” remain judgment calls. A more operational stopping checklist would help, e.g.:

- Are the immediate alternatives represented?
- Are key assumptions visible?
- Is the next information-gathering step identified?
- Are pruned/deferred branches recorded?
- Would further branching change the provisional choice?
