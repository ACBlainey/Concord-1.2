## A. Epistemic separation

### Supplied observations / facts

- A fictional volunteer-run local-history archive has accumulated approximately forty years of material.
- The collection includes paper records, photographs, cassette recordings, VHS tapes, optical discs, external hard drives and born-digital files in many software formats.
- The committee initially frames the problem as: “Which scanner should we buy so we can digitise the archive before it deteriorates?”
- The archive has limited money and volunteer time.
- Some physical materials are visibly deteriorating.
- The collection has never been comprehensively inventoried.
- Some digital storage devices are old and may no longer be reliable.
- Filenames and folder structures are inconsistent.
- Provenance and ownership information are incomplete for some items.
- Some material may contain personal or sensitive information.
- Some formats may require obsolete hardware or software.
- Volunteers have mixed technical skills.
- The archive does not know which material is unique, duplicated elsewhere or replaceable.
- Digitisation itself can create new preservation, metadata, storage and access obligations.
- No detailed collection measurements, legal determinations or conservation assessments are supplied.
- Professional archival, conservation, legal and technical expertise may be consulted later.
- The task is not to select a scanner or produce a final archival-preservation plan.
- The task is to determine what the archive should investigate and whether the original problem should be reformulated.

### Inferences

- The scanner question is a proposed solution embedded in the problem statement, not a neutral observation.
- A scanner purchase cannot be rationally specified from the supplied facts alone because media types, volumes, priorities, condition, uniqueness, rights, storage needs and volunteer capacity are unknown.
- Digitisation is one possible preservation/access action among several, not a complete preservation strategy.
- The archive’s immediate problem is at least partly an information-deficit problem: it does not know what it has, what is most at risk, what is unique, what may be legally restricted, or what technical capability is required.
- Some digital carriers may create urgent preservation risk independently of the physical scanner question.
- Rights, sensitivity and provenance constraints may affect what can be digitised, stored, accessed or shared.
- A phased investigation may be more appropriate than a single equipment purchase.

### Hypotheses

- H1: The greatest near-term risk may not be the absence of a scanner but the combination of uninventoried holdings, unreliable digital storage, obsolete formats and unknown uniqueness.
- H2: The scanner decision is downstream of a preservation-priority and requirements-assessment process.
- H3: A small number of bounded investigation tracks—inventory/risk triage, digital-carrier stabilisation, rights/sensitivity triage, and capability/workflow assessment—can materially improve the framing without producing a final preservation plan.
- H4: If the archive proceeds directly to scanner purchase, it may create new obligations without reducing the most significant loss risks.

### Speculation / research leads

These are exploratory possibilities, not claims:

- There may be unique or irreplaceable recordings or born-digital files on obsolete media.
- Some digital storage devices may already be close to failure, but this cannot be determined from the brief.
- Some materials may be duplicated in other institutions or collections, but this is unknown.
- Rights and privacy constraints may make some materials unsuitable for open digitisation or online access.
- Volunteer capacity may be the binding constraint rather than equipment cost.
- The archive may need different interventions for different media rather than one scanner-centred workflow.

### Reasoning assumptions

- The archive values preservation of material and/or informational content, not merely possession of equipment.
- The committee can pause or defer scanner purchase pending investigation.
- Some investigations can be started with volunteer effort before specialist consultation.
- Specialist expertise may be needed later for conservation, legal, archival and digital-preservation questions.
- The supplied brief is intentionally incomplete and does not authorise inventing measurements, legal statuses or conservation findings.
- The purpose of the current cycle is to improve the question and identify investigations, not to deliver a final plan.

## B. Compare

### What was compared

- The committee’s scanner-centred framing against a preservation-risk framing.
- Physical deterioration against digital obsolescence and storage unreliability.
- “Digitisation” as scanning against digitisation as one action within a wider lifecycle that creates metadata, storage, access and rights obligations.
- A known, measured collection against an un-inventoried, mixed-media, partially undocumented collection.
- Equipment acquisition as the critical path against inventory, prioritisation, rights triage and capability assessment as possible critical paths.
- Limited money and volunteer time against multiple competing preservation and access needs.

### What Compare contributes beyond simple observation

Simple observation records the constraints: limited money, deteriorating physical items, old digital storage, inconsistent filenames, unknown uniqueness, mixed skills, possible sensitive material, obsolete formats, and no comprehensive inventory. Compare exposes that these are not merely background facts around a scanner purchase. They compete with and partially determine whether a scanner is the right first intervention. Comparison reveals the hidden assumption that the scanner is the bottleneck. It also shows that “digitise the archive” is not a single function: it may mean preservation surrogate creation, access copy creation, metadata capture, rights management, format migration, storage migration or some combination. Without comparison, the original framing could remain unchallenged.

Compare therefore contributes a recognisable operation: it contrasts alternative framings and exposes that the surface question may be a solution proposal rather than the underlying problem.

## C. Problem reformulation and abstraction

### Testing the scanner framing

The original framing is:

> “Which scanner should we buy so we can digitise the archive before it deteriorates?”

This framing assumes:

- the scanner is the primary missing capability;
- digitisation is the primary preservation action;
- physical deterioration is the main or only urgent risk;
- the collection is sufficiently known to choose equipment;
- digitisation will not create unmanageable new obligations;
- rights, sensitivity and provenance issues can be handled after digitisation;
- volunteer capacity and workflow are adequate or secondary;
- a single acquisition decision is the appropriate response.

From the supplied facts, these assumptions are not established. Some are directly challenged by the brief: no comprehensive inventory, old digital storage, inconsistent filenames, incomplete provenance, possible sensitive information, obsolete formats, mixed technical skills, unknown uniqueness, and new obligations created by digitisation.

The scanner framing is therefore not rejected as irrelevant, but it is not sufficient as the root problem.

### Functional reformulation

A more useful functional question is:

> **What bounded investigation and risk-triage process should the archive undertake to determine its preservation priorities, identify the most significant loss risks across heterogeneous and partially unknown holdings, and establish requirements for any preservation action—including whether, when and what kind of digitisation capability is needed?**

A shorter version:

> **How should the archive reduce avoidable loss and manage new obligations across an uninventoried, mixed-media collection under limited resources, before committing to digitisation equipment?**

### Abstraction level

The problem has been moved from product acquisition to risk management and information governance under uncertainty. The system boundary expands from “scanner purchase” to “collection, carriers, rights, people, workflows and downstream obligations.” The scanner becomes a contingent downstream choice rather than the defining question.

## E. Branch control

The active branch set was bounded to B1–B5. B1 was subordinated from root framing to contingent candidate. B2–B5 were retained because each addresses a materially distinct uncertainty that could change the reformulated problem or later preservation action.

Branch expansion was controlled using the version 0.2 proportional approach:

1. Only materially distinct branches were generated.
2. The active set was kept small enough to examine within the test.
3. Sub-branches were not opened unless they could materially change the explanation, design, decision or next test.
4. Branches requiring missing evidence or specialist authority were deferred rather than pursued speculatively.
5. Expansion stopped when new branches became variations of existing ones, such as individual scanner models, individual format migrations or item-level legal questions.

No universal numerical branch budget was used. The bound was proportional to the task: a blind usability test with limited supplied evidence and no specialist validation authority.

Further branches were deferred because they are downstream of B2–B5, require evidence not supplied, or require specialist methods that exploratory reasoning should not substitute for.

## F. Examination, failure modes and disposition

### B1. Scanner-as-primary digitisation

- **Examination:** Directly reflects the committee’s initial frame. It would be useful only if the archive already knew what to digitise, in what order, for what purpose, under what rights, with what storage and metadata capacity, and with what volunteer workflow.
- **Failure modes:** Buying the wrong scanner; buying a scanner before identifying more urgent digital risks; creating unmanaged digital objects; breaching privacy or rights; failing to preserve provenance; consuming budget and volunteer time.
- **Disposition:** Pruned as root. Retained as a downstream candidate because a scanner may eventually be part of the solution.

### B2. Inventory, condition and uniqueness/duplication triage

- **Examination:** Addresses the information deficit that blocks rational prioritisation. It can begin with sampling and phased records rather than exhaustive cataloguing.
- **Failure modes:** Trying to inventory everything at once; misdiagnosing condition; mistaking absence of duplicate record for uniqueness; delaying urgent digital stabilisation.
- **Disposition:** Retain active. This is a first-priority investigation because many other decisions depend on it.

### B3. At-risk digital carriers and storage stabilisation

- **Examination:** The brief states that some digital storage devices are old and may no longer be reliable. This creates a preservation risk that a scanner does not address.
- **Failure modes:** Losing data during migration; copying without verification; failing to document formats; exposing sensitive files; creating new unmanaged storage.
- **Disposition:** Retain active. This should proceed in parallel with inventory because delay may be costly.

### B4. Rights, sensitivity, provenance and access triage

- **Examination:** The brief states that provenance and ownership are incomplete for some items and that some material may be personal or sensitive. This affects what can be digitised, stored, shared or published.
- **Failure modes:** Privacy breach; unlawful access; over-restriction based on speculation; under-restriction due to missing provenance; legal decisions made without legal expertise.
- **Disposition:** Retain active. It should constrain digitisation and access planning, while final legal determinations are deferred to specialists.

### B5. Capability, workflow and format-access requirements

- **Examination:** The brief states that volunteers have mixed technical skills and that some formats may require obsolete hardware or software. This affects feasibility, training, workflow and equipment requirements.
- **Failure modes:** Assuming volunteers can operate complex workflows; depending on unavailable obsolete equipment; creating a workflow that cannot be sustained; key-person dependency.
- **Disposition:** Retain active. It informs what interventions are realistic and what scanner requirements, if any, are appropriate.

### Deferred branches

Deferred branches are not rejected as worthless. They are parked because they cannot be usefully examined without evidence or specialist authority. Their reopening conditions are stated in the branch table.

## G. Reconsider → Re-ground → Refine

### G1. Reconsider

**What changed in framing, assumptions or branch structure?**

- The original framing was product-centred: which scanner to buy.
- Reconsideration changes the root frame to risk management and information governance under uncertainty.
- The system boundary expands from equipment acquisition to collection, carriers, rights, people, workflows and downstream obligations.
- The abstraction level moves from “scanner choice” to “what investigation and prioritisation process is required before any preservation action.”
- The branch structure changes: scanner purchase becomes a downstream contingent branch, while inventory/risk triage, digital-carrier stabilisation, rights/sensitivity triage and capability/workflow assessment become active first-order branches.
- The assumption that digitisation is the primary preservation action is no longer treated as given.

### G2. Re-ground

**How does the reconsidered frame reconnect to evidence, constraints and deficits?**

The reconsidered frame must be tested against the supplied constraints:

- limited money and volunteer time;
- no comprehensive inventory;
- some physical deterioration;
- old and possibly unreliable digital storage;
- inconsistent filenames and folder structures;
- incomplete provenance and ownership;
- possible sensitive information;
- obsolete hardware/software risks;
- mixed volunteer technical skills;
- unknown uniqueness/duplication/replaceability;
- digitisation creates new obligations;
- no detailed measurements, legal determinations or conservation assessments are supplied;
- specialist expertise may be consulted later.

From these, the grounded finding is limited but important: the archive cannot justify a specific scanner purchase from the supplied facts alone. It first needs a bounded investigation to classify holdings by risk and to produce requirements.

**Evidence deficits include:**

- what items and media exist, in what approximate quantities;
- condition of physical materials;
- health and contents of digital storage devices;
- file formats and obsolete-software dependencies;
- uniqueness, duplication and replaceability;
- provenance, ownership, consent and sensitivity status;
- volunteer skills, availability and training needs;
- cost and effort estimates for possible interventions;
- legal and conservation determinations requiring specialists.

**Specialist re-grounding targets:**

- archival assessment and collection prioritisation;
- digital preservation and storage stabilisation;
- legal/privacy advice;
- conservation assessment for deteriorating physical items;
- workflow and volunteer-capacity planning.

### G3. Refine

**Improved question / model / test:**

> What bounded investigation and risk-triage process should the archive undertake to determine preservation priorities, identify the most significant loss risks across heterogeneous and partially unknown holdings, and establish requirements for any preservation action—including whether, when and what kind of digitisation capability is needed?

**Model:**

A phased process:

1. bounded inventory and risk triage;
2. parallel digital-carrier stabilisation;
3. rights/sensitivity/provenance triage;
4. capability/workflow/format-access assessment;
5. requirements statement;
6. only then consider scanner or other digitisation options.

**Test or next bounded cycle:**

- Can the archive produce a sampled inventory and risk register?
- Can it identify urgent digital carriers and stabilise them?
- Can it flag rights/sensitivity issues without making legal determinations?
- Can it state realistic volunteer workflow constraints?
- Does this produce a requirements statement that makes any scanner decision better grounded?

**Is another cycle needed?**

Yes, conditionally. After the initial triage produces evidence, a second bounded cycle can evaluate intervention options, including whether a scanner is needed, what kind, and for what subset of materials. The current cycle should stop before selecting equipment or producing a final preservation plan.

## I. Method critique

### Sufficiency without source material

Version 0.2 was sufficient to structure this response without the source corpus, Reality Trees or Concord-specific dependencies. The generic branch table was usable. The handoff packet fields were coherent. The distinction between exploratory reasoning and specialist validation was preservable.

### Remaining ambiguities and defects

- **Compare without external research:** Compare is usable by contrasting supplied elements and competing framings, but the specification does not explicitly say how to use Compare when outside analogues are forbidden or unavailable. Here it worked as internal contrast, but the instruction could be clearer.
- **Branch prioritisation under severe uncertainty:** The proportional branch control worked in principle, but the specification does not give guidance on how to rank materially distinct branches when all are plausible and resources are low. I used dependency and urgency, but that was an inference.
- **Reconsider / Re-ground / Refine distinction:** The three operations are distinguishable in version 0.2 and were auditable here. However, they remain close to ordinary problem-reframing language. More examples or boundary tests would help.
- **Output-label overlap:** HYPOTHESIS, SPECULATIVE BRANCH and RESEARCH LEAD can overlap. The specification acknowledges this, but in practice a branch may need multiple labels. That is manageable but adds interpretive load.
- **Minimal procedure overlap:** The minimal portable procedure includes both “Reframe” and later “Reconsider,” and both “Branch” and “Stress.” This may be useful reinforcement, but it can look redundant to a first-time user.
- **Recursion risk:** The archive problem naturally invites item-level, format-level and legal sub-branches. Version 0.2 warns about this, but the domain-specific temptation is strong. A stronger operational heuristic for “stop at triage, not item-level detail” would help.
- **Handoff packet:** The eight fields form a coherent specialist transition. No blocking omission was found. One possible addition would be a field for “immediate next action,” but the current fields imply it.
- **Portability:** The method transferred to this fresh domain without Reality Trees or source-corpus knowledge. No blocking portability defect was found in this execution.

### Overall usability note

Version 0.2 was sufficient for an independent blind application to the community-archive problem. The main practical difficulty was not missing source material but deciding how much branching is enough under incomplete evidence. The proportional branch-control guidance prevented exhaustive enumeration, but it still required judgement. No BR2-T result class is assigned here, per instruction.
