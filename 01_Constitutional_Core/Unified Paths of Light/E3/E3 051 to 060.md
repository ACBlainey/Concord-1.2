# E3 Unit 051 — Qur'an 4:32
## Do Not Covet Another's Advantages

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:32 warns against coveting advantages given to others and connects persons with what they themselves earn.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not allow comparison with others to become corrosive envy.**
- **Recognise another person's legitimate advantages without treating them as a personal injury.**
- **Direct effort toward one's own responsible action rather than resentful comparison.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Exodus 20:17 within biblical tradition; Galatians 5:26 | Covetousness and envy are condemned. | A | Do not resentfully desire another's possessions or standing. |
| Judaism — Exodus 20:17 | Coveting another's household and possessions is explicitly prohibited. | A | Restrain covetous desire. |
| Buddhism — non-covetousness / mudita | Envy is countered by appreciative joy and non-attachment. | A/B | Do not make another's good fortune a source of hostility. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 12:13; non-attachment | Freedom from envy and possessiveness is praised. | A/B | Restrain envy and attachment. |
| Sikhism — rejection of lobh and haumai | Greed and ego-driven comparison are vices. | B | Do not let greed or ego dominate conduct. |
| Confucianism — cultivation over competitive resentment | The exemplary person attends to righteousness and self-cultivation. | B | Improve oneself rather than resent another's advantage. |
| African Traditional | Envy is frequently treated in proverbial traditions as socially destructive. | B/C | Do not let jealousy corrode communal relations. |

### Conclusion
The source contributes a widely convergent anti-envy principle while directing attention back toward responsible personal action.

# E3 Unit 052 — Qur'an 4:34–35
## Responsibility, Serious Marital Conflict, and Arbitration

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:34–35 presents a historically gendered marital framework, addresses serious marital discord, and then requires arbitration through representatives from both families when rupture is feared.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Household authority carries material responsibility.**
- **Serious relational conflict should move toward structured resolution rather than indefinite escalation.**
- **Use third-party arbitration where direct resolution fails.**
- **Include representation from both sides in reconciliation efforts.**

### Comparative Analysis — Mediation of Serious Relationship Conflict

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 18:15–16; pastoral mediation | Escalating disputes can involve additional trusted persons. | B | Bring third parties into unresolved conflict where useful. |
| Judaism — rabbinic courts and family mediation | Structured adjudication and mediation address serious disputes. | A/B | Conflict can require recognised third-party resolution. |
| Buddhism — sangha dispute procedures | Formal procedures exist for resolving community disputes. | B | Persistent conflict should enter a fair resolution process. |
| Hinduism — family/community adjudication traditions | Household disputes historically involve family and legal mechanisms. | B/C | Serious conflict may require mediation. |
| Sikhism — community deliberative traditions | Community institutions can assist resolution. | B | Use collective processes rather than unchecked unilateral power. |
| Confucianism — family mediation | Family and respected intermediaries traditionally play major roles in dispute resolution. | A/B | Restore relationships through mediated settlement where possible. |
| African Traditional | Elders and kin councils commonly mediate family disputes in many societies. | A/B | Give both sides a path into communal mediation. |

### Discussion
The gendered legal and disciplinary provisions of 4:34 are not treated as universal equivalents. The clear cross-comparative institutional principle in 4:35 is bilateral arbitration. E3 preserves the difference rather than collapsing the entire passage into the modern concept of equality.

### Conclusion
Third-party mediation is broadly convergent; the specific marital authority structure remains historically and tradition-specifically contested and non-universal.

# E3 Unit 053 — Qur'an 4:36
## Wide-Radius Social Responsibility

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:36 commands good treatment of parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, near and distant neighbours, companions, travellers, and persons under one's authority, while condemning arrogance.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Care obligations extend beyond the immediate household.**
- **Treat neighbours well whether socially close or distant.**
- **Assist travellers and people without ordinary support.**
- **Power over dependants does not remove duties of humane treatment.**
- **Do not turn social advantage into arrogance.**

### Comparative Analysis — Duties Beyond the Immediate In-Group

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 10:25–37; Matthew 25:35 | Neighbour-love and care for strangers are explicit. | A | Extend practical care beyond close associates. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:18, 33–34 | Love of neighbour and fair treatment of the stranger are explicit. | A | Treat neighbour and stranger with moral concern. |
| Buddhism — metta | Loving-kindness extends beyond narrow attachment. | A/B | Expand concern beyond one's immediate circle. |
| Hinduism — hospitality and dana traditions | Guests, travellers, and people in need receive ethical consideration. | B | Extend generosity beyond family. |
| Sikhism — langar, seva, equality | Service and shared food cross social boundaries. | A/B | Serve people without narrow status exclusion. |
| Confucianism — ren | Humane concern radiates outward from close relationships. | B | Moral concern expands through social relations. |
| African Traditional — hospitality and communal reciprocity | Hospitality and extended social obligation are strong in many traditions. | B | Community responsibility extends to guests and neighbours. |

### Conclusion
This source creates a broad map of relational obligation and strongly converges with traditions that extend care beyond kin.

# E3 Unit 054 — Qur'an 4:37–39
## Miserliness, Concealment of Resources, and Performative Giving

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:37–39 criticises miserliness, encouraging others to be miserly, concealing resources, and spending merely to be seen by people.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not hoard while promoting hoarding as a social norm.**
- **Do not conceal resources in order to evade legitimate responsibility.**
- **Do not make generosity a performance designed primarily for reputation.**
- **Ethical influence includes what conduct one encourages in others.**

### Comparative Analysis — Reject Performative Generosity

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 6:1–4 | Publicly staged charity for praise is explicitly criticised. | A | Do not turn charity into reputation management. |
| Judaism — tzedakah traditions | Anonymous or dignity-preserving charity is highly valued. | A/B | Give without humiliating recipients or glorifying oneself. |
| Buddhism — dana and non-attachment | Giving should not be driven by egoic reward. | B | Give without clinging to status. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 17:20–22 | Giving is distinguished according to motive and manner. | A | Ethical value depends partly on how and why one gives. |
| Sikhism — seva | Service is ideally selfless. | B | Do not make service an instrument of ego. |
| Confucianism — sincerity | Virtue should be genuine rather than merely displayed. | B | Moral appearance should correspond to actual motive. |
| African Traditional | Public generosity can have communal functions, so publicity itself is not inherently corrupt; exploitative status-seeking varies by context. | C | Social giving should genuinely serve relationship, not merely vanity. |

### Conclusion
The unit reinforces the emerging distinction between material transfer and the ethical quality of giving.

# E3 Unit 055 — Qur'an 4:58
## Return Trusts and Judge Fairly

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:58 commands returning trusts to those entitled to them and judging between people with justice.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Return entrusted property, authority, or responsibility to its rightful holder.**
- **Do not convert temporary custody into permanent possession.**
- **Adjudicate disputes impartially.**
- **Justice applies between people rather than only toward favoured groups.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|

| Judaism — Deuteronomy 16:18–20 | Judges are commanded to pursue justice without distortion. | A | Judge fairly. |
| Buddhism — honest conduct and impartiality | Ethical discipline rejects appropriation and biased harmful conduct. | B | Handle others' interests honestly and impartially. |
| Hinduism — dharma of judges/rulers | Adjudication is subject to dharma rather than private advantage. | A/B | Public judgement should follow justice. |
| Sikhism — truthful living and justice | Authority is morally accountable and honest dealing required. | B | Use entrusted authority justly. |
| Confucianism — righteous government | Office is a responsibility ordered toward just governance. | B | Public authority is a trust, not private property. |
| African Traditional | Chiefs, elders, and councils commonly carry fiduciary/adjudicative duties. | B | Entrusted authority should serve communal justice. |

### Conclusion
This compact verse joins fiduciary integrity and impartial adjudication, two principles with strong cross-traditional support.

# E3 Unit 056 — Qur'an 4:59
## Authority, Rule-Governed Disagreement, and Appeal

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:59 instructs obedience to legitimate authority within its religious framework and directs disputed matters to a higher normative standard.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Stable communities require recognised authority.**
- **Authority is not the sole source of its own legitimacy; it remains answerable to higher norms.**
- **Disagreement should have an appeal mechanism rather than defaulting to force.**

### Comparative Analysis — Authority Subject to Higher Norms

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Acts 5:29; Romans 13 | Civil authority is recognised but not treated as morally ultimate. | A/B | Obedience to authority is bounded by higher moral obligation. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 17; prophetic correction of kings | Rulers and judges operate under Torah rather than above it. | A | Authority is subordinate to law. |
| Buddhism — dhamma and righteous kingship | Legitimate rule is morally assessed through dhamma. | B | Political power remains subject to ethical standards. |
| Hinduism — rajadharma | Rulers are constrained by dharma. | A/B | Authority is legitimate only within moral duty. |
| Sikhism — temporal authority under moral/divine justice | Political power is not morally self-justifying. | B | Power remains answerable to justice. |
| Confucianism — Mandate of Heaven / remonstrance | Rulers are judged by moral performance and may lose legitimacy. | A/B | Authority is conditional upon ethical governance. |
| African Traditional | Sacred/customary law and councils often constrain chiefs, though structures vary. | B/C | Leaders may be bounded by norms beyond personal will. |

### Conclusion
The specific theological appeal mechanism is Islamic. The wider institutional principle—that authority itself is norm-constrained and disputes require recognised resolution—is broadly represented.

# E3 Unit 057 — Qur'an 4:83
## Verify Sensitive Information Before Amplifying It

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:83 criticises immediately broadcasting reports concerning security or danger and instead directs such information toward people capable of properly assessing it.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not indiscriminately broadcast unverified sensitive information.**
- **Route high-stakes information toward competent assessment.**
- **Information handling should account for downstream social harm.**
- **Expertise can matter when interpreting complex or security-relevant reports.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 1:19; Proverbs tradition within biblical corpus | Be slow to speak and avoid reckless communication. | B | Do not amplify information impulsively. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:16; lashon hara traditions | Harmful tale-bearing is prohibited and speech is tightly disciplined. | A/B | Do not circulate damaging information irresponsibly. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Speech should be truthful, timely, and non-harmful. | A/B | Communicate responsibly and with regard to consequences. |
| Hinduism — satya and disciplined speech | Truthful speech is balanced with non-harm. | B | Speech should be both true and responsibly delivered. |
| Sikhism — truthful speech and restraint from slander | Communication should not be false or malicious. | B | Do not spread harmful falsehood or gossip. |
| Confucianism — careful speech and role competence | The exemplary person is cautious in speech; roles require competence. | B | High-stakes communication requires restraint and judgement. |
| African Traditional | Councils/elders can serve as filters for sensitive communal information, but practices vary. | C | Do not let rumours destabilise the community without responsible assessment. |

### Conclusion
This source generates an information-governance ethic: truthfulness alone is insufficient; timing, verification, competence, and consequences also matter.

# E3 Unit 058 — Qur'an 4:85
## Responsibility for the Effects of One's Advocacy

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:85 states that whoever intercedes or advocates for a good cause shares in its benefit, while one who intercedes for an evil cause shares in its burden.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Advocacy carries moral responsibility.**
- **Helping another person perform good can share in the good produced.**
- **Facilitating wrongdoing can make the facilitator partly responsible for its consequences.**
- **Indirect participation does not automatically erase accountability.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 1:32; Matthew 5:19 | Approval or encouragement of wrongdoing can itself be blameworthy. | A/B | Facilitating wrong carries responsibility. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:14 and rabbinic principle against placing a stumbling block | Do not enable another person's wrongdoing. | A | Facilitation can create moral responsibility. |
| Buddhism — karma and encouragement of wholesome/unwholesome action | Intention and participation in others' conduct have moral consequences. | B | Support wholesome action, not harmful action. |
| Hinduism — karma and participation in action | Assistance and intention can connect a person to consequences. | B | Indirect contribution can carry moral weight. |
| Sikhism — company and participation in righteous conduct | Association and action influence moral development. | B | Support conduct that serves truth and justice. |
| Confucianism — moral influence and recommendation of persons | Those who recommend or guide others bear responsibility for judgement. | B | Influence should be exercised responsibly. |
| African Traditional | Collective responsibility and complicity are recognised in many communal systems. | B/C | Helping produce an outcome can create shared responsibility. |

### Conclusion
The source contributes a clear complicity principle: moral responsibility can attach to facilitation, not merely direct execution.

# E3 Unit 060 — Qur'an 4:92–93
## Protection of Life, Responsibility for Accidental Harm, and Repair

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:92–93 sharply distinguishes intentional killing from accidental killing. Accidental killing triggers duties of repair and compensation, while deliberate killing receives severe condemnation.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not intentionally kill without right.**
- **Distinguish deliberate harm from accidental harm.**
- **Accidental harm can still generate duties of repair.**
- **Intent affects culpability without necessarily eliminating responsibility for consequences.**
- **Compensation can form part of restoration after unintended injury.**

### Comparative Analysis — Intention and Repair

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Exodus 21:12–14 | Biblical law distinguishes intentional killing from killing without premeditation. | A | Culpability depends partly on intention. |
| Judaism — Numbers 35:9–34 | Cities of refuge distinguish accidental homicide from murder. | A | Accidental and intentional killing require different treatment. |
| Buddhism — karma places major weight on intention | Intention is central to moral evaluation. | A/B | Mental intention changes moral culpability. |
| Hinduism — karma/dharma distinctions | Intention and circumstance affect moral assessment and expiation. | B | Harm should be evaluated with regard to intention and consequence. |
| Sikhism — justice and moral intention | Deliberate wrongdoing and unintended consequence are not morally identical. | B/C | Responsibility should consider intention. |
| Confucianism — graded responsibility and rectification | Circumstance and intention matter to judgement, alongside restoration of order. | B | Fair judgement distinguishes kinds of fault. |
| African Traditional | Compensation/reconciliation after accidental death occurs in numerous customary systems. | A/B | Unintended harm can still require restoration without being treated as murder. |

### Discussion
This unit provides a sophisticated responsibility structure. It rejects two extremes: treating accident as identical to intentional murder, and treating lack of intention as eliminating all duties toward those harmed.

### Conclusion
The distinction between intention, culpability, consequence, and repair has strong cross-traditional parallels and is an important addition to the emerging ethical catalogue.
