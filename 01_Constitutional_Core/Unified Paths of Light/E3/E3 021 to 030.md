## Introduction to Units 021–030

This file continues the Qur'an-first extraction in canonical order. Inclusion is determined by ethical content in the anchor text. No principle is included, excluded, strengthened, or weakened because of its relationship to the earlier Psalms-derived catalogue.

Evidence grades remain:

- **A — Direct textual equivalent**
- **B — Strong functional equivalent**
- **C — Inferred / corollary equivalent**
- **D — No clear equivalent identified**

Where the Qur'anic source contains a tradition-specific legal mechanism, that mechanism is preserved rather than converted into a falsely universal rule.

# E3 Unit 021 — Qur'an 2:233

## Ethics of Child Welfare, Parental Responsibility, and Mutual Consultation

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:233 regulates infant nursing, parental maintenance, non-harm between parents through the child, and decisions about weaning through mutual consent and consultation.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Protect the child's welfare** | Decisions following relationship change should continue to provide for the dependent child. |
| **Parental responsibility survives conflict** | Separation between adults does not erase responsibilities toward their child. |
| **Do not use a child to harm the other parent** | Dependants should not become instruments of retaliation in adult disputes. |
| **Use mutual consultation in shared decisions** | Important decisions affecting a dependent should be made through genuine consultation where possible. |

### Comparative Analysis — Protect Dependants During Parental Conflict

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — 1 Timothy 5:8; Ephesians 6:4 | Families bear duties of provision and parents must not misuse authority toward children. | B | Continue to provide responsibly for dependants. |
| **Judaism** — parental maintenance and family-law traditions | Parents carry continuing duties toward children. | B | Adult conflict does not cancel parental obligation. |
| **Buddhism** — Sigalovada Sutta | Parents have explicit duties to restrain children from wrong, encourage good, educate, and establish them securely. | A/B | Parenthood entails continuing positive responsibilities. |
| **Hinduism** — household dharma | Parents bear duties of care, protection, and formation toward children. | B | Dependants must be cared for responsibly. |
| **Sikhism** — family responsibility and seva | Family life is embedded in service, honest provision, and responsibility. | B | Care for dependants is part of ethical household life. |
| **Confucianism** — reciprocal family ethics | Parent-child relationships carry strong obligations of care and formation. | B | Family authority is paired with responsibility. |
| **African Traditional** — extended-family child-rearing | Child welfare often engages obligations extending beyond the biological parents to wider kin. | B | Children remain entitled to communal and familial care. |

### Discussion

The verse is unusually procedural. It does not merely command care; it addresses maintenance, nursing, consultation, and the possibility of parental disagreement. Particularly significant is the prohibition on harming one parent through the child.

### Conclusion

Continuing parental responsibility and protection of dependent children are broadly convergent. The Qur'anic passage adds an explicit consultation and anti-retaliation framework.

# E3 Unit 022 — Qur'an 2:234–235

## Ethics of Bereavement, Waiting Periods, and Restraint in Courtship

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:234–235 regulates a widow's waiting period and permits indirect indication of future marriage interest while prohibiting secret commitments before the prescribed period is complete.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Respect periods of bereavement and transition** | Major relational transitions should not be exploited through immediate pressure. |
| **Exercise restraint in courtship** | Desire for a future relationship does not justify bypassing boundaries governing the present situation. |
| **Avoid secret arrangements that undermine legitimate process** | Private commitments should not be used to evade protective social or legal boundaries. |

### Comparative Analysis — Respect Vulnerability During Bereavement and Transition

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — pastoral and biblical concern for widows | Widows receive particular protection and care, though no directly equivalent waiting rule is universal. | B | Do not exploit people made vulnerable through bereavement. |
| **Judaism** — mourning and remarriage traditions | Bereavement is governed by recognised periods and social obligations. | B | Allow structured time for mourning and transition. |
| **Buddhism** — restraint and compassionate conduct | Ethical conduct discourages exploiting vulnerability and uncontrolled desire. | C | Desire should be governed by compassion and restraint. |
| **Hinduism** — mourning and household traditions | Bereavement is socially and ritually structured, though remarriage rules vary historically. | C | Major loss carries recognised transitional obligations. |
| **Sikhism** — dignity, restraint, and family responsibility | Ethical treatment of bereaved persons follows broader duties of compassion and respect. | C | Do not exploit vulnerability following loss. |
| **Confucianism** — formal mourning traditions | Mourning is treated as a serious relational and social period. | B | Bereavement warrants time and respect. |
| **African Traditional** — diverse mourning and widowhood customs | Many societies prescribe mourning periods, though their content and effects vary greatly. | B/C | Bereavement is a protected social transition, but specific customs require local evaluation. |

### Discussion

The specific waiting period is Islamic law and should not be universalised. The cross-traditional ethical extraction is narrower: bereavement creates a period in which restraint, respect, and protection from opportunistic pressure are ethically relevant.

### Conclusion

Respect for bereavement has broad parallels, while the detailed rules of remarriage remain tradition-specific.

# E3 Unit 023 — Qur'an 2:236–237

## Ethics of Fairness and Generosity When Commitments End

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:236–237 addresses divorce before consummation, including fair provision and the division or remission of an agreed marriage gift.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **End failed commitments fairly** | The collapse of an intended relationship does not remove duties of fair dealing. |
| **Recognise unequal economic effects** | Separation can impose material consequences that should be addressed rather than ignored. |
| **Generosity may exceed strict entitlement** | Parties may voluntarily waive claims or act more generously than the minimum legal requirement. |
| **Do not forget graciousness between people** | Legal entitlement need not exhaust ethical responsibility. |

### Comparative Analysis — Fairness Beyond Minimum Entitlement

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 5:40–42 | Generosity may exceed what strict legal demand requires. | B | Ethical conduct can go beyond minimum entitlement. |
| **Judaism** — Deuteronomy 15:12–15 | A departing dependent is not simply released empty-handed but should receive provision. | B | Separation from a relationship of dependence can carry duties of material fairness. |
| **Buddhism** — dana and non-attachment | Generosity and relinquishment of possessive claims are virtues. | B | Do not allow attachment to entitlement to extinguish generosity. |
| **Hinduism** — dana and dharmic fairness | Generosity and appropriate material duty exceed purely self-interested calculation. | B | Use resources with fairness and generosity. |
| **Sikhism** — vand chhakna | Sharing and generosity are positive obligations. | B | Do not reduce moral conduct to keeping everything one can claim. |
| **Confucianism** — righteousness over profit | The morally cultivated person does not make advantage the sole criterion of conduct. | B | Fairness can require foregoing advantage. |
| **African Traditional** — reciprocal settlement practices | Relationship dissolution is often embedded in reciprocal familial and material obligations. | C | Ending a relationship may require restoration or fair settlement. |

### Discussion

The Qur'anic rule is legally specific, but its concluding emphasis on graciousness produces a wider principle: justice sets boundaries, while generosity may appropriately exceed the enforceable minimum.

### Conclusion

The distinction between strict entitlement and gracious conduct has broad functional equivalents across the traditions.

# E3 Unit 024 — Qur'an 2:256

## Ethics of Non-Compulsion in Religion

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:256 states that there is no compulsion in religion, grounding the statement in a distinction between right guidance and error.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Do not compel religious belief** | Religious adherence should not be produced merely through coercion. |
| **Conviction and force are different categories** | A belief cannot acquire genuine moral or spiritual validity simply because compliance is compelled. |

### Comparative Analysis — Religious Non-Compulsion

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — New Testament invitation/mission traditions | Faith is repeatedly invited and preached, but the New Testament does not provide an equally concise universal non-compulsion formula; later Christian history is mixed. | B/C | Genuine faith is ordinarily framed as response rather than mere forced assent. |
| **Judaism** — covenantal identity and rabbinic traditions | Judaism generally does not impose a universal mission to convert humanity, though historical legal contexts differ. | C | Religious identity need not require coercive universal conversion. |
| **Buddhism** — teaching and voluntary refuge | Entry into Buddhist practice is ordinarily framed through hearing, examination, and voluntary commitment. | B | Spiritual adherence should arise through acceptance rather than force. |
| **Hinduism** — plural paths and diverse schools | Many Hindu traditions accommodate multiple paths, though there is no single canonical equivalent rule governing coercion. | C | Religious diversity can coexist without forced uniformity. |
| **Sikhism** — defence of freedom of conscience in Sikh historical tradition | Sikh tradition strongly remembers defence of another community's right to practise its religion. | A/B | Protect the freedom of others to maintain their faith. |
| **Confucianism** — moral cultivation through education and example | Ethical formation is ideally cultivated rather than reducible to coercive punishment. | C | Durable moral commitment cannot be produced by force alone. |
| **African Traditional** — highly diverse traditions | No single pan-African doctrine of religious non-compulsion can responsibly be asserted. | D | No clear universal equivalent identified at this level. |

### Discussion

This unit is important precisely because the comparison is uneven. The Qur'anic formulation is explicit. Some traditions provide strong functional or historical equivalents; others do not supply a sufficiently direct canonical proposition to justify an A-grade comparison.

This is the kind of non-uniformity E3 is designed to preserve.

### Conclusion

Religious non-compulsion is explicit in the Qur'anic source but does **not** produce an artificial eight-tradition universal in this comparison.

# E3 Unit 025 — Qur'an 2:261–264

## Ethics of Generosity Without Humiliation or Manipulation

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:261–264 praises charitable spending, then prohibits following gifts with reminders of generosity or injury. It states that kind speech and forgiveness can be better than charity accompanied by harm and criticises giving for public display.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Give generously** | Resources should be used to benefit others. |
| **Do not humiliate recipients** | A gift does not entitle the giver to demean, injure, or control the recipient. |
| **Do not weaponise past generosity** | Assistance should not create an indefinite moral debt used for domination. |
| **Motive matters** | Public reputation should not be the sole or primary purpose of apparent generosity. |
| **Kindness can matter more than harmful giving** | Material assistance delivered abusively can undermine its ethical value. |

### Comparative Analysis — Give Without Humiliating the Recipient

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 6:1–4 | Charity should not be performed for public praise. | A | Give without turning generosity into self-promotion. |
| **Judaism** — Maimonides' hierarchy of charity | Higher forms of giving preserve recipient dignity and reduce humiliation. | A/B | Protect the dignity and independence of the person receiving help. |
| **Buddhism** — dana | Giving is cultivated with non-attachment rather than egoistic return. | B | Give without possessiveness or demand for status. |
| **Hinduism** — Bhagavad Gita 17:20–22 | Giving is ethically differentiated by motive, appropriateness, and respect. | A | Give appropriately and without contempt or selfish return. |
| **Sikhism** — seva and vand chhakna | Service and sharing should be selfless rather than status-seeking. | B | Help others without converting service into superiority. |
| **Confucianism** — benevolence and ritual propriety | Assistance should preserve humane and respectful relations. | B | Beneficence should respect the recipient's dignity. |
| **African Traditional** — reciprocity and communal sharing | Giving often reinforces relationship and mutual obligation, but humiliating domination conflicts with communal solidarity. | B/C | Assistance should strengthen rather than degrade social relationship. |

### Discussion

This passage significantly refines the generic principle of generosity. The ethical act is evaluated not only by transfer of resources but by **manner, motive, and effect on the recipient's dignity**.

### Conclusion

Generosity without humiliation or self-display has strong cross-traditional support and represents a more sophisticated ethic than simple resource transfer.

# E3 Unit 026 — Qur'an 2:267

## Ethics of Giving What Is Worth Giving

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:267 instructs believers to give from good things they have acquired and not deliberately select inferior things for others that they themselves would be reluctant to accept.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Do not offload worthless goods as charity** | Giving should not become a method for disposing of what the giver considers unacceptable. |
| **Apply reciprocal standards** | Consider whether you would regard the offered treatment or goods as acceptable if positions were reversed. |
| **Quality matters in generosity** | Ethical giving concerns the value of what is transferred, not merely the fact that something was given. |

### Comparative Analysis — Apply to Others a Standard You Would Accept Yourself

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 7:12 | Treat others as you would want them to treat you. | A | Apply reciprocal standards of treatment. |
| **Judaism** — Shabbat 31a, Hillel | What is hateful to you, do not do to another. | A | Do not impose on others what you reject for yourself. |
| **Buddhism** — Udana-Varga / comparable reciprocity formulations | One should not hurt others in ways one would find hurtful oneself. | A/B | Use one's own vulnerability to understand obligations toward others. |
| **Hinduism** — Mahabharata, reciprocity formulations | One should not do to another what one would regard as harmful to oneself. | A/B | Ethical judgement should be reciprocal. |
| **Sikhism** — equality and shared human dignity | Others should not be treated as intrinsically beneath oneself. | B | Extend equal dignity in material dealings. |
| **Confucianism** — Analects 15.24 | Do not impose on others what you do not desire for yourself. | A | Use reciprocity as an ethical test. |
| **African Traditional** — Ubuntu and reciprocal personhood | One's wellbeing and dignity are understood relationally with those of others. | B | Treat others as persons whose dignity is continuous with one's own. |

### Discussion

The Qur'anic passage applies reciprocity to material giving in a concrete way: do not knowingly give another person something you yourself would accept only reluctantly. This provides a practical anti-double-standard heuristic.

### Conclusion

Reciprocity is among the strongest cross-traditional convergences encountered so far, and this verse applies it directly to the quality of charitable conduct.

# E3 Unit 027 — Qur'an 2:268–274

## Ethics of Discreet Giving and Aid Without Discrimination

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:268–274 praises wisdom and charitable spending, states that concealed charity to the poor can be especially good, recognises needy people who may not openly ask, and praises giving by night and day, secretly and openly.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Notice hidden need** | Do not assume that a person is secure merely because dignity prevents them from asking. |
| **Discreet aid can protect dignity** | Privacy may be ethically preferable when publicity would expose or embarrass a recipient. |
| **Give consistently** | Generosity should not depend solely on convenient occasions. |
| **Aid is not merely a reward for ideological agreement** | The immediate responsibility of giving is not reduced to controlling another person's beliefs. |

### Comparative Analysis — Recognise and Protect Hidden Need

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 6:3–4 | Giving in secret is explicitly praised. | A | Charity need not become public performance. |
| **Judaism** — Maimonides on anonymous charity | Anonymous giving protects dignity and reduces social embarrassment. | A | Give in ways that preserve recipient dignity. |
| **Buddhism** — dana | Giving is ideally detached from ego and expected reward. | B | Help without making recognition the purpose. |
| **Hinduism** — Bhagavad Gita 17:20 | Proper giving is made at the right place and time to a worthy recipient without expectation of return. | A/B | Give appropriately without demanding repayment or status. |
| **Sikhism** — seva | Selfless service is valued without dependence on personal recognition. | B | Service should not be motivated primarily by praise. |
| **Confucianism** — benevolence | Humane attention requires sensitivity to people's actual circumstances. | B | Responsible care notices need rather than waiting for spectacle. |
| **African Traditional** — communal mutual aid | Close communal networks can impose duties to recognise need even when it is not formally requested. | B/C | Community responsibility includes noticing vulnerable members. |

### Discussion

This source adds informational sensitivity to the ethics of charity. Need may be concealed by dignity, social appearance, or reluctance to ask. Ethical generosity therefore requires observation and judgement, not merely responding to explicit requests.

### Conclusion

Discreet, dignity-preserving generosity has strong parallels across several traditions, while recognition of hidden need expands the practical demands of beneficence.

# E3 Unit 028 — Qur'an 2:275–281

## Ethics of Exploitative Finance, Fair Exchange, and Debtor Relief

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:275–281 distinguishes trade from riba, condemns the latter, instructs abandonment of outstanding usurious gain, permits recovery of principal, prohibits wronging or being wronged, and commands additional time for a debtor in hardship while praising remission of the debt.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Do not exploit financial dependence** | Financial arrangements should not turn another person's vulnerability into unjust gain. |
| **Distinguish legitimate exchange from exploitative extraction** | Not every profitable transaction is ethically equivalent. |
| **Recover what is legitimately owed without excess** | A creditor's valid claim does not justify taking more through prohibited exploitation. |
| **Give distressed debtors time** | Inability to pay should trigger accommodation rather than automatic intensification of hardship. |
| **Debt forgiveness can exceed strict justice** | Voluntary remission can be ethically superior to insisting upon every enforceable claim. |

### Comparative Analysis — Relief for Debtors in Hardship

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 6:12; Luke 6:34–35; early Christian concern for debt and lending | Mercy in debt and non-exploitative lending are recurring themes. | B | Financial claims should be tempered by mercy and non-exploitation. |
| **Judaism** — Exodus 22:25; Deuteronomy 15:1–11 | Interest against the poor is restricted and periodic debt release is mandated. | A | Do not exploit poverty through debt; provide mechanisms of relief. |
| **Buddhism** — Right Livelihood and non-exploitation | Livelihood should avoid causing harm and exploitation. | B | Profit does not justify harmful economic conduct. |
| **Hinduism** — dharmic regulation of lending and interest | Classical traditions regulate lending and condemn abusive extraction, though rules vary. | B | Credit relations are subject to ethical limits. |
| **Sikhism** — honest earning and rejection of exploitation | Wealth should be acquired through honest work rather than exploitation. | B | Economic power should not be used to exploit vulnerability. |
| **Confucianism** — righteousness over profit | Profit pursued without righteousness is morally suspect. | B | Economic advantage is subordinate to justice. |
| **African Traditional** — reciprocal lending and communal support traditions | Debt and obligation are often embedded in social reciprocity rather than purely impersonal extraction, though systems vary. | C | Financial obligation should remain compatible with communal responsibility. |

### Discussion

The precise definition and legal treatment of riba is a major Islamic jurisprudential question and should not be flattened into a generic modern economic category. The cross-comparative extraction therefore focuses on the explicit ethical structure surrounding it: avoidance of exploitative gain, preservation of legitimate principal, relief for distressed debtors, and voluntary forgiveness.

### Conclusion

Protection against exploitative debt and accommodation of hardship have substantial cross-traditional parallels, with particularly direct precedent in Jewish scripture.

# E3 Unit 029 — Qur'an 2:282

## Ethics of Contracts, Evidence, and Documentary Accountability

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:282 gives detailed instructions for deferred debt: write the obligation down, use a scribe fairly, have the debtor dictate, protect parties with reduced capacity through a guardian, obtain witnesses, avoid harming scribes or witnesses, and preserve evidence rather than relying upon uncertain memory.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Document significant obligations** | Important deferred transactions should be recorded clearly. |
| **Use neutral record-keeping** | The person recording an agreement should do so justly. |
| **Protect persons with reduced bargaining or communicative capacity** | Where a party cannot adequately represent their own interests, safeguards should prevent exploitation. |
| **Use evidence to reduce dispute** | Reliable records and witnesses protect all parties. |
| **Do not intimidate witnesses or record-keepers** | Institutions of accountability must themselves be protected from coercion. |
| **Proportional procedure** | Formality should respond to the risk and nature of the transaction rather than exist for its own sake. |

### Comparative Analysis — Record Obligations and Protect Evidence

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity / biblical tradition** — Luke 16:1–7; broader biblical witness ethics | Written accounts and truthful dealing appear, though no New Testament contractual rule matches the Qur'anic detail. | B/C | Financial dealings should be accountable and truthful. |
| **Judaism** — Deuteronomy 19:15; extensive documentary rules in Jewish law | Multiple witnesses and formal documentation protect adjudication and transactions. | A | Important claims should rest on reliable evidence. |
| **Buddhism** — precepts of truthful dealing | Honesty is clear, but a direct canonical documentary-contract equivalent is weak. | C | Transactions should not depend on deception. |
| **Hinduism** — Dharmashastra traditions on documents and witnesses | Classical legal traditions recognise documentary and testimonial evidence. | A/B | Record and evidence obligations so disputes can be adjudicated fairly. |
| **Sikhism** — honest dealing | Strong ethical support exists for honesty, but no directly equivalent canonical documentary procedure is central. | C | Commercial dealings should be truthful and fair. |
| **Confucianism** — trustworthiness and administrative record traditions | Social order depends on reliable commitments and responsible administration. | B | Public and private obligations require trustworthy handling. |
| **African Traditional** — witnessed agreements and communal adjudication | Oral legal cultures frequently use witnesses and community memory to establish obligations, though practices vary. | B | Significant agreements should be socially verifiable. |

### Discussion

This is one of the clearest examples so far of a Qur'anic ethical principle operating through **institutional design**. The passage does not merely command honesty; it creates procedures that make honest dealing easier to verify and dishonesty harder to conceal.

Its functional ethical core therefore includes a systems principle:

> **Good institutions reduce dependence upon personal virtue alone by creating records, witnesses, and safeguards.**

### Conclusion

The exact contractual procedure is not universal, but evidence, trustworthy records, witness protection, and safeguards for vulnerable parties form a substantial cross-traditional legal-ethical cluster.

# E3 Unit 030 — Qur'an 2:283

## Ethics of Trust, Collateral, and Truthful Testimony

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:283 addresses transactions where written documentation is unavailable, permits security/collateral, requires a trusted person to discharge the trust placed in them, and prohibits concealing testimony.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Honour entrusted obligations** | When another person relies upon your integrity, fulfil the responsibility honestly. |
| **Use safeguards when ordinary documentation is unavailable** | Trust can be supported by proportionate mechanisms rather than blind dependence. |
| **Do not conceal material testimony** | A person possessing relevant evidence should not deliberately suppress it. |
| **Trust and verification can coexist** | Ethical systems need not choose between personal trust and procedural safeguards. |

### Comparative Analysis — Do Not Conceal Material Testimony

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Ephesians 4:25; witness traditions | Truth should be spoken to one another; falsehood and concealment that facilitates injustice conflict with truthful conduct. | B | Give truthful information where others legitimately depend upon it. |
| **Judaism** — Leviticus 5:1; Exodus 23:1–2 | A witness who possesses relevant knowledge bears responsibility, and false testimony is prohibited. | A | Do not suppress or corrupt evidence. |
| **Buddhism** — Fourth Precept / Right Speech | False speech is prohibited. | B | Testimony should be truthful. |
| **Hinduism** — satya and legal traditions of witness testimony | Truthfulness, including truthful witness, is a major duty. | A/B | Evidence should not be knowingly falsified or concealed. |
| **Sikhism** — truthful speech and living | Truthfulness is central to ethical conduct. | B | Do not knowingly distort truth for advantage. |
| **Confucianism** — xin, trustworthiness | Reliable social relations depend on truthful and trustworthy conduct. | B | Do not betray legitimate reliance on your word. |
| **African Traditional** — communal adjudication and witness traditions | Truthful testimony before elders or community mechanisms is central in many customary systems. | B | Community justice depends upon honest witnesses. |

### Discussion

Units 029 and 030 form a connected institutional sequence. Unit 029 establishes documentation and witnesses where possible; Unit 030 addresses circumstances where normal documentation is unavailable and supplements trust with collateral while retaining duties of honesty.

The combination is notable because it avoids a false choice between moral character and institutional procedure. **Trust is ethically necessary, but prudent verification is also legitimate.**

### Conclusion

Honouring trust and preserving truthful evidence are strongly convergent principles. The Qur'anic source also contributes a systems-level insight: trustworthy relationships can be reinforced rather than replaced by procedural safeguards.

# Interim Findings: Units 021–030

The third group broadens the Qur'an-derived ethical field substantially.

| Cluster | Units |
|---|---|
| **Child welfare and continuing parental responsibility** | 021 |
| **Respect for bereavement and transitional vulnerability** | 022 |
| **Fairness and generosity beyond strict entitlement** | 023 |
| **Religious non-compulsion** | 024 |
| **Dignity-preserving generosity** | 025, 026, 027 |
| **Reciprocity** | 026 |
| **Protection against economic exploitation** | 028 |
| **Documentary accountability and evidence** | 029, 030 |
| **Protection of vulnerable parties through procedure** | 021, 022, 028, 029 |
| **Trust combined with verification** | 029, 030 |

Several features emerge from the data without being imposed upon it.

First, generosity becomes progressively more qualified. The Qur'anic corpus does not merely repeat "give." Across Units 025–027 it asks **how**, **what**, **to whom**, **with what motive**, and **with what effect on dignity**.

Second, Units 028–030 move strongly into economic and institutional ethics. They address exploitative finance, debtor hardship, documentation, neutral scribes, witnesses, vulnerable contracting parties, collateral, trust, and suppression of evidence.

Third, Unit 024 is methodologically important because it does not produce uniform equivalence across all seven comparison traditions. This is retained as a genuine result rather than corrected through weak analogies.

Finally, the institutional pattern observed in Units 001–020 becomes more pronounced. The Qur'anic ethical material encountered so far frequently does not rely solely on exhorting individuals to be virtuous. It also establishes **procedures intended to constrain foreseeable misuse of power, information, property, and dependency**.

These remain interim observations. They must not be treated as the final Qur'an-derived ethical architecture until the anchor corpus has been systematically processed.
