# E3 Unit 181 — Qur'an 65:6–7
## Support During Separation According to Need and Capacity

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 65:6–7 requires appropriate housing and support during marital separation, prohibits using living arrangements to harass, protects pregnant women through delivery, provides compensation for nursing, recommends mutual consultation, and scales expenditure to actual means.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not weaponise economic dependence during relationship breakdown.**
- **Transitional support should protect vulnerable parties.**
- **Care work should be recognised rather than assumed costless.**
- **Parents should consult over continuing child needs.**
- **Material duties should be calibrated to actual capacity without erasing the duty itself.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Family support duties remain after relational conflict, though no exact canonical procedure parallels the passage. | B/C | Separation does not erase responsibility for dependants. |
| Judaism — ketubah/divorce-support traditions | Marital dissolution carries formal financial obligations. | A/B | Economic power should not be used to abandon a vulnerable spouse. |
| Buddhism | Canonical marriage law is limited; non-harm and responsibility remain relevant. | C | Do not exploit vulnerability during separation. |
| Hinduism | Family-support duties occur across dharmic/legal traditions with substantial variation. | B/C | Separation does not cancel legitimate support duties. |
| Sikhism | Household responsibility and care remain ethical obligations. | B | Do not use family breakdown as licence for exploitation. |
| Confucianism | Family roles carry continuing responsibilities, though divorce structures differ historically. | B/C | Relational change does not erase all role duties. |
| African Traditional | Divorce settlements and kin obligations vary widely but often involve family/community processes. | B/C | Separation can create continuing support obligations. |

### Conclusion
This source strengthens the corpus's capacity-aware procedural ethics: obligation is neither unlimited nor disposable.

# E3 Unit 182 — Qur'an 68:10–13
## Reject Slander, Malicious Gossip, and Obstruction of Good

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 68:10–13 condemns a cluster of behaviours including habitual swearing, contempt, slander, circulating malicious talk, obstructing good, aggression, and persistent wrongdoing.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not make reputational attack a habitual social practice.**
- **Do not carry malicious information between people to generate conflict.**
- **Do not obstruct beneficial action merely from hostility or self-interest.**
- **Repeated patterns of aggression matter in assessing character and trustworthiness.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 3; Ephesians 4:31 | Slander and malicious speech are condemned. | A | Do not weaponise speech against others. |
| Judaism — lashon hara / rechilut | Slander and tale-bearing are extensively constrained. | A | Do not circulate harmful gossip. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Slanderous and divisive speech is prohibited. | A | Do not use speech to divide. |
| Hinduism — truthful/non-injurious speech | Malicious falsehood conflicts with ethical speech. | A/B | Speak without harmful deception. |
| Sikhism — rejection of slander | Nindya is ethically condemned. | A/B | Do not degrade others through malicious speech. |
| Confucianism — trustworthy speech | Petty, divisive speech is inconsistent with cultivated character. | B | Do not create disorder through speech. |
| African Traditional | Gossip can be socially regulated in many communities, but functions vary. | C | Malicious speech can fracture communal trust. |

### Conclusion
The passage treats corrosive speech as a behavioural pattern rather than an isolated verbal mistake.

# E3 Unit 183 — Qur'an 68:17–33
## Wealth Does Not Eliminate Obligations to the Poor

### Qur'anic Source
The garden narrative in Qur'an 68:17–33 depicts owners planning to harvest secretly and prevent poor people from receiving a customary share; their scheme collapses and is recognised as wrongdoing.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Control of productive assets does not eliminate obligations toward vulnerable people.**
- **Secret coordination to evade social responsibility is ethically significant.**
- **Efficiency in excluding the poor is not moral success.**
- **Property use can be judged by effects on people whose livelihood depends upon established access or generosity.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 12:16–21; 16:19–31 | Wealth without responsibility toward others is sharply criticised. | A/B | Prosperity does not cancel obligations to the needy. |
| Judaism — gleaning laws, Leviticus 19:9–10 | Harvest owners must leave provision for poor people and strangers. | A | Productive property carries distributive duties. |
| Buddhism — dana | Generosity counters possessiveness. | B | Wealth should not become exclusionary attachment. |
| Hinduism — dana | Giving from material surplus is a recognised duty. | B | Prosperity creates opportunities for generosity. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing earnings and food is central. | A/B | Do not monopolise abundance. |
| Confucianism — humane use of wealth | Prosperity should remain subordinate to humaneness. | B | Wealth should not harden into disregard for need. |
| African Traditional | Harvest sharing and kin/community claims occur widely but vary locally. | B | Productive abundance often carries reciprocal obligations. |

### Conclusion
The narrative is a strong property-stewardship source because the wrongdoing lies specifically in coordinated exclusion from abundance.

# E3 Unit 184 — Qur'an 70:22–25
## Recognise a Claim of the Needy on Wealth

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 70:22–25 praises people who recognise a defined claim in their wealth for those who ask and those deprived.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Support for people in need need not be treated as optional sentiment alone.**
- **A portion of controlled resources can carry recognised social claims.**
- **Need exists both among visible requesters and people whose deprivation is less visible.**
- **Distribution systems should not depend solely on a person's willingness or ability to ask.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Acts 4:32–35 | Resources are distributed according to need in the early community. | A/B | Need creates strong claims on communal resources. |
| Judaism — tzedakah | Assistance to the poor is understood as justice/duty, not merely optional generosity. | A | Wealth carries enforceable moral obligations. |
| Buddhism — dana | Giving is central though usually framed more as virtue than recipient right. | B | Direct resources toward need. |
| Hinduism — dana | Giving is a major duty. | B | Wealth should support vulnerable people. |
| Sikhism — dasvandh / vand chhakna | Sharing a portion of earnings is institutionalised. | A | Set aside resources for others. |
| Confucianism — relief obligations | Humane government should ensure subsistence. | B | Need can create social duties. |
| African Traditional | Kinship and mutual-aid systems often create claims beyond voluntary charity. | B | Resources are embedded in reciprocal social obligations. |

### Conclusion
The distinctive feature is the language of **claim or due**, shifting aid from discretionary benevolence toward distributive obligation.

# E3 Unit 185 — Qur'an 70:29–32
## Sexual Boundaries, Trusts, and Promises

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 70:29–32 groups sexual restraint with faithful observance of trusts and promises.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Sexual conduct should respect legitimate relational boundaries.**
- **Trustworthiness applies across intimate and non-intimate domains.**
- **Promises create responsibilities beyond immediate desire.**
- **Self-restraint and reliability are mutually reinforcing aspects of character.**

|---|---|---:|---|

| Buddhism — Third Precept / Right Speech | Sexual misconduct and falsehood are restrained. | A/B | Desire does not cancel responsibility. |
| Hinduism — sexual restraint and satya | Sexual discipline and truthfulness are virtues. | B | Govern appetite and honour commitments. |
| Sikhism — restraint from lust / truthful living | Intimacy and speech are both ethically disciplined. | B | Be faithful and trustworthy. |
| Confucianism — family propriety / xin | Relationship duties and trustworthiness sustain order. | B | Reliability spans family and public life. |
| African Traditional | Sexual and promise norms vary widely. | C | Stable relationships depend on recognised boundaries and trust. |

### Conclusion
The grouping suggests a general architecture of reliability across domains rather than isolated rules.

This is another direct evidentiary-integrity source.

# E3 Unit 188 — Qur'an 76:8–9
## Give Without Demanding Return or Recognition

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 76:8–9 praises feeding the needy, orphan, and captive despite one's own attachment to food, without seeking repayment or thanks.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Aid should not be made conditional on gratitude or social indebtedness.**
- **People outside one's immediate group, including captives, can remain recipients of care.**
- **Generosity is ethically stronger when it does not become a mechanism for domination.**
- **Do not convert assistance into compulsory praise or reciprocal obligation.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 6:34–35 | Give without expecting repayment. | A | Do good without making return the condition. |
| Judaism — higher forms of tzedakah | Giving can be structured to minimise humiliation and personal credit. | A/B | Aid should preserve recipient dignity. |
| Buddhism — dana without attachment | Giving should be free from grasping at reward. | A | Give without attachment to return. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 17:20 | Sattvic giving is given from duty without expectation of return. | A | Give without seeking repayment. |
| Sikhism — seva | Service is ideally selfless. | A | Help without egoic demand for recognition. |
| Confucianism — benevolence | Humaneness should not be reduced to transactional exchange. | B | Beneficence need not demand immediate return. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocity is often important, making exact equivalence variable. | C | Assistance need not always be an immediate transaction. |

### Conclusion
This source distinguishes generosity from patronage: aid should not manufacture dependence through demanded gratitude.

# E3 Unit 189 — Qur'an 83:1–3
## Do Not Demand Full Measure While Giving Less

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 83:1–3 condemns people who demand full measure when receiving but diminish measure when giving.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not apply favourable standards to yourself and inferior standards to others.**
- **Reciprocity requires symmetry in transactional rules.**
- **Hidden asymmetry in measurement is both fraud and hypocrisy.**
- **Fair systems should prevent actors from controlling standards differently depending on whether they gain or lose.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 7:12; Leviticus 19:35–36 | Reciprocity and honest measures are explicit. | A | Apply fair standards in both directions. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 25:13–16 | Different weights for advantage are prohibited. | A | Do not use asymmetric measures. |
| Buddhism — Right Livelihood | Fraudulent exchange is prohibited. | B | Do not exploit informational advantage. |
| Hinduism — commercial dharma | False measures are condemned. | A/B | Use the same honest standard for self and other. |
| Sikhism — honest earning | Gain should not depend on cheating. | B | Trade fairly. |
| Confucianism — reciprocity | Do not impose on others what one would reject oneself. | A/B | Use reciprocal standards. |
| African Traditional | Market fairness norms vary but cheating is broadly disruptive. | B/C | Exchange should not privilege one side through hidden standards. |

### Conclusion
This repeated measurement ethic adds a particularly clear anti-double-standard formulation.

# E3 Unit 190 — Qur'an 89:17–20
## Do Not Neglect Orphans, the Poor, or Inheritance Justice

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 89:17–20 condemns failure to honour orphans, failure to encourage feeding the poor, consuming inheritance greedily, and excessive love of wealth.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Protect people lacking ordinary social power.**
- **Communities should encourage support for the poor rather than treating need as invisible.**
- **Do not manipulate inheritance for private accumulation.**
- **Attachment to wealth can distort duties of justice and care.**
- **Ethical failure can be collective when communities cease encouraging one another toward support.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 1:27; Matthew 25:35 | Orphans and people in need receive explicit concern. | A | Protect vulnerable people and feed the hungry. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 24:17–22 | Orphans, widows, and strangers receive strong legal protection. | A | Structure economic life around protection of vulnerability. |
| Buddhism — dana | Giving to those in need is strongly praised. | B | Share resources with vulnerable people. |
| Hinduism — dana and inheritance duties | Generosity and family/property obligations constrain greed. | B | Wealth should not erase duties to others. |
| Sikhism — langar / vand chhakna | Feeding and sharing are institutionalised. | A | Ensure access to food and shared resources. |
| Confucianism — humane government | People lacking subsistence should receive social concern. | B | Prosperity carries responsibilities toward vulnerable people. |
| African Traditional | Orphan care and kin redistribution are commonly embedded in extended-family structures, though practices vary. | B | Vulnerability creates communal obligations. |

### Conclusion
The source joins distributive justice, inheritance integrity, and critique of wealth fixation into one social-responsibility cluster.
