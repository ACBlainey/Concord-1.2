# E3 Unit 351 — Qur'an 16:90
## Justice, Beneficence, Kin Support, and Rejection of Oppression Form a Compact Ethical Architecture

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 16:90 commands justice, excellence or beneficence, and giving to relatives while prohibiting indecency, wrongdoing, and oppression.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Justice establishes a floor while beneficence can exceed it.
- Social responsibility includes but is not exhausted by kinship.
- Ethical systems need both positive duties and prohibitions.
- Oppression is incompatible with legitimate social order.
- A compact moral framework can combine restraint, fairness, and active care.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Justice, love, generosity, and rejection of oppression strongly converge. | A | Combine justice with active beneficence. |
| Judaism — Micah 6:8 and charity law | Justice and loving conduct strongly converge. | A | Justice should coexist with mercy and care. |
| Buddhism | Non-harm, compassion, and generosity provide strong convergence. | A/B | Avoid harm while actively doing good. |
| Hinduism | Dharma, ahimsa, and dana strongly converge. | A/B | Join right order with beneficence. |
| Sikhism | Justice, seva, sharing, and resistance to oppression strongly converge. | A | Build ethics from justice plus service. |
| Confucianism — yi and ren | Righteousness and benevolence are complementary. | A | Fairness and humane concern belong together. |
| African Traditional | Justice/reciprocity/care traditions vary but commonly overlap functionally. | B | Stable community requires both restraint and positive obligation. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 108.** This remains one of the anchor corpus's most compact high-level ethical summaries.

# E3 Unit 352 — Qur'an 16:91–92
## Do Not Turn Commitments Into Instruments of Strategic Deception

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 16:91–92 commands fulfilment of covenants and condemns using oaths deceptively because one group is stronger or more advantageous than another.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Agreements should not be honoured only while convenient.
- Power imbalance does not legitimise opportunistic breach.
- Formal commitments must not become bait for strategic deception.
- Trust requires stability across changing incentives.
- Institutional reliability is tested when betrayal becomes profitable.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:37 | Speech and commitments should be reliable. | A/B | Do not weaponise promises. |
| Judaism | Covenant and oath integrity are strongly developed. | A | Honour commitments despite changing advantage. |
| Buddhism | Truthful speech opposes deceptive promises. | B | Do not manipulate reliance through false commitment. |
| Hinduism — satya | Truthfulness constrains strategic deception. | B | Keep legitimate commitments. |
| Sikhism | Truthful living strongly opposes opportunistic betrayal. | B | Reliability should survive incentive change. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthiness is central to social order. | A | Keep faith even when breach is advantageous. |
| African Traditional | Covenant/oath practices vary. | B/C | Agreements require reliable performance. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 109 and covenant-integrity cluster.**

Qur'an 16:125 instructs invitation through wisdom, good instruction, and argument conducted in the best manner.

- Persuasion should not default to coercion.
- Communication should be adapted to understanding and context.
- Disagreement can be rigorous without becoming degrading.

# E3 Unit 354 — Qur'an 17:23–24
## Ageing Parents Should Receive Care Without Humiliation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:23–24 commands good treatment of parents, especially in old age, prohibiting expressions of contempt and requiring humble, compassionate speech.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Dependency in old age should not reduce dignity.
- Care obligations include manner as well as material provision.
- Irritation does not justify humiliation of dependants.
- Increased vulnerability can intensify duties of patience.
- Intergenerational reciprocity is part of social continuity.

### Comparative Analysis

|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Mark 7:10–13 / 1 Timothy 5:4 | Parental honour and material care are strongly supported. | A | Care for ageing parents with dignity. |

| Buddhism | Gratitude and repayment of parental care are strongly valued. | A/B | Support parents in recognition of prior care. |
| Hinduism | Filial duty toward parents is strongly developed. | A | Care for parents respectfully. |
| Sikhism | Family responsibility and service provide strong convergence. | B | Do not abandon ageing parents. |
| Confucianism — filial piety | Care and respectful manner toward parents are central. | A | Filial care includes attitude and conduct. |
| African Traditional | Elder care is highly important in many traditions but diverse. | A/B | Ageing elders retain dignity and claims of care. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 112.** Cross-traditional convergence is exceptionally strong.

# E3 Unit 355 — Qur'an 17:26–29
## Resource Ethics Requires Generosity Without Squandering or Self-Ruin

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:26–29 requires giving relatives, poor people, and travellers what is due, condemns waste, instructs kind speech when unable to give, and rejects both miserliness and ruinous overextension.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Resource stewardship must balance generosity and sustainability.
- Inability to provide material aid does not justify degrading speech.
- Waste and hoarding are opposite failures.
- Assistance should be durable rather than self-destructive.
- Social duty includes both allocation and treatment of applicants.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Generosity, stewardship, and proportional giving strongly converge. | A/B | Give responsibly and preserve dignity. |
| Judaism | Tzedakah is obligatory but bounded against destructive self-impoverishment. | A | Sustainable generosity balances duties. |
| Buddhism — Middle Way / dana | Giving and avoidance of extremes strongly converge. | A | Be generous without destructive excess. |
| Hinduism — dana / moderation | Proper giving is disciplined rather than reckless. | A/B | Give appropriately and sustainably. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing is integrated with honest household life. | A | Sustainable earning and sharing belong together. |
| Confucianism — mean / benevolence | Benevolence is governed by appropriate measure. | A/B | Care should be proportionate and sustainable. |
| African Traditional | Sharing obligations vary. | B | Mutual aid should preserve community continuity. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Units 113–115 and equilibrium/resource cluster.**

# E3 Unit 356 — Qur'an 17:31
## Economic Fear Does Not Justify Destroying Dependants

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:31 prohibits killing children because of fear of poverty and frames provision as a concern beyond the parent's immediate calculation.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Dependants should not bear lethal consequences of resource anxiety.
- Scarcity fears can distort moral judgement.
- Economic pressure does not erase basic protections of life.
- Vulnerable persons should not become expendable variables in resource optimisation.
- Systems should address scarcity without sacrificing those with least power.

### Comparative Analysis

| Judaism | Child sacrifice is strongly prohibited and life protected. | A | Scarcity does not justify killing dependants. |
| Buddhism — First Precept | Killing is prohibited. | A | Do not resolve scarcity through lethal harm. |
| Hinduism — ahimsa | Non-harm strongly converges. | A | Protect vulnerable life. |
| Sikhism | Human dignity and family responsibility strongly converge. | A/B | Economic difficulty does not erase protection. |
| Confucianism | Family care and humane governance oppose abandonment of dependants. | B | Protect dependants under hardship. |
| African Traditional | Child and scarcity norms vary historically and culturally. | C | No uniform analogue asserted. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 116 and sanctity-of-life cluster.**

# E3 Unit 357 — Qur'an 17:33
## Retaliatory Authority Must Not Exceed Its Legitimate Boundary

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:33 prohibits wrongful killing and, while recognising a claim for the representative of a murder victim, prohibits excess in retaliation.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Recognition of victim rights does not legitimise unlimited revenge.
- Redress should remain proportionate.
- Delegated retaliatory authority requires explicit limits.
- One wrongful death should not expand into cycles of collective vengeance.
- Justice must constrain the injured party as well as the original offender.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Forgiveness and restraint strongly constrain retaliation. | A/B | Do not escalate vengeance. |
| Judaism — lex talionis | Proportionality directly limits retaliation. | A | Redress should not exceed the wrong. |
| Buddhism | Non-harm strongly discourages retaliatory violence. | B | Break cycles of vengeance. |
| Hinduism | Proportional justice and restraint provide functional parallels. | B | Sanction should remain bounded. |
| Sikhism | Legitimate defence is recognised but vengeance constrained. | A/B | Justice differs from excess retaliation. |
| Confucianism | Punishment should be ordered and proportionate rather than private feud. | B | Bound redress through legitimate norms. |
| African Traditional | Compensation/retaliation systems vary. | B/C | Proportional settlement can restrain feud. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 118 and proportionality cluster.**

# E3 Unit 358 — Qur'an 24:11–16
## Do Not Amplify Serious Allegations Merely Because They Are Circulating

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:11–16 addresses a circulating scandal and criticises people for passing it from tongue to tongue without knowledge, treating a grave allegation as trivial speech.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Repetition does not convert allegation into evidence.
- Information forwarding creates responsibility even when one did not originate the claim.
- High-impact accusations deserve elevated verification thresholds.
- Social networks can magnify harm through low-friction transmission.
- Responsible actors should interrupt rather than automatically propagate unverified scandal.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 3 / false-witness tradition | Harmful speech and false accusation are strongly constrained. | A/B | Do not spread unverified harmful claims. |
| Judaism — lashon hara | Harmful gossip and reputational speech are extensively regulated. | A | Do not amplify damaging reports irresponsibly. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | False, divisive, and harmful speech is prohibited. | A | Verify and restrain harmful transmission. |
| Hinduism — satya / disciplined speech | Truthful, beneficial speech strongly converges. | A/B | Do not circulate harmful uncertainty as fact. |
| Sikhism | Slander is strongly condemned. | A/B | Protect reputation from irresponsible speech. |
| Confucianism | Trustworthy speech and careful judgement provide functional convergence. | B | Do not let rumour substitute for knowledge. |
| African Traditional | Gossip/reputation norms vary. | C | Rumour can damage persons and community. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 134 and information-integrity cluster.**

# E3 Unit 359 — Qur'an 24:27–28
## Refusal of Entry Should Be Accepted Without Retaliatory Entitlement

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:27–28 requires permission before entering another's home and directs the visitor to leave if told to return.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Consent includes the right to refuse.
- A request for access does not create entitlement to access.
- Boundaries remain legitimate even when refusal disappoints the requester.
- Respecting “no” is part of privacy, not an optional courtesy.
- Consent should be revocable and behaviourally enforceable.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Household privacy and hospitality ethics provide functional but indirect parallels. | C | Invitation cannot be presumed. |
| Judaism | Privacy protections and household boundaries provide strong parallels. | B | Respect refusal of access. |
| Buddhism | Monastic access and interpersonal boundaries provide functional convergence. | B/C | Do not intrude without permission. |
| Hinduism | Household/guest propriety provides partial convergence. | C | Entry follows recognised permission. |
| Sikhism | No close canonical procedural analogue identified. | C/D | Respect another's boundary. |
| Confucianism — li | Proper access and guest conduct are regulated by propriety. | B | Do not override household boundaries. |
| African Traditional | Household access norms vary. | C | Permission structures differ but boundaries are socially recognised. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 272 while preserving the explicit right of refusal.**

# E3 Unit 360 — Qur'an 24:58–59
## Privacy Permissions Should Develop With Maturity

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:58–59 gives household permission rules for children and dependants at private times and changes those expectations when children reach maturity.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Privacy expectations can be developmentally graduated.
- Maturity changes both agency and responsibility.
- Household familiarity does not erase boundaries.
- Permission systems should evolve rather than remain permanently childlike.
- Developmental governance should increase responsibility alongside capacity.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|

| Judaism | Age-dependent obligations and privacy norms provide functional parallels. | B | Responsibilities develop with maturity. |
| Buddhism | Novice/full ordination distinctions show graduated responsibilities, though not household privacy specifically. | C | Capacity/status can alter permissions. |
| Hinduism | Life-stage frameworks strongly recognise changing responsibilities. | B | Development changes duty. |
| Sikhism | No close canonical analogue identified. | C | Maturity entails increased responsibility. |
| Confucianism | Age and role strongly structure responsibilities. | B | Permissions and duties develop over time. |
| African Traditional | Age-grade systems are widespread but diverse. | B | Social responsibility can graduate with maturity. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 275.** This has particular relevance to the Concord's Agency Graduation Framework.
