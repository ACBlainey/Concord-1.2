## Introduction to the Extraction Protocol

E3 repeats the original *Unified Paths of Light* comparison from the opposite textual direction. Ethical principles are first extracted from the Qur'an and reduced to their functional behavioural cores. Only then are equivalents sought in the seven comparison traditions.

Exact verbal equivalence is neither expected nor required. The test is functional: what conduct does a teaching prescribe or prohibit, and how directly does another tradition teach substantially the same conduct?

Evidence is graded:

- **A — Direct textual equivalent**
- **B — Strong functional equivalent**
- **C — Inferred / corollary equivalent**
- **D — No clear equivalent identified**

The grades concern the strength of the claimed comparison, not the value of a religion or teaching.

# E3 Unit 001 — Qur'an 2:44

## Ethics of Consistency Between Teaching and Conduct

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:44 criticises commanding righteousness of other people while neglecting one's own conduct.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Practice what you prescribe** | Do not demand moral conduct from others while exempting yourself from the same standard. |
| **Moral self-examination** | Ethical instruction should be accompanied by reflection upon one's own behaviour. |

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 7:3–5; 23:3 | Criticism of judging or instructing others while failing to correct one's own conduct. | A | Apply moral standards to yourself before imposing them on others. |
| **Judaism** — Talmud, Bava Metzia 107b (self-correction before correcting another) | Rabbinic teaching warns a reprover to address the same failing in himself. | A | Correct yourself before rebuking another. |
| **Buddhism** — Dhammapada 158–159 | One should establish oneself in what is proper before instructing others; act as one teaches. | A | Make conduct consistent with moral teaching. |
| **Hinduism** — Bhagavad Gita 3:21 | The conduct of exemplary persons becomes a model followed by others. | B | Moral leadership requires exemplary conduct. |
| **Sikhism** — Guru Granth Sahib, teaching on truthful living | Truth is not merely spoken; it must be lived. | B | Embody the values you profess. |
| **Confucianism** — Analects 13.6 | Upright personal conduct can guide others without coercive instruction. | B | Leadership begins with one's own example. |
| **African Traditional** — communal/proverbial traditions of exemplary elderhood | Moral authority is ordinarily tied to demonstrated character and responsible example. | C | Those who guide others should model the conduct expected of the community. |

### Discussion

The convergence is strong. Christianity, Judaism, and Buddhism contain particularly close formulations of the self-application principle. Hindu, Sikh, and Confucian sources frame it more through exemplary conduct than hypocrisy itself. The African comparison is functional and therefore retained at Grade C rather than presented as a direct textual match.

### Conclusion

The Qur'anic principle that moral instruction should not be separated from personal conduct has strong equivalents across the comparison set.

# E3 Unit 002 — Qur'an 2:83

## Ethics of Family Duty, Vulnerable Persons, and Good Speech

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:83 recalls a covenant requiring worship of God together with good treatment of parents, relatives, orphans and the needy, and good speech toward people.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Care for parents and kin** | Treat parents and close relatives with active goodness. |
| **Protect vulnerable people** | Extend care to orphans and people in material need. |
| **Speak well to others** | Use good, constructive, or humane speech in dealing with people. |

### Comparative Analysis — Care for Parents, Kin, and Vulnerable Persons

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Mark 7:10; James 1:27 | Honour parents; care for orphans and widows in distress. | A | Family duty and protection of vulnerable people are moral obligations. |
| **Judaism** — Exodus 20:12; Deuteronomy 24:17–22 | Honour parents and protect widows, orphans, strangers, and the poor. | A | Honour family and prevent exploitation of vulnerable people. |
| **Buddhism** — Sigalovada Sutta | Children owe duties to parents; generosity and compassionate conduct are commended. | B | Repay parental care and assist others compassionately. |
| **Hinduism** — Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11; traditions of dana | Treat mother and father with reverence and practise generosity. | B | Honour parents and give to those in need. |
| **Sikhism** — Guru Granth Sahib / seva and vand chhakna | Service and sharing with others, including those in need, are central ethical practices. | B | Serve others and share resources. |
| **Confucianism** — Analects 1.2; 2.5–7 | Filial piety is foundational to moral character. | A | Treat parents with sustained respect and care. |
| **African Traditional** — Ubuntu and extended-family ethics | Personhood carries reciprocal duties toward kin and vulnerable community members. | B | Care is a communal responsibility, not merely an individual choice. |

### Discussion

This unit contains several principles rather than one. The strongest cross-traditional convergence concerns family obligation and protection of vulnerable people. The Qur'anic combination of kinship duty, social welfare, and good speech places interpersonal ethics within a single behavioural cluster.

### Conclusion

Care for parents, kin, and vulnerable members of society is strongly represented across the comparison traditions.

# E3 Unit 003 — Qur'an 2:84–85

## Ethics of Non-Violence Within Community and Moral Consistency

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:84–85 recalls a covenant not to shed one another's blood or expel one another from homes, then condemns violating part of that covenant while observing another part.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Do not shed innocent/community blood** | Do not use lethal violence against members of the human or civic community without justification. |
| **Do not dispossess people unjustly** | Do not drive people from their homes through wrongful force. |
| **Reject selective morality** | Do not obey ethical rules only when convenient while violating closely connected duties. |

### Comparative Analysis — Reject Selective Morality

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — James 2:10–11 | Selective obedience does not erase violation of another moral command. | A | Ethical duties cannot simply be cherry-picked. |
| **Judaism** — Torah covenantal ethics | The commandments form an integrated field of obligation rather than optional isolated preferences. | B | Moral obligation requires consistency. |
| **Buddhism** — Five Precepts / Noble Eightfold Path | Ethical discipline operates as a coherent practice rather than selective observance. | B | Cultivate consistent restraint across conduct. |
| **Hinduism** — dharma traditions | Dharma entails interconnected duties appropriate to moral and social life. | B | Duty is not legitimately reduced to convenient fragments. |
| **Sikhism** — truthful living | Religious profession without ethical conduct is repeatedly criticised. | B | Integrity requires consistency between commitment and action. |
| **Confucianism** — Analects, cultivation of the junzi | Virtue is a coherent character practice, not occasional compliance. | B | Ethical character requires consistency. |
| **African Traditional** — communal responsibility | A person cannot claim communal standing while selectively violating reciprocal obligations. | C | Membership carries mutually connected duties. |

### Discussion

The Qur'anic passage combines concrete prohibitions—bloodshed and expulsion—with a meta-ethical criticism of selective obedience. That latter principle is particularly useful for comparison because it concerns the integrity of an ethical system itself.

### Conclusion

The rejection of selective morality has broad functional equivalents, while the passage also preserves concrete protections against violence and wrongful displacement.

# E3 Unit 004 — Qur'an 2:109

## Ethics of Forgiveness and Forbearance

### Qur'anic Source

Within a context of hostility and religious opposition, Qur'an 2:109 instructs believers to forgive and overlook/forbear pending further judgement.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Forbear under provocation** | Hostility does not always require immediate retaliation. |
| **Forgive** | Where possible, release grievance rather than perpetuating conflict. |

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 5:44; 18:21–22 | Love enemies and practise repeated forgiveness. | A | Respond to hostility without reproducing it. |
| **Judaism** — Leviticus 19:18 | Do not take vengeance or bear a grudge. | A | Reject vengeance and sustained resentment. |
| **Buddhism** — Dhammapada 3–5 | Hatred is not ended by hatred but by non-hatred. | A | Break cycles of hostility rather than reciprocating them. |
| **Hinduism** — Mahabharata traditions praising forgiveness | Forgiveness and restraint are treated as virtues. | B | Exercise restraint rather than retaliatory anger. |
| **Sikhism** — Guru Granth Sahib, overcoming anger and enmity | Spiritual discipline rejects hatred and cultivates compassion. | B | Do not allow hostility to govern conduct. |
| **Confucianism** — Analects 14.36 | Injury should be answered through uprightness rather than uncontrolled vengeance. | B | Respond to wrongdoing through principled conduct. |
| **African Traditional** — Ubuntu-oriented reconciliation practices | Restoring relationship and community may take priority over perpetual retaliation. | B | Seek restoration where conflict can be reconciled. |

### Discussion

The sources differ on whether forgiveness is unconditional, discretionary, or embedded within justice. Nevertheless, restraint from retaliatory cycles is widely represented. E3 therefore treats the common functional core as forbearance and conflict de-escalation rather than claiming identical doctrines of forgiveness.

### Conclusion

Forbearance under hostility shows strong cross-traditional convergence, though traditions differ in how forgiveness relates to justice.

# E3 Unit 005 — Qur'an 2:168–169

## Ethics of Good Consumption and Claims Based on Knowledge

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:168–169 calls people toward what is lawful and good and warns against evil, indecency, and speaking about God without knowledge.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Choose what is good rather than harmful or corrupt** | Direct ordinary choices toward what is ethically sound. |
| **Reject indecent or destructive conduct** | Do not follow invitations toward recognised wrongdoing. |
| **Do not assert what you do not know** | Avoid presenting unsupported claims as knowledge. |

### Comparative Analysis — Do Not Assert What You Do Not Know

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — James 3:1; Matthew 12:36 | Speech carries responsibility and teachers face heightened accountability. | B | Speak responsibly, especially when claiming authority. |
| **Judaism** — Pirkei Avot 5:7 | The wise acknowledge what they have not heard or do not know. | A | Intellectual honesty includes admitting ignorance. |
| **Buddhism** — right speech / avoidance of false speech | Do not knowingly speak falsely. | B | Speech should correspond to what is responsibly known to be true. |
| **Hinduism** — traditions of satya | Truthfulness is a central discipline. | B | Do not substitute false assertion for truth. |
| **Sikhism** — truthful living and truthful speech | Truth is foundational to ethical life. | B | Align speech with truth. |
| **Confucianism** — Analects 2.17 | Knowing what one knows and recognising what one does not know is knowledge. | A | Admit the limits of knowledge. |
| **African Traditional** — proverbial traditions concerning truthful speech and wise restraint | Responsible speech is associated with wisdom and social trust. | C | Do not speak authoritatively beyond what can responsibly be claimed. |

### Discussion

The epistemic principle is unusually explicit in the Qur'anic unit. Confucian and Jewish formulations provide especially close parallels. Other traditions more often approach the same functional territory through truthfulness and restraint in speech.

### Conclusion

The prohibition on unsupported authoritative claims has clear parallels, although some traditions formulate it more generally as truthful or disciplined speech.

# E3 Unit 006 — Qur'an 2:177

## Ethics of Righteousness as Conduct

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:177 contrasts ritual orientation with a broader account of righteousness including giving wealth to relatives and vulnerable people, freeing those in bondage, keeping promises, and remaining patient in hardship.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Righteousness requires action** | Moral worth is demonstrated through conduct, not external form alone. |
| **Give to vulnerable people** | Use resources to assist relatives, orphans, the poor, travellers, and those asking for help. |
| **Support liberation from bondage** | Use resources to restore freedom where people are unfree. |
| **Keep promises** | Honour commitments once made. |
| **Practise patience under hardship** | Maintain ethical steadiness during deprivation, distress, and danger. |

### Comparative Analysis — Righteousness Requires Action

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — James 2:14–17 | Faith without corresponding action is dead. | A | Moral/religious commitment must be expressed through conduct. |
| **Judaism** — prophetic and Torah ethics | Ritual observance does not excuse injustice or neglect of ethical duties. | A | Worship and ethical conduct cannot be separated. |
| **Buddhism** — Dhammapada 19–20 | Reciting teachings without practising them has little value. | A | Knowledge or profession without practice is insufficient. |
| **Hinduism** — Bhagavad Gita's emphasis on disciplined action | Right action is central to the ethical path. | B | Ethical life is enacted through conduct. |
| **Sikhism** — truthful living | Living truthfully is superior to merely speaking of truth. | A | Truth must become lived behaviour. |
| **Confucianism** — Analects, virtue expressed through cultivated conduct | Moral cultivation is demonstrated in behaviour and relationships. | B | Virtue is practical, not merely verbal. |
| **African Traditional** — communal ethics | Character is judged through fulfilment of responsibilities to others. | B | Moral standing is demonstrated in relationship and action. |

### Discussion

This unit is structurally important because it explicitly resists reducing righteousness to ritual form. It combines inward commitment with measurable duties toward others. The same distinction between profession and practice appears strongly in several comparison traditions.

### Conclusion

The principle that righteousness must manifest in conduct rather than external identity alone shows very strong convergence.

# E3 Unit 007 — Qur'an 2:178–179

## Ethics of Bounded Justice, Forgiveness, and Preservation of Life

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:178–179 addresses homicide and legal retaliation, while also providing a path involving forgiveness and appropriate compensation. The passage presents legal restraint as life-preserving.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Justice must be bounded** | Punishment should operate within defined limits rather than uncontrolled vengeance. |
| **Forgiveness may interrupt retaliation** | A harmed party may choose a path other than maximal retaliation. |
| **Preserve life through lawful restraint** | A justice system should reduce cycles of lethal violence. |

### Comparative Analysis — Limit Retaliation

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 5:38–39 | The retaliation formula is explicitly displaced toward non-retaliation in personal conduct. | B | Do not escalate injury through personal vengeance. |
| **Judaism** — Exodus 21:23–25 and rabbinic interpretation | Lex talionis limits punishment to proportional response and developed into monetary compensation. | A | Justice must be proportionate rather than excessive. |
| **Buddhism** — Dhammapada 3–5 | Retaliatory hatred perpetuates conflict. | B | Break cycles of vengeance. |
| **Hinduism** — dharma traditions of proportionate punishment and forgiveness | Justice and restraint coexist within duties of rulership and personal virtue. | B | Punishment should be governed rather than vindictive. |
| **Sikhism** — defence combined with restraint and rejection of vengeance | Force may be bounded by justice rather than hatred. | B | Necessary defence does not license limitless retaliation. |
| **Confucianism** — government through justice and moral order | Punishment should serve ordered society rather than private vengeance. | B | Justice is a public moral function. |
| **African Traditional** — restorative justice traditions | Compensation and restoration can resolve serious wrongs without endless reciprocal violence. | B | Repair can substitute for cycles of revenge. |

### Discussion

The exact legal mechanisms differ substantially. The defensible common denominator is not identical criminal law but the replacement of uncontrolled vengeance with bounded, socially regulated responses to serious harm.

### Conclusion

Proportional justice and interruption of revenge cycles are broadly shared functional ethics, despite major differences in legal form.

# E3 Unit 008 — Qur'an 2:180–182

## Ethics of Fair Testamentary Provision and Protection Against Corruption

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:180–182 concerns testamentary provision for parents and close relatives, condemns knowingly altering a testament, and permits intervention to reconcile feared error or injustice.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Provide fairly for dependants and kin** | Use estate planning responsibly rather than abandoning legitimate family obligations. |
| **Do not corrupt entrusted instructions** | Do not knowingly alter another person's legitimate testament for private purposes. |
| **Correct foreseeable injustice** | Intervention may be justified where an arrangement risks unfairness and reconciliation is possible. |

### Comparative Analysis — Do Not Corrupt Entrusted Property or Instructions

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Luke 16:10–12 | Faithfulness in entrusted property is treated as a moral test. | B | Handle what is entrusted to you faithfully. |
| **Judaism** — prohibitions on theft, fraud, and boundary manipulation | Property and entrusted interests may not be dishonestly appropriated or altered. | B | Do not manipulate another's property rights for gain. |
| **Buddhism** — Second Precept | Refrain from taking what has not been given. | B | Respect others' property and entrusted interests. |
| **Hinduism** — asteya | Non-stealing includes restraint from wrongful appropriation. | B | Do not take or manipulate what belongs to another. |
| **Sikhism** — honest earning and rejection of exploitation | Material dealings should be honest and non-exploitative. | B | Do not profit through dishonest handling of another's interests. |
| **Confucianism** — trustworthiness (xin) | Reliability in obligations is a central virtue. | B | Honour entrusted responsibilities. |
| **African Traditional** — inheritance and custodial obligations in kinship systems | Property transmission commonly carries responsibilities to family and community. | C | Custodians should preserve legitimate family interests. |

### Discussion

The Qur'anic passage is more legally specific than most comparison sources. The strongest common ethical core is fiduciary integrity: a person entrusted with another's property or instructions should not manipulate them for advantage.

### Conclusion

Although testamentary rules vary, honest stewardship of entrusted interests has broad functional equivalents.

# E3 Unit 009 — Qur'an 2:188

## Ethics of Property, Corruption, and Bribery

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:188 prohibits consuming other people's wealth unjustly and using access to authorities to obtain property wrongfully.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Do not take wealth unjustly** | Property should not be acquired through fraud, coercion, manipulation, or other wrongful means. |
| **Do not bribe authorities** | Public or legal power must not be purchased to facilitate injustice. |
| **Do not legalise what you know is wrongful** | A favourable ruling or institutional process does not make knowingly unjust appropriation ethical. |

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Luke 3:13–14; ethical prohibitions on extortion | Officials and others are told not to extort or take more than is due. | A | Do not exploit power for wrongful gain. |
| **Judaism** — Exodus 23:8; Deuteronomy 16:19 | Bribes corrupt judgement and are explicitly prohibited. | A | Do not purchase or distort justice. |
| **Buddhism** — Second Precept / Right Livelihood | Do not take what is not given or sustain oneself through wrongful conduct. | B | Acquire resources without exploitation or theft. |
| **Hinduism** — asteya and duties of just rulers | Theft and corrupt acquisition violate ethical duty. | B | Do not enrich yourself through wrongful appropriation. |
| **Sikhism** — kirat karo / honest earning | Livelihood should be earned honestly rather than through exploitation. | B | Wealth should come through honest work. |
| **Confucianism** — Analects 4.5 | Wealth and status obtained improperly should not be accepted. | A | Reject gain acquired contrary to righteousness. |
| **African Traditional** — communal norms against theft and corrupt appropriation | Wrongful enrichment damages reciprocal trust and communal order. | B | Property acquisition must respect communal justice. |

### Discussion

This is one of the clearest cross-traditional social ethics in the first ten units. The Qur'anic verse combines ordinary property ethics with institutional corruption, recognising that injustice can be committed through apparently lawful authorities as well as direct theft.

### Conclusion

The prohibition of wrongful enrichment is strongly convergent, while the explicit anti-bribery component has especially direct parallels in Jewish and Confucian ethical sources.

# E3 Unit 010 — Qur'an 2:190–194

## Ethics of Restraint and Proportionality in Conflict

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:190–194 occurs in a conflict setting. The passage permits fighting against those fighting the community, prohibits transgression, states that aggression should cease when the opposing side ceases, and bounds retaliation by proportionality.

### Contextual Safeguard

These verses concern armed conflict and cannot responsibly be reduced to a free-standing command either for pacifism or unrestricted violence. The ethical extraction here concerns the **limits placed upon force within the passage**.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Do not transgress in conflict** | Permission to use force does not remove ethical limits. |
| **Cease aggression when hostile action ceases** | Conflict should not be continued merely for vengeance once its stated justification ends. |
| **Keep retaliation proportionate** | Response to aggression should not exceed the injury or aggression being answered. |
| **Distinguish defence from unlimited violence** | The existence of an enemy does not create unrestricted permission to harm. |

### Comparative Analysis — Restraint and Proportionality

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 5:38–44; later just-war tradition | The Gospel strongly restrains personal retaliation; later Christian ethics developed limits on legitimate force. | B | Hostility does not license unlimited retaliation. |
| **Judaism** — Deuteronomy 20; lex talionis traditions | Biblical law places rules around warfare and proportional response. | B | Force and punishment remain bounded by law. |
| **Buddhism** — First Precept and non-harming ethics | Buddhist ethics generally impose stronger restraints on intentional killing rather than constructing an equivalent law of armed retaliation. | C | Conflict does not suspend the moral significance of harm. |
| **Hinduism** — Bhagavad Gita / dharma-yuddha traditions | Warfare is placed within duties and constraints rather than treated as unrestricted violence. | B | Even legitimate conflict is governed by ethical rules. |
| **Sikhism** — dharam yudh tradition | Force may be justified against oppression but is ethically constrained and not reducible to vengeance. | B | Defensive force must remain subordinate to justice. |
| **Confucianism** — humane government and restrained punitive force | Legitimate authority should use coercion for ordered justice rather than cruelty or private anger. | C | Public force requires moral restraint. |
| **African Traditional** — customary conflict, reconciliation, and proportional/restorative practices vary by society | Many traditions distinguish legitimate defence from feud escalation, but no single pan-African textual rule should be asserted. | C | Conflict restraint is tradition-specific and should not be generalised beyond evidence. |

### Discussion

This unit demonstrates why evidence grading matters. Several traditions contain strong principles limiting retaliation, but their doctrines of warfare differ substantially. Buddhism's non-harming framework, Christian non-retaliation, Jewish legal regulation, Hindu and Sikh traditions of ethically bounded force, and diverse African customary systems should not be collapsed into one identical doctrine.

The defensible convergence is narrower:

> **Conflict does not automatically suspend ethical restraint.**

### Conclusion

Qur'an 2:190–194 supplies a bounded-conflict ethic: force is contextual, transgression is prohibited, cessation matters, and retaliation is limited. Comparable restraints occur across the comparison traditions, but the underlying doctrines of legitimate force remain materially different.

# Interim Findings: Units 001–010

The first ten Qur'anic source units already produce several recurring ethical clusters:

| Cluster | Units |
|---|---|
| **Integrity between principle and conduct** | 001, 003, 006 |
| **Care for family and vulnerable people** | 002, 006, 008 |
| **Non-retaliation / bounded retaliation** | 004, 007, 010 |
| **Truth, knowledge, and responsible speech** | 005 |
| **Property integrity and anti-corruption** | 008, 009 |
| **Justice constrained by proportionality and mercy** | 007, 010 |

These are **interim observations only**. They do not establish the final Qur'an-derived ethical core, because the remainder of the Qur'anic corpus has not yet been processed.

A notable early feature is that several passages combine personal virtue with institutional or legal ethics. The anchor corpus therefore appears likely to generate both interpersonal principles and principles concerning the ethical use of formal social power.
