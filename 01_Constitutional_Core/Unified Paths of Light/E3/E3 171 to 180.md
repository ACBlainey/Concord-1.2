# E3 Unit 171 — Qur'an 58:8–10
## Do Not Use Secret Coordination for Harm

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 58:8–10 distinguishes harmful secret consultation—used for wrongdoing, hostility, and disobedience—from private coordination directed toward righteousness and responsible conduct.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Privacy is not inherently unethical; purpose matters.**
- **Do not use confidential channels to coordinate harm.**
- **Secret communication should not become a mechanism for intimidation or conspiracy.**
- **Legitimate confidentiality can coexist with safeguards against covert wrongdoing.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — John 3:20–21; conspiracy cautions | Concealment used to facilitate wrongdoing is morally suspect. | B | Privacy should not shield harmful coordination. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:16 and anti-conspiracy principles | One should not facilitate harm against another. | B | Confidentiality does not excuse participation in wrongdoing. |
| Buddhism — Right Action / Right Speech | Coordinated deception or harm remains unethical whether public or private. | B | Private communication remains subject to non-harm. |
| Hinduism — dharma | Secret means do not automatically legitimise wrongful ends. | B | Method and purpose both remain ethically accountable. |
| Sikhism — truthful living | Concealed coordination for harm conflicts with truth and justice. | B | Do not use secrecy to enable wrongdoing. |
| Confucianism — integrity in public/private conduct | Ethical conduct should not depend solely on visibility. | B | Hidden action remains morally assessable. |
| African Traditional | Secret societies and councils vary enormously in purpose and structure. | C | Confidentiality requires legitimate social purpose. |

### Conclusion
The source preserves privacy while rejecting a common failure mode: converting privacy into impunity.

# E3 Unit 172 — Qur'an 58:11
## Make Room for Others and Recognise Knowledge

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 58:11 instructs people in assemblies to make room when asked and links elevated standing with faith and knowledge.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Shared spaces should not be monopolised when others can reasonably be accommodated.**
- **Courtesy includes physically and socially making room for others.**
- **Knowledge is a legitimate basis for respect distinct from inherited status or wealth.**
- **Communities should create space for participation rather than guarding access as prestige.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 2:1–4 | Seating/status discrimination favouring the rich is criticised. | A/B | Do not turn shared space into status exclusion. |
| Judaism — honour for learning | Scholarship and learning receive strong respect. | A/B | Recognise knowledge rather than wealth alone. |
| Buddhism — sangha etiquette | Seating and precedence are formally regulated without simple wealth hierarchy. | B | Shared assemblies require disciplined courtesy. |
| Hinduism — respect for learning | Teachers and knowledge are highly valued. | B | Knowledge can ground legitimate respect. |
| Sikhism — congregational equality | Sangat and langar embody shared participation across status. | A/B | Make communal space accessible across rank. |
| Confucianism — reverence for learning | Education and cultivated knowledge are central bases of respect. | A | Honour learning and proper participation. |
| African Traditional | Assemblies and elder/knowledge-holder roles vary widely. | B/C | Shared deliberative space is structured by recognised social roles. |

### Conclusion
The source combines ordinary courtesy with an anti-status insight: social space and esteem need not be allocated primarily by wealth.

# E3 Unit 173 — Qur'an 59:9
## Prefer Others Without Resentment and Share Despite Scarcity

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 59:9 praises people who welcome migrants, do not resent what others receive, and prefer others even while themselves in need.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Scarcity does not eliminate the possibility of generosity.**
- **Do not let allocation to another person automatically become a source of resentment.**
- **Host communities can integrate displaced people rather than treating them solely as competitors.**
- **Generosity is most informative when it carries real cost.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Philippians 2:3–4; hospitality traditions | Others' interests should be considered alongside one's own. | A/B | Share even when self-interest pulls the other way. |
| Judaism — stranger care and tzedakah | The stranger and needy receive explicit protection. | A/B | Scarcity does not erase duties to vulnerable newcomers. |
| Buddhism — dana | Generosity is a central virtue and antidote to attachment. | A | Give without possessive resentment. |
| Hinduism — dana and hospitality | Giving and hospitality are major duties. | A/B | Share resources with those in need. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna and langar | Sharing food/resources is foundational. | A | Share even across status boundaries. |
| Confucianism — ren | Humaneness requires concern beyond narrow self-interest. | B | Do not reduce allocation to selfish advantage. |
| African Traditional | Hospitality and reciprocal sharing are prominent in many traditions. | A/B | Support newcomers and community members through shared resources. |

### Conclusion
This unit makes generosity a test under scarcity rather than abundance alone.

# E3 Unit 174 — Qur'an 59:18

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 59:18 tells each person to consider what they have sent ahead for tomorrow.

### Extracted Ethical Core

- **Short-term convenience should not erase long-term responsibility.**

- **Ethical agency includes temporal foresight.**

# E3 Unit 175 — Qur'an 60:8
## Justice and Kindness Toward Peaceful Out-Groups

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 60:8 states that people are not forbidden from acting kindly and justly toward those outside the faith who have not fought them because of religion or expelled them from their homes.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Group difference alone does not justify hostility.**
- **Peaceful outsiders remain legitimate recipients of kindness and justice.**
- **Moral treatment should track conduct rather than mere identity.**
- **Out-group classification should not erase ordinary fairness.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 10:25–37 | Moral neighbourliness crosses group boundaries. | A | Help people beyond one's own group. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:34 | The resident stranger is to be loved as oneself. | A | Extend justice and care beyond kin. |
| Buddhism — metta | Loving-kindness extends without narrow group restriction. | A | Benevolence should not stop at faction boundaries. |
| Hinduism — hospitality and universalist strands | Moral concern can extend beyond one's own group. | B | Difference does not cancel humane treatment. |
| Sikhism — sarbat da bhala | Welfare is sought for all. | A | Seek good beyond one's own community. |
| Confucianism — graded but extensible benevolence | Humaneness can extend outward beyond immediate relations. | B | Treat outsiders humanely. |
| African Traditional | Hospitality toward strangers varies but is prominent in many societies. | B | Peaceful outsiders can enter reciprocal moral relations. |

### Conclusion
This is a strong anti-tribal principle: conduct, not identity alone, determines whether ordinary justice and kindness apply.

# E3 Unit 176 — Qur'an 60:9
## Distinguish Peaceful Difference From Active Persecution

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 60:9 distinguishes the peaceful outsiders of the previous verse from those engaged in persecution, expulsion, or assistance in expulsion.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not collapse peaceful disagreement and active aggression into the same category.**
- **Relationships may legitimately change when another party moves from difference to persecution.**
- **Tolerance need not require collaboration with actors actively violating fundamental rights.**
- **Classification should follow behaviour rather than identity alone.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 12:18 | Peace is pursued insofar as it depends on oneself, implying recognition that reciprocity matters. | B | Seek peace without denying actual aggression. |
| Judaism — distinctions between neighbour, stranger, and hostile aggressor | Treatment can vary with conduct and legal status. | B | Behaviour can alter relational duties. |
| Buddhism | Non-harm remains broad; direct alliance distinctions are less close. | C | Compassion does not require enabling ongoing harm. |
| Hinduism — dharmic distinctions in conflict | Peaceful relations and hostile aggression are treated differently. | B | Respond to conduct proportionately. |
| Sikhism — defence against oppression | Peaceful difference is tolerated; persecution may be resisted. | A/B | Do not confuse pluralism with submission to oppression. |
| Confucianism — harmony with corrective force against disorder | Humane treatment coexists with response to serious disorder. | B | Distinguish ordinary difference from harmful conduct. |
| African Traditional | Intergroup relations vary widely. | C | Behaviour affects the terms of reciprocal relationship. |

### Conclusion
Together Units 175–176 produce a behaviour-sensitive out-group ethic: **peaceful difference invites justice and kindness; active persecution changes the relationship without turning identity itself into guilt**.

# E3 Unit 177 — Qur'an 61:2–3
## Do Not Claim Commitments You Do Not Practise

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 61:2–3 sharply criticises saying what one does not do.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not publicly claim ethical commitments while knowingly acting against them.**
- **Speech and conduct should correspond.**
- **Performative virtue without practice damages trust.**
- **Standards applied to others should also constrain the speaker.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 23:3 | Saying without doing is explicitly criticised. | A | Do not preach standards one refuses to practise. |
| Judaism — ethical integrity traditions | Study and speech should culminate in action. | A/B | Align declared principle with conduct. |
| Buddhism — practice over mere recitation | Knowing teachings without practising them is insufficient. | A/B | Ethical knowledge should become behaviour. |
| Hinduism — disciplined action | Declared duty without corresponding action lacks integrity. | B | Make conduct match professed values. |
| Sikhism — truthful living | Truth must be lived rather than merely spoken. | A | Align speech and life. |
| Confucianism — sincerity | Words should correspond with action and character. | A | Do not use moral language performatively. |
| African Traditional | Reputation often depends on demonstrated conduct rather than verbal claim alone. | B | Credibility requires enacted commitment. |

### Conclusion
This is a direct anti-hypocrisy rule and strongly converges with the project's earliest anchor principles.

# E3 Unit 178 — Qur'an 63:9–10
## Do Not Let Wealth and Family Obligations Crowd Out Wider Responsibility

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 63:9–10 warns against allowing wealth and children to distract from higher obligations and urges giving before opportunities disappear.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Legitimate private goods can become harmful when they consume all attention.**
- **Family and wealth should not erase responsibilities beyond the household.**
- **Opportunities for beneficial action are time-limited.**
- **Resource allocation should not be postponed indefinitely when need and capacity are present.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 12:15–21 | Wealth accumulation can crowd out higher obligations. | A | Do not let possessions become the whole purpose of life. |
| Judaism — tzedakah | Wealth creates timely duties toward others. | A/B | Private property remains subject to charitable responsibility. |
| Buddhism — non-attachment | Attachment to possessions and family identity can obstruct wider compassion. | A/B | Do not let attachment capture ethical agency. |
| Hinduism — staged duties / non-attachment | Material and family duties exist within wider dharma. | B | Legitimate attachments remain morally bounded. |
| Sikhism — household life plus sharing | Family life and honest earning coexist with seva and sharing. | A/B | Private prosperity should not erase communal duty. |
| Confucianism | Strong family emphasis makes direct equivalence partial, though righteousness outranks profit. | B/C | Material gain should remain subordinate to rightness. |
| African Traditional | Family obligations are often central but embedded within wider communal reciprocity. | B | Household interests exist within community obligations. |

### Conclusion
The source does not reject family or wealth; it rejects their conversion into totalising claims on moral attention.

# E3 Unit 179 — Qur'an 64:14–16
## Family Conflict Requires Caution, Forgiveness, and Capacity-Aware Duty

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 64:14–16 warns that even close family members can sometimes become sources of conflict, while counselling caution, forgiveness, generosity, and duty according to capacity.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Close relationships do not eliminate conflicts of interest.**
- **Caution toward harmful conduct can coexist with forgiveness toward the person.**
- **Family loyalty should not require blindness.**
- **Obligation should be calibrated to actual capacity.**
- **Generosity remains important even amid relational strain.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 10:36; forgiveness teachings | Family relationships can contain conflict without cancelling duties of forgiveness. | A/B | Do not confuse kinship with automatic moral correctness. |
| Judaism — family duties within wider law | Kinship creates duties but does not override all moral obligations. | B | Family loyalty remains bounded by justice. |
| Buddhism — non-attachment | Close attachment can distort judgement; compassion remains appropriate. | B | Care without blindness. |
| Hinduism — family dharma within wider dharma | Family duty is substantial but not necessarily absolute. | B | Balance relational duty with broader right conduct. |
| Sikhism — household ethics | Family life is valued while moral discipline remains wider than family preference. | B | Kinship does not erase ethical judgement. |
| Confucianism — family centrality plus remonstrance | Family loyalty includes corrective responsibility. | B | Respect family while responding to wrongdoing. |
| African Traditional | Kinship obligations are often strong but regulated by wider community norms. | B | Family solidarity does not imply unlimited permission. |

### Conclusion
The source again rejects absolute authority by relationship: intimacy increases responsibility but does not eliminate moral discernment.

# E3 Unit 180 — Qur'an 65:1–2
## Procedural Fairness in Divorce and Reliable Witnessing

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 65:1–2 regulates divorce through waiting periods, prohibits wrongful expulsion during that period except in specified circumstances, and requires reliable witnesses at the point of separation or reconciliation.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Relationship dissolution should follow a process rather than impulsive expulsion.**
- **A vulnerable spouse retains protections during transition.**
- **Cooling-off or waiting periods can preserve options and reduce irreversible impulsive action.**
- **Consequential changes in status benefit from reliable witnesses or documentation.**
- **Procedure can protect both fairness and later evidentiary clarity.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Divorce traditions vary; procedural and pastoral safeguards exist but no exact canonical parallel. | C | Relationship dissolution should not be casual or abusive. |
| Judaism — get/divorce law | Divorce is formalised through legal procedure and documentation. | A/B | Major status changes require recognised process. |
| Buddhism | Marriage/divorce is generally less centrally regulated canonically. | C | Non-harm remains relevant during separation. |
| Hinduism | Historical marriage law varies widely; modern procedure is not a direct sacred-text analogue. | C | Separation should remain subject to duties of fairness. |
| Sikhism | Marriage is strongly valued; no close canonical divorce procedure identified. | C | Relational conflict should avoid exploitation. |
| Confucianism | Family dissolution historically governed by social/legal norms rather than a close ethical analogue. | C | Family status changes have wider relational consequences. |
| African Traditional | Marriage dissolution commonly involves kin/community procedures, with major variation. | B/C | Separation can require recognised process and settlement. |

### Conclusion
The transferable principle is procedural: **high-impact relational transitions should not be governed solely by momentary power or impulse**.
