# E3 Unit 091 — Qur'an 8:27
## Do Not Betray Trusts

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 8:27 prohibits knowingly betraying trusts.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not knowingly betray responsibilities entrusted to you.**
- **Access, custody, office, or confidence creates duties rather than opportunities for private exploitation.**
- **Intentional breach of trust is distinct from accidental failure.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 16:10–12 | Faithfulness in entrusted matters is explicitly valued. | A/B | Be reliable with what another entrusts to you. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 6:2–5 | Dealing falsely with entrusted property is condemned and restitution required. | A | Do not misuse entrusted property. |
| Buddhism — Second Precept | Taking what is not given violates ethical discipline. | B | Custody does not create permission to appropriate. |
| Hinduism — asteya and fiduciary duties | Wrongful appropriation violates non-stealing and duty. | B | Do not convert trust into private gain. |
| Sikhism — honest earning and truthful living | Betrayal and dishonest gain conflict with truthful conduct. | B | Honour entrusted responsibilities. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthiness is foundational to social relationships. | A | Be faithful to legitimate trust. |
| African Traditional | Entrusted communal roles and property commonly create obligations, though structures vary. | B | Trust carries responsibility to the community and owner. |

### Conclusion
The verse adds another direct formulation of fiduciary integrity to the corpus.

# E3 Unit 092 — Qur'an 8:46
## Discipline and Unity Under Collective Pressure

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 8:46 warns against internal dispute that causes loss of courage and collective strength, and commends patience.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not allow avoidable internal conflict to destroy collective capacity.**
- **Disagreement has system-level consequences beyond the immediate dispute.**
- **Patience and discipline can preserve cooperation under pressure.**
- **Unity should serve legitimate collective purpose rather than suppress all disagreement.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Mark 3:25; Ephesians 4:3 | A divided community loses cohesion; unity is encouraged. | A/B | Destructive internal division weakens common purpose. |
| Judaism — Psalm 133; communal peace traditions | Unity and peace within community are valued. | B | Preserve cooperation where possible. |
| Buddhism — sangha concord | Schism and divisive conduct are serious concerns. | A | Community integrity requires disciplined dispute management. |
| Hinduism — social harmony and dharma | Cooperative order is valued. | B | Internal conflict should not destroy legitimate common action. |
| Sikhism — sangat | Collective fellowship and disciplined solidarity are important. | B | Preserve unity around just purposes. |
| Confucianism — harmony without sameness | Harmony need not require identical views. | A/B | Manage difference without destroying cooperation. |
| African Traditional | Consensus and communal solidarity are important in many traditions. | B | Resolve internal conflict before it becomes collective weakness. |

### Conclusion
The source treats conflict management as a systems problem: unmanaged disagreement can degrade the capacity of the whole group.

# E3 Unit 093 — Qur'an 8:58
## Transparency When Ending a Treaty

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 8:58 states that where serious treachery is feared from a treaty partner, the agreement should be returned or terminated openly so that both sides are on equal notice rather than answered through secret betrayal.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not answer suspected betrayal with covert betrayal.**
- **If a binding agreement must be terminated, communicate that termination openly.**
- **Both parties should know when the cooperative framework has ended.**
- **Procedural fairness remains relevant even under severe distrust.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:37 | Commitments and changes in commitment should be truthful. | B | Do not hide behind deceptive assurances. |
| Judaism — covenant and treaty traditions | Agreements carry duties; deceptive breach is morally problematic. | B | End obligations through recognised rather than fraudulent means. |
| Buddhism — truthful speech | Deliberate deception violates right speech. | B | Do not use false assurances as a weapon. |
| Hinduism — dharmic statecraft | Treaty practice can distinguish declared hostility from treachery, though traditions are complex. | B/C | Conflict does not automatically justify perfidy. |
| Sikhism — truthful conduct in conflict | Justice in conflict does not erase integrity. | B | Do not make deceit the default response to distrust. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthiness and clear commitments sustain interstate and social relations. | B | Changes in obligation should not depend on secret fraud. |
| African Traditional | Intergroup treaty customs vary substantially. | C | Agreements should be ended through socially recognised notice where applicable. |

### Conclusion
This is a procedural conflict ethic: even when trust collapses, the response should not manufacture false security through hidden breach.

# E3 Unit 094 — Qur'an 8:61
## Accept Genuine Openings for Peace

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 8:61 instructs inclination toward peace when the opposing side inclines toward peace.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not continue conflict merely because conflict has already begun.**
- **A credible opening for peace should receive a peaceful response.**
- **De-escalation is reciprocal: movement toward peace can be answered with movement toward peace.**
- **Past hostility need not make future reconciliation impossible.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:9; Romans 12:18 | Peacemaking and living peaceably where possible are praised. | A | Seek peace when peace is genuinely available. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 20:10 | Peace is offered before siege in a specific legal context. | A/B | Conflict should leave room for peaceful settlement. |
| Buddhism — non-harm | Peaceful resolution is preferable to violence. | A/B | De-escalate rather than perpetuate harm. |
| Hinduism — peace diplomacy within epic/statecraft traditions | Negotiation precedes or can interrupt conflict. | B | Preserve a route from conflict to peace. |
| Sikhism — force as constrained by justice | Armed resistance is not an end in itself. | B | End conflict when just peace becomes possible. |
| Confucianism — harmony and humane government | Restoration of peaceful order is preferable to prolonged coercion. | B | Use peace rather than force where workable. |
| African Traditional | Reconciliation and negotiated cessation of feud occur in many traditions. | B | Accept legitimate opportunities to restore peace. |

### Conclusion
The source makes peace dynamic rather than static: adversaries can change posture, and ethical response should remain capable of changing with them.

# E3 Unit 095 — Qur'an 9:4
## Honour Agreements With Parties Who Honour Them

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 9:4 exempts treaty partners who have not violated their agreements or assisted aggression, commanding fulfilment of the treaty until its term.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not punish reliable parties for the breaches of others.**
- **Honour agreements with those who continue to honour them.**
- **Group-level conflict does not erase distinctions among different counterparties.**
- **Reliability should be reciprocated with reliability.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:37 | Commitments should remain reliable. | B | Keep one's word. |
| Judaism — Numbers 30:2 | Binding undertakings should be fulfilled. | A/B | Honour legitimate commitments. |
| Buddhism — truthful speech | Promises should not be deceptive. | B | Reliability in speech supports trust. |
| Hinduism — satya and vow traditions | Legitimate promises create moral duties. | B | Keep faith with agreements. |
| Sikhism — truthful living | Word and action should correspond. | B | Do not betray reliable partners. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthiness is central to stable relationships. | A | Reciprocity requires reliability. |
| African Traditional | Treaties and oaths commonly bind parties within recognised customary structures. | B | Honour agreements while the other party honours them. |

### Conclusion
This unit combines contract fidelity with anti-generalisation: misconduct by some does not justify indiscriminate breach toward others.

# E3 Unit 096 — Qur'an 9:6
## Protection and Safe Passage for a Requesting Opponent

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 9:6 instructs that if an opponent from the hostile group seeks protection, protection should be granted and the person then conveyed to a place of safety.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **A member of an opposing group can acquire individual protection.**
- **Do not treat enemy classification as eliminating all individual rights.**
- **A granted refuge creates a duty of safe conduct.**
- **Protection includes practical delivery to safety, not merely temporary non-attack.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — sanctuary and enemy-love traditions | Protection can extend beyond one's own group. | B | Enemy status does not erase all duties of humane treatment. |
| Judaism — cities of refuge and stranger-protection traditions | Protected status and safe refuge are recognised in distinct contexts. | B | Law can create safety even amid serious conflict. |
| Buddhism — compassion and non-harm | Hostility does not eliminate the other's moral significance. | B | Protect rather than harm where protection is sought and possible. |
| Hinduism — refuge traditions | Granting protection to one who seeks refuge appears as a strong epic/dharmic ideal. | A/B | A request for refuge can create protective obligation. |
| Sikhism — sanctuary and defence of the vulnerable | Protection may extend to people beyond one's own community. | B | Protect vulnerable persons despite group difference. |
| Confucianism — humane treatment | No close direct safe-passage rule identified. | C | Opposition does not license cruelty. |
| African Traditional | Sanctuary/refuge customs occur in particular societies but cannot be generalised universally. | C | Granted refuge creates recognised protection where such institutions exist. |

### Conclusion
The source strongly separates individual treatment from collective enemy classification and adds a concrete safe-passage obligation.

# E3 Unit 097 — Qur'an 9:60
## Structured Allocation of Social Support

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 9:60 specifies categories for obligatory charitable distribution, including poor and needy people, administrators, persons requiring social support, liberation from bondage, debtors, public/religious causes, and travellers.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Social support can be organised rather than left entirely to spontaneous charity.**
- **Need has multiple forms: poverty, debt, displacement, loss of liberty, and social vulnerability.**
- **Administrative systems may legitimately exist to distribute aid.**
- **Resource allocation should identify intended beneficiary classes rather than depend only on donor preference.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Acts 6:1–6; organised almsgiving | Community structures administer aid to vulnerable members. | A/B | Charity can require institutions and accountable distribution. |
| Judaism — tzedakah funds and communal welfare | Organised communal charity has deep historical roots. | A | Support for need can be institutionally administered. |
| Buddhism — sangha and lay dana institutions | Giving is organised through established religious/social structures. | B | Generosity can be structured and sustained. |
| Hinduism — dana and institutional charity | Giving to multiple categories of need is established, though structures differ. | B | Social need merits organised support. |
| Sikhism — langar, dasvandh, seva | Community institutions systematically pool and distribute resources. | A/B | Share resources through durable communal mechanisms. |
| Confucianism — relief responsibilities of humane government | Authorities have responsibilities during poverty and disaster. | B | Social welfare can be a governance duty. |
| African Traditional | Kinship, mutual-aid, age-grade, and communal systems distribute support in diverse ways. | B | Assistance can be organised through social institutions. |

### Conclusion
The unit shifts charity from individual virtue toward **allocation architecture**, recognising differentiated forms of need and the legitimacy of administration.

# E3 Unit 100 — Qur'an 10:99
## Belief Cannot Be Legitimately Produced by Compulsion

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 10:99 asks whether people can be compelled until they become believers, in a context emphasising that universal belief cannot simply be forced into existence.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Conviction is not equivalent to compelled outward conformity.**
- **Coercion cannot reliably manufacture genuine belief.**
- **Where the desired state is inward conviction, force has intrinsic limits as a tool.**
- **Uniformity of belief should not be assumed achievable through compulsion.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — New Testament invitation/faith traditions | Faith is generally framed as belief and response rather than mechanically producible compliance, though Christian history includes coercive episodes. | B/C | Genuine conviction differs from forced conformity. |
| Judaism | Jewish history and law contain complex communal boundaries; no simple universal non-compulsion analogue is asserted. | C | Coercion and conviction are not identical, but direct equivalence is limited. |
| Buddhism — Kalama Sutta / voluntary practice | Understanding is cultivated through examination and practice rather than forced assent. | B | Genuine understanding cannot be substituted by mere compulsion. |
| Hinduism — plural schools and paths | Diverse approaches coexist, though historical practice is varied. | C | Religious conviction is not reducible to one compelled form. |
| Sikhism — defence of freedom of conscience | Sikh historical memory strongly values protection of another's right to religious conviction. | A/B | Protect freedom of conscience, including for other communities. |
| Confucianism | Primarily ethical/social rather than a close doctrinal-belief analogue. | D/C | Moral cultivation is not identical to forced verbal assent. |
| African Traditional | Highly diverse traditions do not support a single comparable rule. | D | No clear universal equivalent asserted. |

### Discussion
This source repeats the non-compulsion theme encountered in Unit 024 but from a different angle. Unit 024 states non-compulsion directly; Unit 100 highlights a functional limitation: **force can produce outward behaviour but not necessarily genuine inward conviction**.

The distinction matters analytically because it separates the ethics of coercion from the engineering question of whether coercion can even produce the state being sought.

### Conclusion
The source strengthens the Qur'anic non-compulsion cluster while preserving the unevenness of cross-traditional comparison.
