# E3 Unit 261 — Qur'an 18:83–88
## Power Should Distinguish Wrongdoing From Innocence

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 18:83–88 depicts a powerful ruler distinguishing between wrongdoing and constructive conduct rather than treating all subjects identically.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Power should discriminate between conduct, not punish indiscriminately.
- Wrongdoing and constructive behaviour warrant different responses.
- Authority should combine accountability with fair treatment.
- Collective control does not justify collective punishment without distinction.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 13:3–4 | Governing authority is framed as distinguishing good from wrongdoing. | B | Sanction wrongdoing rather than category membership. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 24:16 | Responsibility should not be transferred indiscriminately. | A/B | Punishment should track the actor. |
| Buddhism | Karma individualises intentional conduct, though political punishment is distinct. | B | Distinguish persons by conduct. |
| Hinduism — rajadharma | Rulers are expected to administer differentiated justice. | B | Authority should judge conduct fairly. |
| Sikhism | Just rule opposes indiscriminate oppression. | B | Power should remain bounded by justice. |
| Confucianism | Government should distinguish virtue and misconduct. | B | Governance should respond to actual behaviour. |
| African Traditional | Adjudication varies widely. | C | Authority should avoid indiscriminate sanction. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with individualised-responsibility cluster.**

# E3 Unit 262 — Qur'an 18:94–95
## Public Infrastructure Can Be a Cooperative Project, Not Private Enrichment

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 18:94–95 depicts a community offering payment for protective infrastructure, while the ruler declines personal enrichment and instead requests their labour/cooperation.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Public protection can be produced cooperatively.
- Leaders need not monetise every public service for personal gain.
- Community members can contribute labour or resources to shared infrastructure.
- Authority can convert private payment into participatory public capacity.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — communal service traditions | Shared work for common welfare provides functional convergence. | B | Public goods can be cooperatively produced. |
| Judaism — communal infrastructure obligations | Communities maintain shared institutions and protections. | B | Common goods create shared duties. |
| Buddhism — communal monastic works | Shared labour supports common institutions. | B | Cooperate in maintaining shared resources. |
| Hinduism | Community works and ruler duties provide parallels. | B/C | Public infrastructure should serve common welfare. |
| Sikhism — kar seva | Collective voluntary labour for shared institutions is a strong parallel. | A | Build common goods through shared service. |
| Confucianism | Public works are a legitimate governmental responsibility. | B | Authority should organise common benefit. |
| African Traditional | Communal labour systems are widespread but diverse. | A/B | Shared infrastructure can be built through collective contribution. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a useful common-goods and non-extractive leadership principle.

# E3 Unit 263 — Qur'an 20:44
## Use Gentle Speech Even When Confronting a Tyrant

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 20:44 instructs Moses and Aaron to speak gently even to Pharaoh in the hope that correction remains possible.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- The seriousness of wrongdoing does not automatically justify abusive communication.
- Correction can begin with the least escalatory effective mode.
- Opponent status should not erase strategic civility.
- Persuasion may preserve possibilities that humiliation closes.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 2 Timothy 2:24–25 | Opponents should be corrected with gentleness. | A | Confront wrong without unnecessary harshness. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 15:1 | Gentle speech can reduce anger. | A | Use speech to de-escalate. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Harsh speech is constrained even in disagreement. | A | Correct without hatred. |
| Hinduism — disciplined speech | Speech should be truthful and non-agitating. | A/B | Firmness need not become verbal aggression. |
| Sikhism | Truthful resistance can coexist with restraint from egoic anger. | B | Oppose injustice without becoming abusive. |
| Confucianism — remonstrance | Correction of rulers should be principled and appropriately expressed. | A/B | Speak truth to power with disciplined form. |
| African Traditional | Elder/council remonstrance varies. | C | Corrective speech can preserve dignity. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with discourse ethics.** Its striking feature is that gentleness is prescribed toward a paradigmatic oppressor.

# E3 Unit 264 — Qur'an 20:81
## Enjoy Legitimate Provision Without Excess

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 20:81 permits consumption of good provision while warning against excess.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Legitimate enjoyment is not itself unethical.
- Excess can convert a legitimate good into harmful conduct.
- Ethical regulation can target quantity and loss of restraint rather than prohibit the underlying good.
- Moderation is a design principle between deprivation and overconsumption.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Temperance constrains excess without requiring rejection of all material goods. | A/B | Enjoy without being ruled by excess. |
| Judaism | Permitted goods remain subject to moderation and blessing. | B | Legitimate use differs from excess. |
| Buddhism — Middle Way | Both indulgence and severe deprivation are rejected. | A | Avoid extremes. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 6:16–17 | Moderation in food and activity is explicit. | A | Regulate consumption proportionately. |
| Sikhism | Householder life is compatible with disciplined consumption. | B | Use goods without attachment or excess. |
| Confucianism — moderation | Proper measure is central to cultivated conduct. | A/B | Avoid excess. |
| African Traditional | Consumption norms vary. | C | Sustainable use requires restraint. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Units 087/217/250 as source-frequency evidence for equilibrium.**

# E3 Unit 265 — Qur'an 22:39–40
## Defensive Force Is Linked to Wrongful Expulsion and Protection of Worship

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 22:39–40 permits fighting for people who have been wronged and expelled, and frames resistance partly in terms of preventing destruction of monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Force requires justification rather than mere capability.
- Defence against severe aggression differs from aggression for gain.
- Protection can extend to institutions belonging to different religious communities.
- Plural civic order may require defending spaces of conscience beyond one's own group.
- Coercive power should be tied to identifiable harm and protective purpose.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — just-war traditions | Defensive justification and protection of innocents provide strong functional convergence. | B | Force should be bounded by just cause. |
| Judaism | Defensive war and protection of community are recognised, though frameworks differ. | B | Coercive force requires legitimate protective purpose. |
| Buddhism | Strong non-harm creates tension, though some political traditions recognise protective action. | C | Direct equivalence is limited. |
| Hinduism — dharma-yuddha | Force can be bounded by duty and rules. | A/B | Legitimate force differs from predatory violence. |
| Sikhism — dharam yudh | Defensive force against oppression is strongly developed. | A | Force is justified only under ethical constraints. |
| Confucianism — punitive/just warfare traditions | Some classical thought distinguishes righteous from aggressive war. | B | Coercion should protect order rather than appetite. |
| African Traditional | Warfare ethics vary substantially. | C | No single general analogue asserted. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** The explicit cross-community protection of worship spaces is especially significant.

# E3 Unit 266 — Qur'an 23:8
## Honour Trusts and Commitments

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 23:8 praises those who faithfully observe trusts and covenants.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Reliability is a positive virtue, not merely absence of theft.
- Trusts and promises both create continuing obligations.
- Social coordination depends on predictable fulfilment.
- Ethical character includes what one does when breach would be advantageous.

| Hinduism — satya / asteya | Truthfulness and non-stealing support reliability. | B | Honour legitimate commitments. |
| Sikhism — truthful living | Trustworthiness is integral to ethical life. | B | Be dependable. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthiness is foundational. | A | Keep faith in relationships. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with trust/covenant cluster.**

# E3 Unit 267 — Qur'an 24:4
## Serious Accusations Require Adequate Evidence

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:4 imposes a demanding evidentiary standard for accusations of sexual misconduct and penalises unsupported accusation.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Severe reputational accusations require strong evidence.
- The more damaging the allegation, the more important evidentiary safeguards become.
- Speech can cause grave harm even without physical force.
- Institutions should deter reckless accusation as well as underlying misconduct.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Deuteronomy 19:15 | Serious matters require multiple witnesses. | A | High-stakes accusations require evidence. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 19:15 | Evidentiary thresholds are explicit. | A | Do not convict on unsupported allegation. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | False and harmful speech is prohibited. | B | Do not make damaging claims falsely. |
| Hinduism | Witness/evidence traditions provide strong functional parallels. | B | Serious judgement requires proof. |
| Sikhism | Truthfulness constrains defamatory accusation. | B | Do not destroy reputation through unsupported claims. |
| Confucianism | Trustworthy testimony and careful judgement provide functional convergence. | B | Verify consequential accusations. |
| African Traditional | Evidentiary procedures vary widely. | C | Grave allegations require recognised proof. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** It adds explicit proportionality between reputational harm and evidentiary burden.

# E3 Unit 268 — Qur'an 24:6–9
## Procedures Are Needed When Serious Claims Arise Without Ordinary Evidence

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:6–9 establishes a special procedure for a grave marital accusation where ordinary witnesses are unavailable.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Difficult evidence cases require procedures rather than arbitrary judgement.
- Absence of ordinary proof does not justify inventing certainty.
- Competing claims should be handled through known processes.
- Procedure can constrain both false accusation and automatic dismissal.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | General procedural parallels exist, but no close canonical equivalent. | C | Difficult disputes need fair process. |
| Judaism | Formal evidentiary and oath procedures provide strong functional parallels. | B | Use defined procedure where evidence is contested. |
| Buddhism — Vinaya | Disputes and accusations can be handled through specified procedures. | A/B | Procedure protects communities from arbitrary judgement. |
| Hinduism | Dharma/legal texts contain evidence and oath procedures. | B | Resolve difficult claims through recognised process. |
| Sikhism | No close canonical procedural analogue identified. | C | Fair adjudication should avoid arbitrary certainty. |
| Confucianism | Legal procedure is secondary to moral governance but still functionally relevant. | C | Contested claims require orderly resolution. |
| African Traditional | Oath and adjudication procedures vary greatly. | C | Use recognised process for difficult disputes. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain cautiously.** The transferable principle is procedural handling of underdetermined high-stakes disputes, not the historical procedure itself.

# E3 Unit 269 — Qur'an 24:11–16
## Do Not Amplify Serious Rumours Without Knowledge

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:11–16 addresses a damaging accusation spreading through a community, criticising people for passing it from tongue to tongue without knowledge and for treating a grave claim as trivial.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Do not amplify unverified high-impact claims.
- Repetition is an action with consequences, not neutral transmission.
- Information can become more harmful as it propagates socially.
- Serious allegations should trigger verification and restraint, not entertainment.
- Collective epistemic failure can emerge from many individually small acts of forwarding.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 3 | Speech can cause disproportionate social damage. | A/B | Do not spread destructive claims carelessly. |
| Judaism — lashon hara / rechilut | Harmful gossip and tale-bearing are strongly constrained. | A | Do not transmit damaging information irresponsibly. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | False and divisive speech are prohibited. | A | Verify before repeating harmful claims. |
| Hinduism — satya / disciplined speech | Truthfulness constrains rumour. | A/B | Do not propagate unsupported accusation. |
| Sikhism — rejection of slander | Malicious or false speech is condemned. | A/B | Do not amplify reputational harm. |
| Confucianism — trustworthy speech | Reliable social communication is a moral concern. | B | Do not spread claims beyond knowledge. |
| African Traditional | Gossip regulation varies widely. | C | Rumour can damage communal trust. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a particularly modern-relevant information-propagation ethic.

# E3 Unit 270 — Qur'an 24:19
## Do Not Desire or Promote the Spread of Socially Destructive Indecency

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:19 condemns those who desire the spread of grave indecency or scandal among the community.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Ethical responsibility can extend beyond performing harm to intentionally promoting its diffusion.
- Attention systems can reward destructive content.
- Desire for scandal can itself incentivise social degradation.
- Institutions should distinguish legitimate exposure of wrongdoing from entertainment-driven amplification of indecency.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 1:32 | Approval or encouragement of wrongdoing is morally relevant. | B | Do not promote harmful conduct merely because others perform it. |
| Judaism | Enabling wrongdoing and harmful speech provide functional parallels. | B | Do not facilitate social harm. |
| Buddhism | Encouraging unwholesome conduct conflicts with right intention and speech. | B | Do not cultivate or spread harmful states. |
| Hinduism | Participation in or encouragement of adharma is ethically significant. | B | Do not become a promoter of wrongdoing. |
| Sikhism | Company and speech should reinforce truthful conduct rather than vice. | B | Do not normalise destructive behaviour. |
| Confucianism | Moral culture is shaped by what communities praise and disseminate. | B | Social incentives should reinforce virtue rather than degradation. |
| African Traditional | Norm enforcement and taboo structures vary widely. | C | Communities shape conduct through what they reward and circulate. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain cautiously.** It adds a dissemination-level responsibility distinct from direct commission.

This range contributes several important institutional principles: cooperative public infrastructure (262), civil correction even toward severe opponents (263), plural-space protection within defensive-force ethics (265), evidence proportional to accusation severity (267), procedures for hard evidentiary cases (268), and responsibility for information propagation (269–270).
