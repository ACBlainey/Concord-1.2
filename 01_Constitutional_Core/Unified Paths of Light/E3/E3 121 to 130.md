# E3 Unit 121 — Qur'an 17:36
## Do Not Assert What You Do Not Know

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:36 prohibits following or asserting matters without knowledge and links hearing, sight, and understanding with accountability.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not present unsupported claims as knowledge.**
- **Evidence and epistemic responsibility should precede confident judgement.**
- **Information received through perception or testimony should be used accountably.**
- **Uncertainty should remain visible rather than being concealed by confidence.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 1 Thessalonians 5:21; Proverbs 18:13 | Claims should be tested and premature answers avoided. | A/B | Examine before concluding. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 19:15; evidentiary law | Serious claims require adequate evidence. | A/B | Do not judge consequential matters without support. |
| Buddhism — Kalama Sutta | Beliefs should not rest merely on report, tradition, or unsupported authority. | A | Examine claims rather than accepting them uncritically. |
| Hinduism — traditions of pramana | Knowledge claims are analysed through recognised means of knowledge. | A/B | Distinguish warranted knowledge from unsupported assertion. |
| Sikhism — truthful living | False or careless claims conflict with truthfulness. | B | Speak within what can responsibly be maintained. |
| Confucianism — Analects 2.17 | Knowing what one knows and recognising what one does not know is presented as knowledge. | A | Admit the limits of knowledge. |
| African Traditional | Evidentiary and testimonial practices vary substantially. | C | Serious claims should not rest on baseless assertion. |

### Conclusion
This is a strong epistemic ethic: accountability attaches not only to intentional lying but also to claiming knowledge without adequate grounds.

# E3 Unit 122 — Qur'an 17:37
## Reject Arrogant Self-Importance

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:37 prohibits walking the earth arrogantly and uses human physical limitation to puncture exaggerated self-importance.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not treat status or confidence as evidence of superior human worth.**
- **Remember the limits of individual power.**
- **Humility is a corrective to distorted self-assessment.**
- **Public conduct should not be organised around domination or contempt.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Philippians 2:3; James 4:6 | Humility is preferred to conceit. | A | Do not elevate oneself through arrogance. |
| Judaism — Micah 6:8 | Humble conduct is explicitly praised. | A | Walk humbly rather than boastfully. |
| Buddhism — non-self and restraint of conceit | Conceit is an obstacle to ethical development. | A/B | Do not build identity around superiority. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 13:7–8 | Humility and absence of pretence are virtues. | A | Cultivate humility. |
| Sikhism — rejection of haumai | Ego-centred pride is a central spiritual and ethical problem. | A | Do not let ego dominate conduct. |
| Confucianism — modesty of the junzi | The cultivated person is restrained rather than boastful. | A/B | Competence need not become arrogance. |
| African Traditional | Humility before community, elders, and inherited order is prominent in many traditions. | B | Status should not erase reciprocal respect. |

### Conclusion
Humility displays strong convergence and functions as a constraint on the social misuse of perceived superiority.

# E3 Unit 123 — Qur'an 17:53
## Use the Best Available Speech to Prevent Conflict

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:53 instructs people to say what is best because destructive influence can create conflict between them.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Choose forms of speech that reduce unnecessary conflict.**
- **Communication can either stabilise or destabilise relationships.**
- **Being technically able to say something does not make the most inflammatory formulation ethically preferable.**
- **Prevent escalation at the communication layer where possible.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Ephesians 4:29 | Speech should build up rather than corrupt. | A | Use speech constructively. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 15:1 | A gentle answer can turn away wrath. | A | Speech can de-escalate anger. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Harsh and divisive speech should be avoided. | A | Speak truthfully and non-destructively. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 17:15 | Speech should be truthful, beneficial, and non-agitating. | A | Ethical speech considers effects. |
| Sikhism — truthful and non-slanderous speech | Speech should not be captured by anger or falsehood. | B | Communicate without needless injury. |
| Confucianism — disciplined speech | The cultivated person is careful in speech. | B | Words should preserve propriety and relationship. |
| African Traditional | Proverbs and deliberative speech frequently regulate conflict, though forms vary. | B/C | Use communication to contain rather than inflame disputes. |

### Conclusion
The source treats speech as social infrastructure: conflict prevention can begin with the form in which disagreement is expressed.

# E3 Unit 124 — Qur'an 17:70
## Human Dignity Is Not Restricted to a Narrow Subgroup

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:70 describes the children of Adam collectively as honoured and provided with capacities and resources.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Human dignity precedes many later social classifications.**
- **Do not assume dignity belongs only to one's own faction.**
- **Shared humanity supplies a basis for broad moral consideration.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Genesis 1:27 | Humanity shares a common image-bearing status. | A/B | Human worth is not confined to one social class. |
| Judaism — Genesis 1:27 | Humanity is created in the divine image. | A/B | Shared humanity carries moral significance. |
| Buddhism | No exact creator-grounded dignity analogue; universal capacity for moral concern supports broad consideration. | B/C | Moral concern extends beyond faction. |
| Hinduism — shared spiritual reality traditions | Some schools ground common worth in a deeper shared reality. | B | Surface distinction need not exhaust moral status. |
| Sikhism — human equality | Caste and inherited superiority are strongly challenged. | A | Human dignity is not owned by a privileged class. |
| Confucianism — common human moral potential | Human beings share capacities for cultivation. | B | Status differences do not eliminate human moral standing. |
| African Traditional — Ubuntu and related personhood traditions | Personhood is understood relationally in many traditions. | B | Human worth is recognised through reciprocal belonging. |

### Conclusion
The verse contributes a broad dignity principle while leaving the exact philosophical foundation distinct from other traditions.

# E3 Unit 125 — Qur'an 18:28
## Do Not Prefer Status and Wealth Over Sincere People

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 18:28 instructs perseverance with sincere worshippers and warns against turning away from them because of attraction to worldly display.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not rank people primarily by wealth, prestige, or appearance.**
- **Status incentives can distort judgement about character.**
- **Sincerity and conduct may be more ethically relevant than social display.**
- **Communities should resist exclusion driven by elite preference.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 2:1–9 | Preferential treatment of the rich over the poor is explicitly condemned. | A | Do not make wealth the basis of human preference. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:15 | Neither poverty nor greatness should bias judgement. | A | Status should not distort fairness. |
| Buddhism | Renunciation traditions reject wealth as a measure of spiritual worth. | A/B | Material rank does not establish ethical superiority. |
| Hinduism | Traditions vary significantly on social hierarchy; spiritual texts can relativise material status. | C | Wealth alone does not determine moral worth. |
| Sikhism — rejection of caste and status hierarchy | Shared worship and langar strongly embody status equality. | A | Do not privilege people by inherited or material rank. |
| Confucianism — virtue over wealth | The junzi is defined by cultivated virtue rather than riches. | A/B | Character outranks material display. |
| African Traditional | Status systems vary; wealth commonly carries obligations rather than unlimited moral priority. | C | Social standing should not erase communal duties. |

### Conclusion
This unit identifies prestige bias as a moral distortion: visible advantage is not a reliable proxy for character.

# E3 Unit 126 — Qur'an 18:71–82
## Apparent Harm, Hidden Information, and Caution in Judgement

### Qur'anic Source
The Moses–Khidr narrative presents actions that appear plainly wrong from limited information but are later given explanations based on facts unavailable to the observer.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Recognise that judgement can be constrained by incomplete information.**
- **Do not confuse immediate appearance with complete causal understanding.**
- **Epistemic humility is especially important where hidden context could materially alter interpretation.**
- **This does not establish permission for arbitrary harm; the narrative instead highlights limits on observer knowledge.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 1 Corinthians 13:12 | Human understanding is acknowledged as partial. | B | Recognise limits in present knowledge. |
| Judaism — Pirkei Avot 2:4 | One should not judge another until standing in their place. | A/B | Context can matter to judgement. |
| Buddhism — caution about perception and attachment to views | Immediate perception may be conditioned and incomplete. | B | Hold conclusions with appropriate epistemic humility. |
| Hinduism — limits of ordinary perception | Appearance does not necessarily exhaust reality. | B | Distinguish partial perception from complete knowledge. |
| Sikhism — humility regarding human understanding | Human perspective is limited. | B | Avoid certainty beyond one's knowledge. |
| Confucianism — careful investigation | Judgement should follow adequate understanding. | B | Investigate before concluding. |
| African Traditional | No single equivalent can be responsibly generalised. | C | Context may alter the meaning of observed conduct. |

### Conclusion
The ethical extraction is deliberately narrow: the narrative supports epistemic humility, not a general licence to commit harmful acts on claims of hidden justification.

# E3 Unit 127 — Qur'an 20:43–44
## Speak Gently Even to an Oppressive Ruler

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 20:43–44 sends Moses and Aaron to Pharaoh, described as having transgressed, yet instructs them initially to speak gently so that he may reflect or respond.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Moral opposition does not require gratuitously abusive communication.**
- **Even serious wrongdoing can initially be confronted through measured speech.**
- **The purpose of correction is improved conduct, not merely humiliation of the wrongdoer.**
- **Gentleness and moral firmness are compatible.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 2 Timothy 2:24–25 | Opponents should be corrected with gentleness. | A | Firm correction need not be abusive. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 15:1 | Gentle speech can reduce wrath. | A/B | Manner can improve the chance of correction. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Speech should avoid harshness and serve beneficial ends. | A | Correct without unnecessary verbal harm. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 17:15 | Speech should be non-agitating and beneficial. | A | Truth should be communicated constructively. |
| Sikhism — truthful speech without egoic anger | Opposition to injustice need not become hatred. | B | Resist wrong without surrendering speech ethics. |
| Confucianism — remonstrance | A minister may correct authority through principled, respectful remonstrance. | A/B | Speak truth to power with disciplined form. |
| African Traditional | Elder/council mediation often uses respectful speech, though political traditions vary. | B/C | Correction can preserve dignity while challenging conduct. |

### Conclusion
The verse reinforces a recurring distinction between **strength of moral position** and **harshness of delivery**.

# E3 Unit 128 — Qur'an 20:81
## Consume Good Provision Without Excess

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 20:81 permits enjoyment of good provision while prohibiting excess or transgression in its use.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Legitimate resources may be enjoyed.**
- **Permission does not imply unlimited consumption.**
- **Abundance should remain governed by restraint.**
- **Excess can transform a legitimate good into harmful conduct.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — temperance traditions | Legitimate appetite should remain self-controlled. | B | Enjoy without becoming governed by excess. |
| Judaism | Permitted goods remain subject to disciplined use. | B | Permission is not identical to excess. |
| Buddhism — Middle Way | Indulgence and severe deprivation are both rejected. | A/B | Avoid extremes of consumption. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 6:16–17 | Excess and deficiency in consumption are rejected. | A | Moderate bodily consumption. |
| Sikhism — restraint from greed | Appetite should not dominate the person. | B | Use material goods without greed. |
| Confucianism — mean and frugality | Proper measure avoids excess. | A/B | Consume within fitting bounds. |
| African Traditional | Consumption norms vary with context and communal obligations. | C | Legitimate use remains socially bounded. |

### Conclusion
This repeats the moderation cluster and contributes source-frequency evidence rather than a new distinct ethic.

# E3 Unit 129 — Qur'an 22:39–40
## Defensive Force Against Persecution and Destruction of Worship

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 22:39–40 permits fighting for people who have been wronged and expelled, and links resistance to preventing destruction of monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Force requires justification rather than being intrinsically self-authorising.**
- **Defence against serious persecution can be distinguished from aggression.**
- **Protection can extend to institutions belonging to different religious communities.**
- **Plural spaces of worship can be treated as goods worth protecting from violent destruction.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — just-war traditions | Defensive force can be distinguished from aggression under specified conditions. | B | Force requires morally relevant justification. |
| Judaism — defensive-war traditions | Defence of community against attack is recognised. | B | Resistance to aggression can be legitimate. |
| Buddhism | Strong non-harm commitments create a less direct equivalence; historical traditions vary. | C | Protection from violence is valued, but force remains ethically difficult. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita / dharma-yuddha traditions | Force can be morally constrained by duty and rules. | B | Conflict should be justified and bounded. |
| Sikhism — sant-sipahi tradition | Defence against oppression can become a duty when peaceful means fail. | A/B | Protect others against serious oppression. |
| Confucianism — punitive/defensive war traditions | Force may be justified against grave disorder under humane authority. | B/C | Political force should answer serious wrong, not appetite. |
| African Traditional | Defensive warfare and protection of sacred/community institutions vary by society. | C | Collective defence can be distinguished from predatory violence. |

### Conclusion
The notable feature is not merely permission for defence but the plural list of protected worship spaces, which broadens the protected interest beyond a single community.

# E3 Unit 130 — Qur'an 22:60
## Proportionate Redress After Injury

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 22:60 recognises response to injury in equivalent measure while condemning subsequent renewed aggression against the responder.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Redress should remain proportionate to the wrong suffered.**
- **A victim's response does not give the original aggressor a fresh licence to escalate.**
- **Conflict rules should distinguish original aggression, bounded response, and renewed aggression.**
- **Reciprocity needs limits to prevent endless escalation.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:38–44 | The proportional retaliation formula is further restrained toward non-retaliation. | A/B | Do not escalate injury into vengeance. |
| Judaism — Exodus 21:23–25 | Lex talionis provides a proportionality principle. | A | Redress should not exceed the injury. |
| Buddhism — non-retaliation | Retaliation is strongly restrained. | B | Break cycles of reciprocal harm. |
| Hinduism — bounded punishment traditions | Punishment should correspond to wrong and role. | B | Response requires proportionality. |
| Sikhism — constrained defensive force | Resistance should not become vindictive excess. | B | Defend without escalating beyond necessity. |
| Confucianism — measured punishment | Sanction should serve order rather than private rage. | B | Keep response governed by principle. |
| African Traditional | Compensation systems often provide alternatives to escalating feud. | B/C | Convert injury into bounded settlement. |

### Conclusion
The source reinforces proportionality as an anti-escalation architecture.
