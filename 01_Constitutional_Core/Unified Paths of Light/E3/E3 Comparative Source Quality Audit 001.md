# Sacred Codes and Civilizational Longevity — E3
## Comparative Source-Quality Audit — Initial Findings

**Scope sampled:** E3 detailed comparison files 001–200  
**Purpose:** Identify systematic source-quality risks before final prevalence claims.

---

## 1. Initial Inventory Finding

The detailed E3 files consistently compare each Qur'anic unit against the seven non-anchor traditions. The comparative architecture is therefore present.

However, documentary precision is uneven.

The audit finds three broad classes:

### Stronger rows

Many rows identify recognisable primary or foundational sources, for example:

- named biblical passages;
- Dhammapada passages;
- Bhagavad Gita or other named Hindu sources;
- Guru Granth Sahib material;
- Analects material;
- named Jewish legal or scriptural sources.

These are suitable candidates for SQ-A/B after passage-level checking.

### Provenance-light rows

Some rows describe a tradition generally without an identifiable passage or source. These should remain SQ-C until upgraded.

### Deliberately cautious rows

Several E3 entries already refuse to overclaim, especially for African Traditional Religions. Examples explicitly state that practices vary by society or that no single pan-African equivalent can responsibly be asserted.

These are methodologically preferable to unsupported A/B matches and should be preserved.

## 2. Tradition-Specific Risk Profile

### Christianity / biblical tradition

**Risk:** comparatively low.

The Christian comparison can usually be tied to identifiable biblical material. The main task is passage accuracy and functional fit rather than provenance recovery.

### Judaism

**Risk:** low to moderate.

Many claims can be grounded in Hebrew Bible or named rabbinic material. Some E3 rows, however, make broad statements such as what “Judaism generally” does without a precise source. Those rows require upgrade or conservative grading.

### Buddhism

**Risk:** comparatively low.

The project frequently uses identifiable canonical material such as the Dhammapada and named suttas. The principal audit issue is whether the passage supports the precise operational claim rather than only its broad ethical parent.

### Hinduism

**Risk:** moderate.

Named sources are often available, but “Hinduism” contains multiple textual and philosophical traditions. Broad claims should identify the specific text or school rather than treating all Hindu traditions as interchangeable.

### Sikhism

**Risk:** moderate.

The Guru Granth Sahib supplies a strong canonical basis, but some rows use summarised Sikh teaching without precise passage identification. These should be upgraded where possible.

### Chinese / Confucian tradition

**Risk:** moderate.

The Analects and related classical texts can support many claims, but generic statements about “Confucianism” should not automatically be treated as direct textual equivalents.

### African Traditional Religions

**Risk:** high.

This is the clearest systematic provenance weakness. The E3 files themselves frequently acknowledge the problem with formulations such as:

- practices “vary by society”;
- “no single pan-African textual rule should be asserted”;
- “no single cross-traditional equivalent can be responsibly asserted”;
- sanctuary or refuge customs occur only in particular societies.

These cautions are correct. They also mean that generic African Traditional rows cannot support high-confidence universality claims without more specific provenance.

## 3. Examples Requiring Conservative Treatment

The sampled corpus includes cases where:

- conflict and reconciliation practices are acknowledged as society-specific;
- kinship and marriage systems are explicitly described as diverse;
- reciprocal-credit practices resist generalisation;
- refuge customs are said to exist only in particular societies;
- sexual norms are explicitly described as too diverse for a uniform detailed equivalent.

Such rows should not be silently counted as equivalent to a canonical textual citation.

## 4. Important Positive Finding

The source problem does **not** invalidate the Qur'an-derived catalogue.

The catalogue's identity comes from the Qur'anic anchor and the normalisation process.

What source weakness can change is:

- the match grade assigned to another tradition;
- whether that tradition counts as securely present;
- the 8/8, 7/8, 5–6/8 prevalence tier;
- and the strength of claims made in the final comparative synthesis.

This distinction protects the derivation from retrospective contamination.

## 5. Audit Priority

The next detailed source audit should prioritise:

1. any final Q principle currently classified **Universal**;
2. then **Near-universal**;
3. then **Common**;
4. with African Traditional evidence checked first where it is the weakest link;
5. followed by generic Confucian, Sikh, Hindu and Jewish claims.

A claimed 8/8 principle whose eighth tradition rests only on an unspecified generalisation should be reported as **provisionally universal**, not securely universal, until repaired.
