# E3 Unit 241 — Qur'an 12:92
## Forgiveness After Serious Betrayal

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 12:92 depicts Joseph responding to the brothers who seriously wronged him without retaliatory blame once reconciliation becomes possible.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- A victim may choose reconciliation rather than vengeance.
- Serious prior wrongdoing does not make restoration impossible.
- Forgiveness can interrupt long retaliatory chains.
- Reconciliation need not deny that the original harm occurred.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 18:21–22 | Repeated forgiveness is strongly encouraged. | A | Do not make retaliation the only response to wrong. |
| Judaism — Genesis 50:15–21 | Joseph's forgiveness of his brothers is itself a direct parallel. | A | Reconciliation can follow grave family betrayal. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 5 | Hatred is not ended by hatred. | A | Break retaliatory cycles. |
| Hinduism — kshama | Forgiveness is a recognised virtue. | A/B | Restrain vengeance. |
| Sikhism | Forgiveness and control of anger are strongly valued. | B | Do not let injury permanently govern conduct. |
| Confucianism | Reconciliation and restoration of relationship provide partial convergence. | B | Repair can be preferable to continuing feud. |
| African Traditional | Restorative reconciliation is prominent in many traditions but locally structured. | B | Serious wrong can be answered through restoration. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with forgiveness cluster.** The narrative provides a particularly strong model of forgiveness after severe interpersonal betrayal.

# E3 Unit 242 — Qur'an 13:20–22
## Covenant, Relationship, Patience, Giving, and Repelling Evil With Good

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 13:20–22 praises people who fulfil commitments, maintain relationships that should be maintained, persevere, give from their resources, and answer evil with good.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Keep legitimate commitments.
- Preserve relationships carrying valid obligations.
- Maintain ethical conduct under difficulty.
- Use resources for others.
- Constructive response can interrupt cycles of harm.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 12:17–21 | Evil should not be repaid with evil; generosity and peace are praised. | A | Overcome harmful reciprocity with constructive conduct. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 25:21–22 | Aid can be extended even to an enemy. | A | Do good despite hostility. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 5 | Hatred is not ended by hatred. | A | Break cycles of retaliation. |
| Hinduism | Forgiveness, duty, and generosity provide strong functional parallels. | B | Maintain duty rather than mirror wrongdoing. |
| Sikhism | Service, truthfulness, and control of anger converge strongly. | B | Answer hostility without becoming hostile in kind. |
| Confucianism | Trustworthiness and relational duty are strong; response to wrong is more context-dependent. | B | Maintain principled relationships and reliability. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocity and reconciliation vary across traditions. | B/C | Preserve obligations and repair damaged relations. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain as a compound source; code constituent principles separately during synthesis.**

# E3 Unit 243 — Qur'an 13:25
## Covenant-Breaking and Destructive Severance as Social Corruption

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 13:25 condemns breaking commitments after they are established, severing relationships that should remain connected, and spreading corruption.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Repeated breach erodes social trust.
- Destruction of legitimate relationships can have systemic consequences.
- Reliability is infrastructure for cooperation.
- Corruption can arise through fragmentation as well as direct material harm.

### Comparative Analysis

| Buddhism — sangha concord | Divisive conduct can seriously damage community. | B | Do not fracture legitimate cooperative bonds. |
| Hinduism — relational dharma | Social roles generate continuing duties. | B | Honour legitimate relational obligations. |
| Sikhism — truthful living / sangat | Trust and community require reliability. | B | Do not betray sustaining relationships. |
| Confucianism — relational ethics | Durable role relationships structure social order. | A/B | Relationship obligations matter. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: duplicate in principle of Unit 202, but independent source-frequency evidence. Merge in final catalogue.**

# E3 Unit 244 — Qur'an 16:90
## Justice, Excellence, Generosity, and Rejection of Oppression

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 16:90 commands justice, excellence in conduct, and giving to relatives while prohibiting indecency, wrongdoing, and oppressive transgression.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Justice is a minimum, not necessarily the ceiling of good conduct.
- Social ethics includes positive generosity as well as prohibitions.
- Oppressive exercise of power is ethically constrained.
- Stable moral systems combine fairness, beneficence, and restraint.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Micah 6:8 / Luke 6:31–36 | Justice, mercy, and generous conduct strongly converge. | A | Combine fairness with active goodness. |
| Judaism — justice and hesed | Justice and loving-kindness operate together. | A | Law and generosity are complementary. |
| Buddhism — compassion / non-harm | Ethical life combines restraint with positive compassion. | A/B | Avoid harm and actively cultivate good. |
| Hinduism — dharma / dana / ahimsa | Justice-like duty, giving, and non-harm converge. | A/B | Ethical order includes duty and beneficence. |
| Sikhism — seva / justice | Service and resistance to oppression are strong themes. | A | Combine justice with active service. |
| Confucianism — yi / ren | Righteousness and humaneness are complementary virtues. | A | Fairness should be joined to benevolence. |
| African Traditional | Justice, reciprocity, and generosity are widespread but diverse. | B | Social order requires both fairness and positive obligation. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a high-density summary source likely important to final synthesis.

# E3 Unit 245 — Qur'an 16:91–92
## Do Not Use Solemn Commitments as Instruments of Deception

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 16:91–92 commands fulfilment of covenants and warns against breaking confirmed oaths in ways that make commitments instruments of deception.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Formal commitments should increase reliability rather than facilitate manipulation.
- Do not exploit another party's reliance on your promise.
- Strength or advantage does not justify opportunistic breach.
- Institutions require credible commitment mechanisms.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:37 | Speech and commitments should be reliable. | A | Let commitments mean what they appear to mean. |
| Judaism — Numbers 30:2 | Vows are binding. | A | Honour solemn commitments. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Deliberate deception is prohibited. | A | Do not weaponise promises. |
| Hinduism — satya | Truthfulness constrains deceptive commitments. | A/B | Keep one's word. |
| Sikhism — truthful living | Commitments should reflect truth. | A/B | Do not promise strategically and betray opportunistically. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthiness is foundational to social order. | A | Commitments must be dependable. |
| African Traditional | Oaths and covenants vary but often carry strong social force. | B | Solemn promises should constrain opportunism. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with covenant cluster.** The anti-deception function is especially explicit.

# E3 Unit 246 — Qur'an 16:116
## Do Not Invent Moral Prohibitions and Present Them as Authoritative Truth

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 16:116 warns against falsely labelling things lawful or unlawful and attributing invented rules to divine authority.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Do not manufacture normative rules without adequate authority or evidence.
- False moral certainty can impose real burdens on others.
- Rule-making requires epistemic accountability.
- Institutional legitimacy is damaged when preferences are disguised as higher law.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Mark 7:7–8 | Human rules can be wrongly elevated as divine commands. | A | Do not disguise human preference as sacred law. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 4:2 | Adding to or subtracting from commandments is constrained. | A | Do not invent authoritative rules. |
| Buddhism | False claims of attainment or teaching are constrained, though not identical. | B | Do not fabricate spiritual authority. |
| Hinduism | Scriptural/epistemic traditions distinguish valid authority from unsupported assertion. | B | Normative claims require legitimate grounds. |
| Sikhism | Empty ritual and false religious authority are criticised. | B | Do not impose invented religiosity as truth. |
| Confucianism | Rectification of names and epistemic modesty provide functional parallels. | B | Authority claims should correspond to reality. |
| African Traditional | Authority structures vary too widely for a single equivalent. | C | Rule claims should arise from legitimate sources. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This strengthens the emerging constitutional principle against unsupported normative authority.

# E3 Unit 248 — Qur'an 16:126
## Retaliation Must Not Exceed the Harm Received

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 16:126 permits response to injury only to an equivalent extent while presenting patience as preferable.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Response to wrongdoing should be proportionate.
- Victimhood does not grant unlimited retaliatory licence.
- Restraint can be ethically preferable even where proportionate response is permitted.
- Conflict rules should cap escalation.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:38–44 | Retaliation is further constrained in favour of non-retaliation. | A/B | Do not escalate injury. |
| Judaism — lex talionis | Retaliation/compensation is bounded proportionately. | A | Response must not exceed the wrong. |
| Buddhism — non-harm | Retaliation is generally discouraged. | B | Restrain harmful response. |
| Hinduism | Proportionate punishment exists within dharmic/legal traditions. | B | Sanction should fit wrongdoing. |
| Sikhism | Force is bounded by necessity and justice in martial ethics. | B | Do not exceed what protection requires. |
| Confucianism | Punishment should be ordered and proportionate rather than vindictive. | B | Correct without excess. |
| African Traditional | Restorative and compensatory systems vary widely. | B/C | Response should restore order rather than amplify feud. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with Units 007 and 010.** This is another explicit proportionality source.

# E3 Unit 249 — Qur'an 17:23–24
## Care for Parents Without Contempt in Their Vulnerability

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:23–24 commands good treatment of parents, particularly in old age, prohibiting even contemptuous speech and requiring humility and care.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Age-related dependency should not become grounds for humiliation.
- Care obligations include manner as well as material support.
- Power reversal between parent and adult child should be handled without contempt.
- Vulnerability deserves dignity-preserving care.

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with family-care cluster.** The explicit prohibition on contempt during elder dependency is distinctive.

# E3 Unit 250 — Qur'an 17:26–29
## Support Need Without Waste, Miserliness, or Reckless Giving

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:26–29 requires giving due support to relatives, poor people, and travellers while condemning waste and also warning against both extreme withholding and financially self-destructive giving.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Generosity should be sustainable.
- Waste is not the same as generosity.
- Miserliness and reckless depletion are opposite failures.
- Social support should balance beneficiary need with continuing capacity to help.
- Resource ethics requires calibrated stewardship rather than a single maxim of “more giving.”

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 2 Corinthians 8:12–14 | Giving is related to means and equitable burden. | A/B | Give generously without denying real capacity constraints. |
| Judaism — tzedakah limits | Charity is obligatory but traditionally bounded to avoid self-impoverishment. | A | Sustainable generosity balances duty and capacity. |
| Buddhism — Middle Way / dana | Generosity and moderation coexist. | A/B | Give without destructive excess. |
| Hinduism — dana / moderation | Proper giving considers context and discipline. | B | Generosity should be well-directed. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing is integrated into productive household life. | A/B | Sustain both earning and sharing. |
| Confucianism — mean/moderation | Virtue avoids destructive extremes. | B | Resource conduct should be proportionate. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocal support often operates within capacity constraints. | B | Sharing should sustain rather than destroy the support network. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a strong equilibrium principle: neither hoarding nor uncontrolled expenditure constitutes sound stewardship.
