# E3 Unit 311 — Qur'an 68:8–13
## Do Not Follow Slanderous, Obstructive, or Habitually Aggressive Influence

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 68:8–13 presents a cluster of destructive traits including habitual oath-taking, contempt, slander, malicious gossip, obstruction of good, aggression, and harshness.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Character assessment can legitimately consider repeated behavioural patterns.
- Slander and gossip can be instruments of influence and capture.
- Obstructing good is ethically relevant even when no direct injury is visible.
- Institutions should be cautious about entrusting influence to actors with persistent patterns of manipulation and aggression.
- Wealth or status should not neutralise behavioural evidence.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 7:16–20 / James 3 | Character is known through conduct; destructive speech is condemned. | A/B | Evaluate influence by repeated behaviour. |
| Judaism — harmful speech traditions | Slander and tale-bearing are strongly prohibited. | A | Do not empower corrosive speech. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Slander, harsh speech, and divisive speech are prohibited. | A | Communication character matters. |
| Hinduism | Truthfulness, non-harm, and disciplined speech converge strongly. | B | Persistent harmful conduct undermines trust. |
| Sikhism | Slander, ego, and oppression are strongly criticised. | B | Do not mistake status for integrity. |
| Confucianism | Character and trustworthy conduct are central to fitness for influence. | A/B | Office and influence require cultivated conduct. |
| African Traditional | Leadership-character criteria vary. | C | Repeated antisocial behaviour can disqualify trust. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This contributes to trustworthy-agent selection and anti-capture analysis.

# E3 Unit 312 — Qur'an 68:17–33
## Wealth-Holders Should Not Conspire to Exclude the Poor

### Qur'anic Source
The parable of the garden in Qur'an 68:17–33 depicts owners planning to harvest secretly so that needy people cannot receive a share, followed by recognition of their wrongdoing.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Coordination among owners can be used to evade social obligations.
- Exclusion can be intentional even where no explicit theft occurs.
- Property rights do not necessarily exhaust ethical responsibility.
- Collective planning can amplify selfishness just as it can amplify cooperation.
- Recognition of wrongdoing should permit correction rather than permanent moral classification.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 12:16–21 | Hoarded abundance without wider responsibility is criticised. | A/B | Wealth should not become closed self-regard. |
| Judaism — gleaning laws | Landholders must leave access for poor and vulnerable persons. | A | Property use carries distributive duties. |
| Buddhism — dana | Hoarding and attachment are ethically problematic. | B | Wealth should support generosity. |
| Hinduism — dana | Possession carries duties of giving. | B | Do not organise wealth solely around exclusion. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing is a central duty. | A | Wealth should circulate beyond its holder. |
| Confucianism | Humane governance and benevolence constrain elite indifference. | B | Material advantage carries social responsibility. |
| African Traditional | Communal resource obligations vary widely. | B/C | Owners may have obligations to vulnerable community members. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is particularly useful as an anti-collusive-exclusion source.

# E3 Unit 313 — Qur'an 70:24–25
## Vulnerable People Can Have a Claim on Wealth, Not Merely Receive Optional Charity

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 70:24–25 praises those whose wealth contains a recognised share for people who ask and those deprived of means.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Assistance can be framed as a claim rather than discretionary benevolence.
- Need can generate obligations on resource holders.
- Social provision should include people whose deprivation is not effectively voiced.
- Systems should not rely solely on the vulnerable having to ask successfully.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Strong duty to aid poor persons, though often framed as charity rather than a property claim. | B | Need generates serious duties of support. |
| Judaism — tzedakah / gleaning | Assistance is strongly duty-based and includes enforceable-like social obligations. | A | Vulnerable people possess recognised claims on surplus/resources. |
| Buddhism — dana | Giving is strongly valued but usually framed through giver virtue rather than recipient right. | B | Resources should reach people in need. |
| Hinduism — dana | Giving is obligatory/virtuous in many traditions but differently grounded. | B | Wealth carries distributive obligation. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing one's earnings is integral to ethical life. | A/B | Need should be met through structured sharing. |
| Confucianism | Humane governance bears responsibility for people's material welfare. | B | Social order includes provision for need. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocal entitlement within kin/community networks is common but diverse. | B | Vulnerability can create legitimate claims on community resources. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** Distinguish this rights-like formulation from optional generosity.

# E3 Unit 314 — Qur'an 70:32–33
## Trustworthiness Includes Both Commitments and Testimony

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 70:32–33 praises those who preserve trusts and promises and stand faithfully by their testimony.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Trustworthiness spans property, commitments, and information.
- Reliable testimony is a public good.
- A person who is dependable privately but deceptive as a witness is not fully trustworthy.
- Institutional trust depends on cross-domain integrity.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition | Promise integrity and false-witness prohibitions directly converge. | A | Be reliable in commitment and testimony. |
| Judaism | Covenant, deposit, and witness law directly converge. | A | Trustworthiness spans promises and evidence. |
| Buddhism | Truthful speech and non-stealing provide strong functional parallels. | A/B | Do not betray reliance or testimony. |
| Hinduism — satya / asteya | Truth and non-appropriation converge strongly. | A/B | Preserve entrusted goods and truth. |
| Sikhism | Truthful living strongly converges. | B | Reliability should span conduct and speech. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthiness is foundational across relationships. | A | Be dependable in word and role. |
| African Traditional | Oath/testimony systems vary. | B/C | Social trust requires reliable commitments and evidence. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with trust and truthful-testimony clusters while preserving source recurrence.**

# E3 Unit 315 — Qur'an 74:38
## Responsibility Should Track What the Individual Has Earned

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 74:38 states that every person is held in pledge for what they have earned.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Responsibility should track an actor's own conduct.
- Collective identity should not automatically transfer individual guilt.
- Accountability should be linked to actions and choices.
- Systems should resist inherited or category-based culpability where individual conduct can be distinguished.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 14:12 | Each person gives account for themselves. | A/B | Accountability is individualised. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 24:16 | Persons should not be punished for another's wrongdoing. | A | Guilt should not be transferred indiscriminately. |
| Buddhism — karma | Intentional action produces consequences for the actor. | A | Responsibility follows conduct. |
| Hinduism — karma | Consequence is strongly linked to action. | A | Conduct generates responsibility. |
| Sikhism | Individuals are accountable for their deeds. | A/B | Moral responsibility tracks action. |
| Confucianism | Role relationships complicate strict individualism, but personal cultivation and conduct matter. | B | Judge persons substantially by their own behaviour. |
| African Traditional | Collective responsibility can be stronger in some traditions. | C | Individual conduct remains relevant but not always exclusive. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with individual-responsibility cluster.**

# E3 Unit 316 — Qur'an 74:42–44
## Failure to Feed the Poor Is a Serious Moral Failure

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 74:42–44 includes failure to feed poor people among the conduct associated with moral failure.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Harm can arise through omission as well as commission.
- Severe deprivation creates positive duties.
- Ethical systems should not define innocence solely as “I did not directly cause the problem.”
- Food security for vulnerable people is a core social concern.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 25:42 | Failure to feed the hungry is explicitly morally significant. | A | Omission of aid can be wrongful. |
| Judaism — tzedakah / gleaning | Provision for poor persons is an established duty. | A | Meet basic needs. |
| Buddhism — dana | Feeding and generosity are strongly valued. | A/B | Respond to material need. |
| Hinduism — anna-dana | Giving food is highly valued. | A | Feed those in need. |
| Sikhism — langar | Universal food provision is institutionally central. | A | Make food accessible across status. |
| Confucianism | Humane government must address subsistence. | B | Material welfare is a public moral concern. |
| African Traditional | Food-sharing obligations are widespread but diverse. | B | Community should not ignore hunger. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** Strong evidence for positive-duty non-harm.

# E3 Unit 317 — Qur'an 76:8–9
## Aid the Needy, Orphan, and Captive Without Demanding Return

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 76:8–9 praises feeding needy people, orphans, and captives despite valuing the food oneself, without seeking repayment or gratitude.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Aid should not always be transactional.
- Vulnerability can outweigh group or captive status.
- Assistance can extend to people under one's power.
- Beneficence is ethically stronger when it does not create hidden debt.
- Support should not be conditioned on praise.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 6:35 | Give without expecting return, including toward enemies. | A | Aid without creating reciprocal debt. |
| Judaism | Charity should preserve dignity and can avoid dependence on gratitude. | A/B | Help without humiliating the recipient. |
| Buddhism — dana | Giving without attachment to reward is strongly valued. | A | Give without demanding return. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 17:20 | Proper giving is given without expectation of return. | A | Non-transactional generosity. |
| Sikhism — seva/langar | Service is ideally selfless and non-discriminatory. | A | Serve without seeking reward. |
| Confucianism | Benevolence supports aid, though reciprocity remains important. | B | Help should not be reduced to private gain. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocity is often prominent, though hospitality and aid can exceed direct exchange. | B/C | Vulnerable persons may merit aid without immediate return. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** The explicit inclusion of a captive strengthens the principle of aid across power and group boundaries.

# E3 Unit 318 — Qur'an 80:1–10
## Do Not Neglect a Vulnerable Seeker in Order to Court the Powerful

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 80:1–10 rebukes turning attention away from a blind person who earnestly seeks guidance while concentrating on socially important people who appear less receptive.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Disability should not reduce a person's claim to respectful attention.
- Prestige can distort allocation of institutional attention.
- Expected influence or status should not automatically outrank sincere need.
- Inclusion requires examining whose participation is being treated as interruptive.
- Leaders should be corrigible when status bias shapes their priorities.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 2:1–4 | Preferential attention to the wealthy is explicitly condemned. | A | Do not privilege status over need. |
| Judaism | Justice traditions prohibit partiality and protect vulnerable persons. | A/B | Status should not distort fair attention. |
| Buddhism | Spiritual accessibility is not restricted by caste/status; compassion includes vulnerable persons. | A/B | Need and sincerity outrank prestige. |
| Hinduism | Traditions contain both hierarchical and egalitarian strands. | C | Some strands reject status as determinant of spiritual access. |
| Sikhism | Caste/status exclusion is strongly rejected. | A | Equal access should cross social rank. |
| Confucianism | Merit can outrank inherited rank, though social hierarchy remains significant. | B | Attend to worthy participation rather than prestige alone. |
| African Traditional | Disability/status norms vary greatly. | C | No single cross-traditional equivalent asserted. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is one of the clearest Qur'anic anti-status-capture and disability-inclusion passages.

# E3 Unit 319 — Qur'an 83:1–3
## Do Not Demand Full Value While Giving Less in Return

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 83:1–3 condemns those who demand full measure when receiving but give less when measuring for others.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Reciprocity should not be structurally asymmetric in one's own favour.
- Ethical failure can lie in using one standard for oneself and another for others.
- Market fairness requires symmetry between claims and obligations.
- Systems should detect advantages hidden in measurement or rule asymmetry.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 7:12 / honest-measure tradition | Reciprocity and fair measure strongly converge. | A | Apply fair standards both ways. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 25:13–16 | Unequal weights are explicitly prohibited. | A | Do not manipulate standards for self-benefit. |
| Buddhism | Non-stealing and right livelihood oppose deceptive exchange. | B | Do not take more than one fairly gives. |
| Hinduism | Commercial honesty and fair measure strongly converge. | A/B | Exchange symmetrically. |
| Sikhism | Honest earning rejects exploitative exchange. | B | Do not profit through asymmetric standards. |
| Confucianism — reciprocity | Do not impose on others what one would reject oneself. | A/B | Standards should be reciprocal. |
| African Traditional | Market norms vary. | B/C | Fair exchange requires consistent standards. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with commercial-integrity cluster.** The symmetry formulation is especially clear.

# E3 Unit 320 — Qur'an 89:17–20
## Social Failure Includes Neglect of Orphans, Poor People, and Greedy Accumulation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 89:17–20 criticises failure to honour orphans, failure to encourage feeding poor people, consuming inheritance greedily, and excessive love of wealth.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Vulnerable people require more than passive non-harm.
- Communities can be responsible for failing to encourage mutual provision.
- Inheritance and wealth systems can become vehicles for predatory accumulation.
- Wealth attachment can distort treatment of low-power persons.
- Social ethics should examine both individual acts and cultural incentives.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 1:27 / concern for poor | Orphans and poor persons receive explicit protection. | A | Protect vulnerable people and resist greed. |
| Judaism | Orphan protection, tzedakah, and inheritance law strongly converge. | A | Wealth rules should protect vulnerable parties. |
| Buddhism | Generosity and non-attachment oppose greed and neglect. | A/B | Do not let attachment defeat compassion. |
| Hinduism | Dana and duties toward vulnerable persons provide strong parallels. | B | Wealth carries obligations. |
| Sikhism | Sharing and rejection of greed strongly converge. | A | Wealth should not displace service. |
| Confucianism | Humane government and family/property duties provide partial convergence. | B | Material appetite should remain subordinate to humane obligation. |
| African Traditional | Orphan/kin support and inheritance systems vary widely. | B/C | Wealth allocation should not abandon vulnerable dependants. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a compact social-justice cluster connecting vulnerability, inheritance, provision, and greed.

# Interim Findings: Audit Units 311–320

This range strengthens anti-capture and leadership character (311), anti-collusive exclusion (312), rights-like social provision (313), individualised accountability (315), positive duties toward hunger (316), non-transactional aid across power boundaries (317), disability/status inclusion (318), reciprocal market standards (319), and protection against wealth-driven neglect (320).
