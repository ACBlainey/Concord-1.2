# E3 Unit 291 — Qur'an 42:40–43
## Justice Permits Proportionate Redress but Praises Reconciliation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 42:40–43 limits the response to harm to an equivalent response, praises pardon and reconciliation, protects those who defend themselves after injustice, condemns aggressors, and praises patient forgiveness.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Victims retain legitimate claims to redress.
- Redress should not exceed the original harm.
- Forgiveness is praiseworthy but should not be confused with denying victim rights.
- Aggression is distinct from justified self-protection.
- Reconciliation is strongest when chosen within a framework that recognises justice.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:38–44 | Retaliation is strongly restrained and forgiveness elevated. | A/B | Prefer non-escalation while recognising wrongdoing. |
| Judaism — lex talionis | Proportionality is explicit. | A | Redress must be bounded. |
| Buddhism — non-harm | Retaliation is discouraged; forgiveness strongly converges. | B | Do not perpetuate harm. |
| Hinduism | Legal proportionality and forgiveness traditions both provide parallels. | B | Justice and restraint can coexist. |
| Sikhism | Legitimate defence is recognised while vengeance is constrained. | A/B | Protect without becoming aggressor. |
| Confucianism | Justice and reconciliation both matter, though framed differently. | B | Correct wrongdoing without excess. |
| African Traditional | Restorative systems vary but often combine accountability and reconciliation. | B | Repair should restrain feud. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain as a high-value synthesis source for proportionality, victim rights, and reconciliation.**

# E3 Unit 292 — Qur'an 42:42
## Accountability Should Focus on Oppression and Wrongful Aggression

### Qur'anic Source
Within the preceding sequence, Qur'an 42:42 specifically identifies blame with those who wrong people and transgress without right.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Accountability should track wrongful conduct rather than mere unpopularity.
- Power used to oppress others warrants particular scrutiny.
- Systems should distinguish aggression from legitimate self-protection.
- Sanction criteria should be behaviourally legible.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Oppression and unjust harm are repeatedly condemned. | A/B | Direct accountability toward actual wrongdoing. |
| Judaism | Justice traditions strongly condemn oppression. | A | Sanction wrongdoing rather than identity. |
| Buddhism | Harmful intentional action is ethically central. | B | Focus blame on harmful conduct. |
| Hinduism | Adharma and abuse of power are subject to sanction. | B | Accountability follows wrongful action. |
| Sikhism | Oppression is a paradigmatic object of resistance. | A | Hold oppressive power accountable. |
| Confucianism | Tyrannical or abusive rule violates humane governance. | B | Authority is judged by conduct. |
| African Traditional | Accountability systems vary. | C | Sanction should respond to actual transgression. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge within Unit 291 rather than count separately in final principle catalogue.**

# E3 Unit 293 — Qur'an 49:6
## Verify Consequential Information Before Acting

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:6 instructs verification when consequential information comes from an unreliable source, lest people be harmed in ignorance and regret follow.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Information reliability should affect action thresholds.
- Higher potential harm demands stronger verification.
- Source credibility is relevant but does not replace evidence.
- Acting quickly on unverified reports can create irreversible damage.
- Epistemic procedure is part of non-harm.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Proverbs 18:17 | The first account can seem right until examined. | A/B | Investigate before consequential judgement. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 13:14 | Serious allegations can require inquiry and careful investigation. | A | Verify before sanction. |
| Buddhism — Kalama Sutta | Do not accept claims solely through report or authority. | A/B | Examine information before acting on it. |
| Hinduism — pramana | Valid action depends on valid knowledge. | A/B | Assess grounds of knowledge. |
| Sikhism | Truthfulness and discernment support verification. | B | Do not harm on the basis of rumour. |
| Confucianism | Careful inquiry and rectification support accurate judgement. | B | Investigate before deciding. |
| African Traditional | Evidentiary practices vary. | C | Consequential claims should be checked through recognised process. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a central evidence-before-action principle.

# E3 Unit 294 — Qur'an 49:9–10
## Intervene in Internal Conflict With Justice, Including Against the Aggressor

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:9–10 instructs reconciliation between fighting groups; if one persists in aggression, the community should resist that aggression until it stops, then reconcile with justice and fairness.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Neutrality between aggressor and victim can perpetuate injustice.
- Peace processes should identify ongoing aggression rather than merely split the difference.
- Intervention should aim at ending aggression and restoring fair relations.
- Once aggression stops, the goal returns to just reconciliation rather than punishment without end.
- Community members bear responsibility for conflict containment.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:9 | Peacemaking is valued, though the anti-aggressor procedure is less explicit. | B | Actively restore peace. |
| Judaism | Justice and peace are jointly valued; courts distinguish wrongdoing. | B | Peace should not erase justice. |
| Buddhism | Reconciliation is strong, though coercive intervention creates tension with non-harm. | B/C | Stop harm and restore concord. |
| Hinduism | Dharmic conflict traditions can distinguish aggressor from defender. | B | Peace may require restraining aggression. |
| Sikhism | Defence against oppression strongly supports this structure. | A/B | Resist aggression, then restore justice. |
| Confucianism | Order and righteous intervention provide partial parallels. | B | Peace requires correction of disorder. |
| African Traditional | Mediation and peace-making institutions vary. | B/C | Reconciliation should address the source of conflict. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a sophisticated conflict-resolution sequence: mediate, identify aggression, restrain it, then reconcile justly.

# E3 Unit 295 — Qur'an 49:11
## Do Not Ridicule, Defame, or Reduce People to Derogatory Labels

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:11 prohibits groups from ridiculing others, prohibits defamation, and rejects insulting labels.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Social status should not be built through humiliation.
- Derogatory labelling can freeze people into contemptuous identities.
- Group-based ridicule is especially capable of escalating factional hostility.
- Dignity includes how people are publicly named and represented.
- One should not assume superiority over the person being mocked.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:22 / James 3 | Contemptuous speech is morally serious. | A/B | Do not degrade people through speech. |
| Judaism — ona'at devarim / harmful speech traditions | Verbal humiliation is strongly constrained. | A | Do not injure dignity through words. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Harsh and divisive speech is prohibited. | A | Do not use speech to humiliate. |
| Hinduism — disciplined speech | Speech should be non-agitating and beneficial. | A/B | Avoid degrading language. |
| Sikhism | Slander and ego-driven contempt are condemned. | A/B | Do not elevate self by degrading others. |
| Confucianism — propriety | Respectful naming and conduct are socially important. | B | Preserve dignity in interaction. |
| African Traditional | Naming/taboo and ridicule norms vary. | C | Public humiliation can damage communal standing. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** It supplies a clear dignity-in-language principle.

# E3 Unit 296 — Qur'an 49:12
## Avoid Unsupported Suspicion, Spying, and Backbiting

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:12 warns against excessive suspicion, spying, and speaking destructively about absent people.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Suspicion is not evidence.
- Privacy should not be invaded merely to satisfy suspicion or curiosity.
- Information access itself can become a form of harm.
- People deserve protection from reputational attack when absent.
- Ethical information systems need limits on collection as well as dissemination.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 7:1–5 / James 4:11 | Rash judgement and harmful speech are constrained. | B | Do not convert suspicion into condemnation. |
| Judaism — lashon hara / privacy norms | Gossip and intrusive harm are strongly regulated. | A | Protect privacy and reputation. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Backbiting and divisive speech are prohibited. | A | Do not spread harmful speech about others. |
| Hinduism | Truthfulness and non-harm constrain gossip and intrusive conduct. | B | Respect informational boundaries. |
| Sikhism | Slander is strongly criticised. | A/B | Do not damage absent persons through speech. |
| Confucianism | Trust and propriety constrain corrosive suspicion. | B | Maintain relational trust absent evidence. |
| African Traditional | Gossip and privacy norms vary. | C | Information practices can injure social standing. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a major privacy and epistemic-restraint source.

# E3 Unit 297 — Qur'an 49:13
## Human Diversity Is Not a Hierarchy of Intrinsic Worth

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:13 addresses humanity as originating from a shared human pair and describes peoples and tribes as means of mutual recognition, while rejecting lineage as the ultimate measure of honour.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Group difference does not establish intrinsic superiority.
- Diversity can serve recognition rather than domination.
- Inherited identity should not substitute for moral character.
- Shared humanity precedes many social classifications.
- Status systems should be cautious about converting ancestry into worth.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Galatians 3:28 | Major inherited distinctions are relativised within spiritual standing. | A/B | Identity category does not determine ultimate worth. |
| Judaism — Genesis common ancestry | Humanity shares common origin, though covenant identity remains distinct. | B | Shared humanity underlies group difference. |
| Buddhism — anti-caste teachings | Birth does not determine moral/spiritual worth. | A | Conduct outranks inherited rank. |
| Hinduism | Traditions contain both hierarchical and universalist strands. | C | Some strands locate deeper worth beyond birth category. |
| Sikhism — rejection of caste | Inherited hierarchy is explicitly challenged. | A | Birth does not establish superiority. |
| Confucianism | Moral cultivation can outrank inherited position, though hierarchy remains. | B | Worth should track virtue rather than lineage alone. |
| African Traditional | Kin/lineage structures vary greatly. | C | Diversity need not imply a universal hierarchy of worth. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a major equality-and-diversity source.

# E3 Unit 298 — Qur'an 53:32
## Avoid Self-Certification of Moral Purity

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 53:32 warns people not to declare themselves pure or morally superior.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Self-assessment is vulnerable to bias.
- Moral identity should not become immunity from scrutiny.
- Claims of purity can conceal error and inhibit correction.
- Institutions should not treat self-certification as sufficient evidence of integrity.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 18:9–14 | Self-righteousness is explicitly criticised. | A | Do not treat oneself as morally beyond correction. |
| Judaism | Humility and repentance traditions strongly converge. | B | Moral standing should not become boastful self-certification. |
| Buddhism | Ego attachment and conceit are major obstacles. | A/B | Do not cling to superior self-image. |
| Hinduism | Humility and freedom from pride are praised. | A/B | Avoid moral conceit. |
| Sikhism — haumai | Ego and self-exaltation are central problems. | A | Do not make virtue a basis for ego. |
| Confucianism | The cultivated person remains self-critical and modest. | A/B | Virtue requires continuing self-examination. |
| African Traditional | Humility norms vary. | B/C | Public self-exaltation can undermine communal trust. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This has strong relevance to recursive oversight: no actor should be exempt from review merely by asserting its own virtue.

# E3 Unit 299 — Qur'an 57:7
## Possession Can Be Understood as Stewardship Rather Than Absolute Ownership

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 57:7 describes people as successors or trustees over resources and links that condition to giving.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Control of resources can be temporary and fiduciary rather than metaphysically absolute.
- Present holders inherit systems and assets they did not create alone.
- Stewardship creates obligations toward other people and future continuity.
- Ownership rights can coexist with responsibilities of use.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — stewardship traditions | Resources are entrusted rather than absolutely self-originated. | A | Possession carries responsibility. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 25:23 | Land is not framed as absolutely owned by human holders. | A | Ownership is bounded by stewardship. |
| Buddhism | Impermanence and non-attachment weaken absolute possessiveness. | B | Possession should not become absolute identification. |
| Hinduism | Trusteeship-like and non-attachment traditions provide functional convergence. | B | Use resources without absolute possessiveness. |
| Sikhism | Wealth is to be honestly earned and shared. | B | Possession creates duties. |
| Confucianism | Property is legitimate but morally subordinate to righteousness and social obligation. | B | Use resources within ethical relations. |
| African Traditional | Communal and custodial land/resource traditions provide strong but diverse parallels. | B | Present holders may be custodians within longer continuity. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a significant resource-stewardship principle.

# E3 Unit 300 — Qur'an 57:23
## Do Not Let Loss Destroy Agency or Gain Produce Arrogance

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 57:23 counsels against being consumed by what has been lost or becoming boastful because of what has been gained.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Emotional equilibrium protects judgement under changing fortune.
- Loss should not wholly disable agency.
- Success should not become grounds for superiority.
- Stable character should not depend entirely on external possession.
- Resilience and humility are complementary responses to volatility.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Philippians 4:11–12 | Contentment across changing material conditions is explicit. | A | Maintain stability through gain and loss. |
| Judaism | Humility and trust traditions provide functional convergence. | B | Fortune should not produce arrogance or despair. |
| Buddhism — equanimity | Equanimity toward gain and loss is central. | A | Do not be governed by changing fortune. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 2:38 | Equanimity in gain/loss and success/failure is explicit. | A | Maintain disciplined balance under outcomes. |
| Sikhism | Equanimity and rejection of ego strongly converge. | A/B | Neither gain nor loss should dominate character. |
| Confucianism | The cultivated person is not governed solely by profit or adversity. | B | Preserve virtue across changing fortune. |
| African Traditional | Resilience norms vary widely. | C | Endure changing circumstances without destructive extremes. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This adds a psychologically important resilience-and-humility principle.
