# E3 Unit 301 — Qur'an 58:8–10
## Secret Deliberation Should Not Be Used for Harm, Aggression, or Intimidation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 58:8–10 condemns secret consultation directed toward wrongdoing, hostility, and disobedience, while permitting deliberation oriented toward good conduct. It also recognises that secretive communication can distress excluded people.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Confidentiality does not remove ethical accountability.
- Private coordination should not become infrastructure for aggression.
- Secrecy can itself be used to intimidate or destabilise others.
- The ethical status of private deliberation depends partly on purpose and effect.
- Legitimate confidentiality and conspiratorial harm should be distinguished.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Conspiracy toward wrongdoing is condemned; constructive private counsel is permissible. | B | Privacy does not legitimise harmful coordination. |
| Judaism | Plotting harm and false conspiracy are strongly condemned. | B | Secret coordination remains morally accountable. |
| Buddhism | Intention and harmful collective action remain ethically relevant regardless of privacy. | B | Secrecy does not excuse harmful intention. |
| Hinduism | Ethical statecraft distinguishes legitimate counsel from destructive plotting. | B | Confidentiality should serve legitimate ends. |
| Sikhism | Truthful conduct conflicts with covert oppression or deceit. | B | Do not hide harmful coordination behind secrecy. |
| Confucianism | Counsel is legitimate, but factional intrigue is socially corrosive. | B | Private deliberation should serve proper order. |
| African Traditional | Council secrecy and conspiracy norms vary widely. | C | Confidential process should not become covert harm. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a useful governance distinction between legitimate confidentiality and harmful secrecy.

# E3 Unit 302 — Qur'an 58:11
## Make Room for Others in Shared Assemblies

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 58:11 instructs participants to make room for one another in assemblies and to comply when asked to rise or rearrange themselves.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Shared civic or communal space should not be monopolised by incumbents.
- Small acts of accommodation can determine whether others can participate.
- Access is partly a matter of social behaviour, not only formal entitlement.
- Established participants should sometimes yield space so others can enter.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 2:1–4 | Assembly access should not be distorted by status preference. | B | Shared spaces should not privilege incumbents unfairly. |
| Judaism | Synagogue/community etiquette provides functional parallels. | B | Make communal participation practically possible. |
| Buddhism — sangha procedures | Seating and assembly conduct are regulated to preserve order and participation. | B | Shared assemblies require reciprocal accommodation. |
| Hinduism | Assembly etiquette provides partial parallels. | C | Participation requires appropriate spatial conduct. |
| Sikhism — sangat/langar | Shared communal participation strongly resists status exclusion. | A/B | Make common space genuinely common. |
| Confucianism — li | Seating and assembly conduct are highly regulated by propriety. | B | Order shared space considerately. |
| African Traditional | Council and assembly practices vary substantially. | C | Participation can require accommodation by existing members. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** A small behavioural rule carries a broader inclusion principle.

# E3 Unit 303 — Qur'an 59:7
## Prevent Shared Wealth From Circulating Only Among the Rich

### Qur'anic Source
Within a specific distribution context, Qur'an 59:7 states a rationale that wealth should not merely circulate among the rich.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Concentration can be an ethical property of a system, not only of individual behaviour.
- Distribution rules can be evaluated by whether resources become structurally locked among already advantaged groups.
- Economic design should consider circulation as well as ownership.
- Public or common resources should not automatically reinforce existing concentration.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Acts 4:34–35 / concern for poor | Strong concern with material concentration and unmet need. | B | Resources should circulate toward need. |
| Judaism — Jubilee / gleaning / tzedakah | Institutions constrain permanent concentration and support vulnerable people. | A/B | Economic rules should limit exclusionary concentration. |
| Buddhism — dana / social teachings | Generosity counters attachment and deprivation, though systemic design is less canonical. | B | Wealth should not become closed circulation among elites. |
| Hinduism — dana | Redistribution through giving provides partial convergence. | B | Wealth carries wider obligations. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna / langar | Sharing directly counters exclusive accumulation. | A | Resources should circulate across status boundaries. |
| Confucianism | Humane government includes material welfare and opposition to severe deprivation. | B | Economic order should sustain the population broadly. |
| African Traditional | Communal/redistributive institutions vary but often constrain isolated accumulation. | B | Wealth exists within reciprocal social obligations. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a major system-level distributive principle and should remain distinct from individual generosity.

# E3 Unit 304 — Qur'an 59:9
## Prefer Others Even Under Conditions of Personal Need

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 59:9 praises people who welcome others and give them preference even while experiencing need themselves, while identifying protection from selfish greed as a virtue.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Generosity has greater moral significance when it carries real opportunity cost.
- Community solidarity can require sharing under scarcity, not only from surplus.
- Self-interest need not always dominate allocation.
- Greed can become a structural obstacle to mutual support.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Mark 12:41–44 | Giving from scarcity is treated as especially significant. | A | Sacrificial generosity can outweigh surplus giving. |
| Judaism | Tzedakah remains meaningful even for persons of limited means. | B | Need does not eliminate all reciprocal responsibility. |
| Buddhism — dana | Generosity is a central virtue and can involve meaningful sacrifice. | A/B | Give without attachment to possession. |
| Hinduism — dana | Giving without selfish expectation is strongly valued. | A/B | Share even where giving has real cost. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing is integral to ordinary life rather than reserved for the wealthy. | A | Share what one has. |
| Confucianism — ren | Benevolence can require prioritising another's legitimate need. | B | Self-interest should not be absolute. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocal sharing under scarcity is important in many traditions but highly diverse. | B | Community can require costly mutual support. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with generosity cluster while preserving scarcity-specific emphasis.**

# E3 Unit 305 — Qur'an 60:8–9
## Distinguish Peaceful Outsiders From Active Aggressors

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 60:8–9 permits and commends kindness and justice toward people who do not fight or expel the community, while distinguishing those actively engaged in persecution and expulsion.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Out-group identity alone does not justify hostility.
- Behaviour, rather than category membership, should determine defensive response.
- Justice and kindness can extend across deep belief differences.
- Peaceful outsiders and active aggressors should not be collapsed into one class.
- Security policy should preserve discrimination between threat and non-threat.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 10:25–37 | Moral concern crosses group boundaries. | A/B | Treat outsiders according to conduct and need, not identity alone. |
| Judaism | Resident-alien and neighbour ethics provide meaningful cross-group protections, though boundaries differ. | B | Outsider status does not erase justice obligations. |
| Buddhism | Compassion is not limited to one's own group. | A | Moral concern crosses identity boundaries. |
| Hinduism | Universalist and hospitality traditions provide functional convergence. | B | Difference need not imply hostility. |
| Sikhism | Service and human equality extend across religious identity. | A | Treat peaceful others justly. |
| Confucianism | Humane concern can extend outward in graded form. | B | Difference does not automatically make an enemy. |
| African Traditional | Intergroup ethics vary greatly. | C | Peaceful and hostile outsiders should not automatically be treated identically. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a major anti-collective-hostility and threat-discrimination principle.

# E3 Unit 306 — Qur'an 61:2–3

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 61:2–3 rebukes people for saying what they do not do.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Public moral claims should correspond to conduct.
- Declared commitments create expectations and accountability.
- Performative virtue without implementation corrodes trust.
- Institutions should compare stated policy with observable behaviour.

| Buddhism | Ethical attainment is demonstrated through conduct rather than claim. | B | Practice should correspond to profession. |
| Hinduism | Right action and truthfulness constrain empty declaration. | B | Words and deeds should align. |
| Sikhism | Truthful living is superior to merely speaking truth. | A | Integrity requires enacted truth. |
| Confucianism | The cultivated person is cautious in speech and exceeds words in action. | A | Let conduct substantiate claims. |
| African Traditional | Integrity norms vary. | B/C | Credibility depends on correspondence between promise and action. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a strong implementation-integrity principle.

# E3 Unit 307 — Qur'an 64:14
## Injury Within Close Relationships Can Be Met With Caution and Forgiveness

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 64:14 warns that even close family relationships can become sources of opposition or harm, while also commending pardon, overlooking, and forgiveness.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Intimacy does not guarantee alignment or safety.
- Ethical systems should not romanticise family relationships as incapable of conflict.
- Caution can coexist with forgiveness.
- Forgiveness need not require denial of relational risk.
- Boundaries and compassion can operate together.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Family conflict is recognised alongside strong forgiveness teachings. | A/B | Forgiveness need not deny conflict. |
| Judaism | Family duties coexist with accountability and reconciliation. | B | Kinship does not erase wrongdoing. |
| Buddhism | Attachment can generate suffering; compassion can coexist with discernment. | B | Care does not require blindness to harm. |
| Hinduism | Family dharma coexists with wider moral duties. | B | Relationship does not override ethical judgement. |
| Sikhism | Family life is valued without making kinship morally absolute. | B | Preserve truth and compassion within conflict. |
| Confucianism | Family bonds are foundational but include remonstrance and correction. | B | Loyalty can include principled caution. |
| African Traditional | Kinship obligations are strong but conflict-management norms vary. | C | Close relationship does not eliminate accountability. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain cautiously.** Its value lies in separating forgiveness from naïve trust.

# E3 Unit 308 — Qur'an 64:16
## Obligation Should Be Calibrated to Actual Capacity

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 64:16 instructs people to fulfil obligations to the extent of their ability while encouraging generosity and resistance to selfish greed.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Ethical demands should account for real capacity.
- Inability and unwillingness are not the same.
- Systems become unjust when they demand impossible performance.
- Capacity-sensitive obligation can coexist with aspiration toward generosity.
- Fair governance should distinguish refusal from genuine constraint.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 2 Corinthians 8:12 | Giving is acceptable according to what a person has, not what they lack. | A | Obligation should track capacity. |
| Judaism | Legal duties frequently incorporate capacity and circumstance. | A/B | Do not demand the impossible. |
| Buddhism | Discipline includes contextual allowances for capacity and condition. | B | Ethical expectations can be context-sensitive. |
| Hinduism | Dharma varies with role, stage, and capacity. | B | Duty is context-sensitive. |
| Sikhism | Service is integrated with ordinary capacities and responsibilities. | B | Contribute according to ability. |
| Confucianism | Role obligations are calibrated by circumstance and position. | B | Responsibility should reflect actual role/capacity. |
| African Traditional | Obligation systems vary but commonly reflect role and ability. | B/C | Contribution should be proportionate to capacity. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is an important proportional-obligation principle.

# E3 Unit 309 — Qur'an 65:1–2
## Relationship Dissolution Requires Procedure, Time, and Witness

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 65:1–2 regulates divorce through waiting periods, restrictions on expulsion, and witnessing at the point of final separation or reconciliation.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Ending a close legal relationship should not be purely impulsive.
- Procedure can protect vulnerable parties during transition.
- Time can create space for reconsideration and orderly settlement.
- Housing/security should not be weaponised during dissolution.
- Witness or documentation can reduce later dispute.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Divorce traditions differ, but orderly and accountable treatment provides partial convergence. | C | Relationship dissolution should not become arbitrary harm. |
| Judaism | Divorce is formally documented and procedurally regulated. | A/B | Legal separation requires process. |
| Buddhism | Marriage/divorce is less centrally canonical; non-harm remains relevant. | C | Separation should minimise harm. |
| Hinduism | Historical marriage law differs considerably; procedural parallels are tradition-specific. | C | Relationship status changes should be orderly. |
| Sikhism | No close canonical divorce procedure identified. | C | Fair treatment remains relevant. |
| Confucianism | Family dissolution historically followed structured social/legal norms. | C | Major relational transitions require order. |
| African Traditional | Divorce procedures vary greatly by society. | C | Separation commonly involves recognised process. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain at functional procedural level.** Do not universalise historically specific divorce rules.

# E3 Unit 310 — Qur'an 65:6–7
## Do Not Use Economic Dependence to Harass During Separation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 65:6–7 requires housing according to means, prohibits harassment intended to make life difficult, regulates support around pregnancy and nursing, calls for consultation, and calibrates expenditure to resources.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Economic leverage should not be weaponised against a separating partner.
- Vulnerability during pregnancy and caregiving creates support obligations.
- Continuing shared responsibilities can require consultation after relationship breakdown.
- Financial duties should reflect actual means.
- Separation does not terminate all duties of non-harm and care.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Care obligations and non-harm provide functional convergence, though procedures differ. | B/C | Do not exploit dependency during separation. |
| Judaism | Maintenance and divorce obligations provide strong functional parallels. | A/B | Separation carries continuing material duties. |
| Buddhism | Non-harm provides a general parallel but no close canonical family procedure. | C | Do not weaponise economic vulnerability. |
| Hinduism | Maintenance duties exist in legal traditions, though historically variable. | B/C | Separation can leave continuing support obligations. |
| Sikhism | Family responsibility provides functional convergence. | C | Do not abandon vulnerable dependants. |
| Confucianism | Family responsibilities continue beyond interpersonal preference. | B/C | Material power should remain ethically constrained. |
| African Traditional | Kin and separation obligations vary greatly. | C | Economic dependence should not license harassment. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a significant anti-coercion principle at the intersection of family law and economic power.
