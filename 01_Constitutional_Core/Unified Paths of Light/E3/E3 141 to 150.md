# E3 Unit 141 — Qur'an 25:67
## Balance Spending Between Extravagance and Miserliness

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 25:67 praises people whose spending is neither extravagant nor miserly but follows a balanced course.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Avoid wasteful expenditure.**
- **Avoid withholding resources through excessive miserliness.**
- **Responsible spending occupies a sustainable middle range.**
- **Resource ethics should consider both present use and continuing obligations.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 2 Corinthians 8:12–14 | Giving is related to capacity and fair balance. | A/B | Use resources generously but sustainably. |
| Judaism — charity and anti-waste traditions | Giving is required while needless waste is restrained. | A/B | Avoid both hoarding and waste. |
| Buddhism — Middle Way | Extremes of indulgence and deprivation are rejected. | A | Use material goods with balance. |
| Hinduism — moderation | Ethical life disciplines both greed and excess. | B | Keep consumption and giving proportionate. |
| Sikhism — sharing and honest household life | Sharing is integrated with responsible livelihood. | B | Share without surrendering stewardship. |
| Confucianism — frugality and the mean | Extravagance and deficiency are both disfavoured. | A/B | Spend according to proper measure. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocal obligations often constrain both hoarding and reckless depletion. | B | Resources should sustain continuing relationships. |

### Conclusion
This is another explicit source for sustainable resource balance.

# E3 Unit 142 — Qur'an 25:68–71
## Grave Wrongdoing Does Not Eliminate the Possibility of Reform

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 25:68–71 condemns killing and serious sexual wrongdoing but then explicitly recognises repentance, restored belief, and corrective action.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Serious wrongdoing requires moral accountability.**
- **A person need not be permanently identical to the worst act they committed.**
- **Genuine reform requires change rather than verbal regret alone.**
- **Ethical systems can combine condemnation of conduct with a path back from it.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 15; repentance traditions | Serious wrong can be followed by repentance and restoration. | A | Preserve a path from wrongdoing to reform. |
| Judaism — teshuvah | Return, confession, repair, and changed conduct are central. | A | Wrongdoers can change through genuine repentance. |
| Buddhism — transformation through practice | Unwholesome conduct can be abandoned and replaced. | A/B | Past wrongdoing need not determine all future conduct. |
| Hinduism — reform and purification traditions | Conduct and character can be transformed. | B | Moral development remains possible after failure. |
| Sikhism — transformation through truthful living | Ego and vice can be overcome through changed orientation and action. | B | Reform should remain possible. |
| Confucianism — self-cultivation | People can correct faults through learning and practice. | A/B | Error should lead to correction rather than fixed identity. |
| African Traditional | Restorative processes frequently aim at reintegration after acknowledgement/repair, though forms vary. | B | Accountability can coexist with reintegration. |

### Conclusion
The source reinforces an important distinction: **accountability is not the same as permanent moral exclusion**.

# E3 Unit 143 — Qur'an 25:72
## Do Not Participate in Falsehood; Pass Through Abuse With Dignity

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 25:72 praises those who do not bear or participate in falsehood and who pass by degrading or frivolous conduct with dignity.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not lend credibility to falsehood through participation.**
- **Refusal to join harmful conduct can itself be an ethical act.**
- **Disengage from degrading environments without needing to imitate them.**
- **Personal dignity can be preserved through non-participation.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Ephesians 5:11 | Do not participate in fruitless works of darkness. | A | Refuse complicity in wrongdoing. |
| Judaism — Exodus 23:1 | Do not join in false reports or malicious witness. | A | Do not become a participant in falsehood. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech / Right Action | Avoid false speech and unwholesome conduct. | A | Withdraw from harmful participation. |
| Hinduism — satya | Truthfulness constrains participation in deception. | B | Do not support falsehood. |
| Sikhism — truthful living | Truth should govern both speech and association. | B | Do not legitimise falsehood by joining it. |
| Confucianism — integrity | The cultivated person does not follow a crowd into improper conduct. | B | Maintain principle under social pressure. |
| African Traditional | Community conformity can be strong, but integrity norms also constrain false accusation and dishonour. | C | Participation carries moral responsibility. |

### Conclusion
The unit develops the corpus's complicity principle: ethical responsibility includes what one chooses to join, endorse, or normalise.

# E3 Unit 144 — Qur'an 26:181–183
## Fair Measurement, Non-Deprivation, and Non-Corruption

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 26:181–183 commands full measure, accurate weighing, non-deprivation of people's goods, and rejection of corrupt conduct.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Use honest measures.**
- **Do not diminish what others are due.**
- **Economic cheating contributes to wider social corruption.**
- **Shared standards should protect weaker parties from hidden extraction.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Hinduism — commercial dharma | False measures are condemned. | A/B | Exchange requires honest standards. |

This repeated principle is retained for source-frequency analysis and should later collapse into a single distinct ethical category.

# E3 Unit 145 — Qur'an 28:26
## Select Agents for Strength and Trustworthiness

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 28:26 recommends hiring Moses because the best person to employ is described as strong/capable and trustworthy.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Selection for responsibility should consider capability and integrity together.**
- **Trust without competence can fail operationally.**
- **Competence without trust can create exploitation risk.**
- **Role requirements should matter more than prestige alone.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Acts 6:3 | Administrators are chosen for character and wisdom. | A | Select capable, trustworthy agents. |
| Judaism — Exodus 18:21 | Officials should be capable, trustworthy, and resistant to dishonest gain. | A | Office requires competence and integrity. |
| Buddhism — leadership virtues | Moral character and practical wisdom are both valued. | B | Responsibility requires ethics and judgement. |
| Hinduism — administrative/statecraft traditions | Officials are assessed for competence and loyalty/integrity. | A/B | Match office with capable reliable people. |
| Sikhism — service-oriented leadership | Authority should be exercised with ability and moral discipline. | B | Capability should serve trustworthy ends. |
| Confucianism — merit and virtue | Officials should be both able and virtuous. | A | Appoint by merit and character. |
| African Traditional | Experience, reputation, and trust often influence selection of office-holders. | B | Responsibility should go to recognised capable and reliable persons. |

### Conclusion
This independently repeats Unit 104's competence-plus-trust principle, now as an explicit selection criterion rather than retrospective appointment.

# E3 Unit 146 — Qur'an 28:77
## Use Material Good Without Corrupting the World

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 28:77 counsels use of worldly provision without forgetting legitimate worldly needs, urges doing good as good has been received, and prohibits seeking corruption.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Material resources are tools, not inherently corrupt possessions.**
- **Benefit received can generate reasons to benefit others.**
- **Personal prosperity should not be pursued through social destruction.**
- **Ethical stewardship integrates present life, generosity, and anti-corruption.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 12:48; 1 Timothy 6:17–19 | Resources create responsibilities for generosity and good works. | A/B | Use advantage for constructive purposes. |
| Judaism — tzedakah and stewardship | Wealth carries duties toward justice and charity. | A/B | Prosperity does not cancel social obligation. |
| Buddhism — dana and non-attachment | Resources can be used beneficially without possessive attachment. | B | Use wealth without being governed by it. |
| Hinduism — artha within dharma | Material prosperity is legitimate when governed by ethical duty. | A/B | Pursue material goods within moral constraints. |
| Sikhism — kirat karo and vand chhakna | Earn honestly and share. | A | Material success should support others. |
| Confucianism — righteous acquisition | Wealth is acceptable when consistent with righteousness. | A/B | Gain should not outrank right conduct. |
| African Traditional | Wealth often entails reciprocal obligations to kin/community. | B | Prosperity carries social responsibility. |

### Conclusion
The source rejects both material absolutism and material rejection: resources are legitimate but ethically conditioned.

# E3 Unit 147 — Qur'an 29:46
## Disagree in the Best Manner and Begin From Common Ground

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 29:46 instructs disputation with People of the Book in the best manner, except in cases of wrongdoing, and points to shared revelation and a common object of worship.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Disagreement should normally use the most constructive available method.**
- **Begin from genuine shared ground where it exists.**
- **Difference need not erase recognition of common commitments.**
- **Respectful engagement does not require passivity toward active wrongdoing.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 1 Peter 3:15–16 | Give reasons with gentleness and respect. | A | Disagree without contempt. |
| Judaism — traditions of principled debate | Deep disagreement can occur within disciplined forms. | B | Preserve relationship while disputing ideas. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Disagreement should remain truthful and non-harmful. | A/B | Use constructive communication. |
| Hinduism — philosophical debate traditions | Rival schools developed formal methods of reasoned disagreement. | B | Contest claims through disciplined argument. |
| Sikhism — interreligious engagement | Shared human/spiritual concerns can coexist with doctrinal disagreement. | B | Recognise common ground without erasing difference. |
| Confucianism — harmony without sameness | Harmony does not require identical views. | A/B | Difference and cooperation can coexist. |
| African Traditional | Deliberative traditions vary widely. | C | Shared interests can structure disagreement. |

### Conclusion
The source provides a pluralism heuristic: identify genuine common ground, preserve principled disagreement, and distinguish ordinary difference from wrongdoing.

# E3 Unit 148 — Qur'an 31:14–15
## Honour Parents Without Surrendering Moral Agency

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 31:14–15 commands gratitude and good companionship toward parents but states that parental pressure toward a prohibited religious act should not be obeyed.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Parents deserve care and gratitude.**
- **Family authority has moral limits.**
- **Refusal of an illegitimate demand does not justify cruelty toward the person making it.**
- **Respect and autonomy can coexist.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Acts 5:29 alongside parental honour | Human authority can be limited by higher moral obligation. | B | Respect authority without treating it as absolute. |
| Judaism — parental honour within halakhic limits | Parental commands do not override all other obligations. | A/B | Filial duty is real but bounded. |
| Buddhism — Sigalovada Sutta | Reciprocal family duties coexist with individual moral practice. | B | Honour family without abandoning ethical responsibility. |
| Hinduism — strong filial duty with higher dharmic constraints | Family obligations exist within wider dharma. | B | Role authority is not necessarily unlimited. |
| Sikhism — equality before moral/spiritual truth | Family hierarchy does not erase conscience. | B | Preserve respect while retaining moral agency. |
| Confucianism — remonstrance toward parents | Filial children may respectfully correct parents rather than blindly comply. | A | Respect can include principled disagreement. |
| African Traditional | Elder authority is often strong but varies in scope and constraint. | C | Respect for elders need not logically imply unlimited obedience. |

### Conclusion
This is a nuanced authority rule: **legitimate relationship does not imply unlimited jurisdiction**.

# E3 Unit 149 — Qur'an 31:18–19

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 31:18–19 prohibits contemptuous treatment of people and arrogant bearing, while recommending moderation in movement and lowering the voice.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not communicate superiority through contemptuous behaviour.**
- **Moderate outward conduct rather than performing dominance.**
- **Volume and manner can become instruments of aggression.**
- **Humility should be embodied socially, not only privately believed.**

| Hinduism — humility in Bhagavad Gita 13:7–8 | Humility and absence of pretence are virtues. | A | Do not perform superiority. |
| Sikhism — rejection of haumai | Ego is a central obstacle. | A | Restrain egoic conduct. |

The source turns humility into observable behaviour: facial expression, gait, and voice can all communicate domination or respect.

# E3 Unit 150 — Qur'an 33:5
## Correct Mistakes Without Treating Them as Deliberate Wrongdoing

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 33:5 distinguishes mistakes from what people deliberately intend, in the context of accurate family naming and attribution.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Intent matters when assessing culpability.**
- **An accidental error should not automatically be treated as deliberate deception.**
- **Correction remains appropriate even where blame is reduced.**
- **Ethical systems should distinguish factual repair from punitive judgement.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 12:47–48 | Responsibility varies with knowledge and awareness. | A/B | Culpability depends partly on what was known and intended. |
| Judaism — Numbers 15:27–31 | Unintentional and deliberate wrongdoing are explicitly distinguished. | A | Intent affects culpability. |
| Buddhism — intention (cetana) | Intention is central to karmic moral assessment. | A | Moral responsibility depends strongly on intention. |
| Hinduism — intention and karma | Intention can affect moral evaluation, though systems vary. | B | Deliberate and accidental acts are not identical. |
| Sikhism | Moral responsibility distinguishes egoic intention from error, though no exact legal analogue is asserted. | B/C | Assess conduct with attention to intention. |
| Confucianism | Character and deliberate motive matter in evaluating conduct. | B | Mistake and wilful misconduct should not be conflated. |
| African Traditional | Customary systems often distinguish accident from intentional harm, with substantial variation. | B | Repair may be required even when blame differs. |

### Conclusion
The unit contributes a foundational accountability distinction: **harm, correction, and culpability are related but not identical variables**.
