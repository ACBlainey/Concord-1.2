# E3 Unit 131 — Qur'an 23:3
## Turn Away From Worthless or Harmful Discourse

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 23:3 praises those who turn away from futile or morally empty speech and activity.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Attention is a resource and need not be given to every provocation or distraction.**
- **Disengagement can be an ethical response to unproductive discourse.**
- **Avoid conduct that consumes social energy without constructive purpose.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 2 Timothy 2:23 | Foolish quarrels are to be avoided because they generate conflict. | A | Do not feed pointless disputes. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 26:4 | Some foolish discourse is better left unanswered. | A/B | Disengagement can prevent unproductive escalation. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Idle chatter is restrained. | A | Do not waste speech on harmful or purposeless talk. |
| Hinduism — disciplined speech | Speech and attention should serve worthwhile ends. | B | Avoid purposeless verbal conduct. |
| Sikhism — restraint from slander and empty speech | Speech should remain truthful and constructive. | B | Do not cultivate destructive talk. |
| Confucianism — careful speech | The cultivated person is restrained in words. | B | Speak with purpose and propriety. |
| African Traditional | Speech norms vary, though restraint in quarrels is common. | C | Not every dispute merits engagement. |

### Conclusion
The source treats selective disengagement as a form of attention governance rather than passivity.

# E3 Unit 132 — Qur'an 23:8
## Honour Trusts and Commitments

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 23:8 praises those who faithfully observe trusts and covenants.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Protect what has been entrusted to you.**
- **Fulfil legitimate commitments.**
- **Reliability is a stable character trait, not merely an occasional transaction.**

| Hinduism — satya and duty | Truth and obligation constrain conduct. | B | Honour entrusted responsibility. |

### Conclusion
This repeats one of the corpus's most persistent institutional ethics: dependable cooperation requires trustworthy agents.

# E3 Unit 133 — Qur'an 24:4
## Serious Accusations Require Evidence

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:4 imposes severe consequences for accusations of sexual misconduct made without the required witnesses.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Highly damaging accusations require strong evidentiary support.**
- **Reputation should not be destroyable through unsupported allegation.**
- **The seriousness of a claim can justify a correspondingly demanding evidence threshold.**
- **Speech rights do not erase accountability for reckless accusation.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Deuteronomy 19:15; Matthew 18:16 | Serious matters are established through multiple witnesses. | A | Consequential accusations require evidence. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 19:15–21 | Evidentiary thresholds and false-witness accountability are explicit. | A | Protect adjudication from unsupported accusation. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | False and harmful speech is prohibited. | B | Do not damage another through false claims. |
| Hinduism — witness and defamation traditions | Formal testimony carries responsibilities. | B | Serious accusations should be evidentially grounded. |
| Sikhism — truthfulness and rejection of slander | Reputation should not be attacked through falsehood. | B | Do not slander. |
| Confucianism — trustworthy speech | Public claims should be reliable. | B | Speech affecting others carries responsibility. |
| African Traditional | Evidentiary rules differ widely across customary systems. | C | Serious allegations require recognised proof procedures. |

### Conclusion
The transferable principle is evidentiary proportionality; the specific legal penalty remains tradition-specific rather than universalised.

# E3 Unit 134 — Qur'an 24:11–16
## Do Not Amplify Unverified Scandal

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:11–16 criticises a community for circulating a serious accusation, asking why people did not presume good and demand evidence, and condemning transmission of claims about which speakers had no knowledge.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not propagate serious allegations merely because they are circulating.**
- **Transmission creates responsibility even when one did not originate the claim.**
- **Demand evidence before amplifying reputationally destructive information.**
- **Virality or repetition does not convert a rumour into knowledge.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 3; Ephesians 4:25 | Speech can cause extensive harm and should be truthful. | A/B | Do not spread harmful falsehood. |
| Judaism — lashon hara traditions | Harmful speech and gossip receive extensive ethical regulation. | A | Do not circulate damaging speech irresponsibly. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | False, divisive, and harmful speech is prohibited. | A | Do not amplify unverified harmful claims. |
| Hinduism — truthful/non-harmful speech | Speech should be truthful and non-injurious. | A/B | Do not turn rumour into social harm. |
| Sikhism — rejection of slander | Slander conflicts with truthful living. | A/B | Do not degrade others through unsupported speech. |
| Confucianism — rectification and careful speech | Words should correspond responsibly to reality. | B | Public language should not outrun knowledge. |
| African Traditional | Gossip sanctions vary, but reputational speech is commonly socially consequential. | B/C | Rumour transmission carries communal consequences. |

### Conclusion
This is a strong information-ethics unit: responsibility attaches to **forwarding** as well as originating a claim.

# E3 Unit 135 — Qur'an 24:19
## Do Not Desire or Promote the Spread of Social Corruption

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:19 condemns those who desire that indecency or scandal spread through the community.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not promote harmful behaviour merely because it attracts attention.**
- **The incentive to spread scandal can itself become ethically relevant.**
- **Information ecosystems can reward corruption by making degradation entertaining or socially profitable.**
- **Avoid becoming an amplifier of harm for curiosity, status, or spectacle.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 1:32; Ephesians 5:11 | Approval or participation in wrongdoing is morally significant. | B | Do not celebrate or promote destructive conduct. |
| Judaism — prohibition on placing stumbling blocks / harmful speech | Facilitating another's wrongdoing is constrained. | B | Do not create conditions that spread harm. |
| Buddhism — encouragement toward wholesome rather than unwholesome states | One should not cultivate or promote harmful mental/social states. | B | Do not reinforce unwholesome conduct. |
| Hinduism — responsibility for facilitating wrongdoing | Association and encouragement can carry moral consequences. | B | Do not promote destructive behaviour. |
| Sikhism — truthful living and resistance to vice | Vice should not be socially glorified. | B | Do not normalise harmful conduct. |
| Confucianism — moral example | Social norms are shaped by what communities honour. | B | Do not reward behaviour that degrades social order. |
| African Traditional | Shame, reputation, and communal sanction can regulate antisocial conduct, with major variation. | C | Communities influence what conduct spreads. |

### Conclusion
The source adds an incentive-layer ethic: systems should consider not only who commits harm, but who benefits from making harm spread.

# E3 Unit 136 — Qur'an 24:27–29
## Privacy and Consent Before Entering Another's Space

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:27–29 instructs people not to enter homes other than their own without permission and greeting, and to leave if told to return.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Private space should not be entered without consent.**
- **Absence of permission is not permission.**
- **A refusal of entry should be respected without coercion.**
- **Privacy rules can distinguish inhabited private space from genuinely unoccupied places serving legitimate public use.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | No close canonical household-entry rule identified; respect for neighbour and property supplies functional support. | C | Respect another's private domain. |
| Judaism | Property and privacy traditions strongly protect household boundaries, though exact formulations differ. | B | Entry into private space requires legitimate authority or permission. |
| Buddhism — monastic rules | Access to spaces and households is regulated by propriety and permission. | B | Do not intrude improperly. |
| Hinduism — household propriety | Guest/host relations imply recognised boundaries and permission. | B/C | Respect household autonomy. |
| Sikhism | No exact canonical equivalent identified. | C | Respect another person's legitimate private space. |
| Confucianism — li | Proper conduct regulates entry, visiting, and household boundaries. | B | Observe relational and spatial propriety. |
| African Traditional | Household boundaries and guest protocols are widespread but culturally diverse. | B | Entry is governed by recognised consent and hospitality rules. |

### Conclusion
This is a remarkably explicit privacy rule: consent governs access, and the occupant retains the right to refuse entry.

# E3 Unit 137 — Qur'an 24:30–31
## Mutual Sexual Restraint and Respect for Bodily Privacy

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:30–31 instructs both believing men and women to lower their gaze and guard sexual modesty, with additional dress and disclosure rules for women.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Sexual self-restraint applies to both men and women.**
- **Responsibility begins with management of one's own gaze and conduct rather than treating another person as responsible for all desire.**
- **Bodily privacy can be ethically protected.**
- **Detailed dress prescriptions remain tradition-specific and are not universalised by the extraction.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:27–28; modesty traditions | Responsibility includes control of one's own gaze and desire. | A | Govern sexual attention rather than objectifying others. |
| Judaism — sexual modesty traditions | Gaze, dress, and sexual boundaries are regulated. | A/B | Protect sexual boundaries and privacy. |
| Buddhism — Third Precept and sense restraint | Sexual misconduct and undisciplined sensory craving are restrained. | A/B | Manage desire without exploiting others. |
| Hinduism — brahmacharya and modesty | Sexual self-control is a recognised discipline. | B | Desire is subject to ethical governance. |
| Sikhism — restraint from lust | Kaam is to be governed rather than allowed to dominate conduct. | B | Control one's own sexual impulses. |
| Confucianism — propriety | Sexual and bodily conduct is regulated through propriety. | B | Respect relational and bodily boundaries. |
| African Traditional | Modesty and sexual norms vary substantially. | C | Sexual conduct is socially bounded rather than consequence-free. |

### Conclusion
The broadly transferable core is reciprocal self-restraint and bodily respect; detailed clothing rules are retained as source context rather than treated as universal ethics.

# E3 Unit 138 — Qur'an 24:33
## Do Not Coerce Vulnerable People Into Sexual Exploitation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 24:33 explicitly prohibits forcing slave women into prostitution for material gain when they seek chastity.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not use economic or ownership power to compel sexual activity.**
- **A vulnerable person's refusal remains morally significant despite unequal status.**
- **Profit does not justify sexual exploitation.**
- **Power over livelihood or legal status must not be converted into sexual entitlement.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 1 Corinthians 6:18; anti-exploitation implications | Sexual exploitation conflicts with bodily dignity, though no exact parallel command is identified. | B | Do not profit from sexual exploitation. |
| Judaism | Biblical law contains historically different slavery structures; no exact direct equivalent asserted. | C | Power over a dependent does not erase moral constraints. |
| Buddhism — Third Precept | Sexual misconduct and exploitation are prohibited. | A/B | Do not exploit vulnerability sexually. |
| Hinduism — non-harm and sexual restraint | Coercive sexual exploitation violates non-harm. | B | Desire and profit do not justify coercion. |
| Sikhism — equality and restraint from lust | Exploitation conflicts with human equality and self-control. | B | Do not turn power into sexual coercion. |
| Confucianism | Historical structures complicate direct equivalence; humane conduct constrains abuse. | C | Authority should not become predation. |
| African Traditional | Historical practices vary greatly; no pan-African equivalent asserted. | D/C | No responsible universal claim beyond the general anti-exploitation principle. |

### Conclusion
The unit is unusually direct in linking sexual coercion to economic exploitation and unequal power.

# E3 Unit 140 — Qur'an 25:63
## Respond to Provocation Without Becoming Provocative

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 25:63 praises humble people who, when addressed aggressively or ignorantly, respond peacefully rather than mirroring the provocation.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not let another person's aggression dictate your own manner.**
- **A peaceful response can interrupt escalation.**
- **Humility and self-command are compatible with dignity.**
- **Not every insult requires a symmetrical answer.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 1 Peter 3:9 | Do not repay insult with insult. | A | Refuse reciprocal verbal hostility. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 15:1 | A gentle answer turns away wrath. | A | De-escalate through measured speech. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 3–5 | Hatred is not ended by hatred. | A | Break hostile reciprocity. |
| Hinduism — forgiveness and disciplined speech | Forbearance restrains reactive hostility. | B | Do not mirror provocation automatically. |
| Sikhism — overcoming anger | Anger should not capture conduct. | B | Maintain self-command under insult. |
| Confucianism — cultivated restraint | The junzi is not governed by petty provocation. | B | Preserve propriety under insult. |
| African Traditional | Reconciliation and restraint norms vary by community. | B/C | Avoid converting insult into escalating feud. |

### Conclusion
The verse reinforces a highly recurrent Qur'anic and cross-traditional pattern: ethical agency includes the ability not to mirror another person's worst behaviour.

# Interim Findings: Units 131–140

| Cluster | Units |
|---|---|
| Attention and discourse restraint | 131 |
| Trust and covenant | 132 |
| Evidentiary protection of reputation | 133–135 |
| Privacy and consent | 136, 139 |
| Sexual self-restraint and anti-exploitation | 137–138 |
| Non-escalatory response | 140 |

Units 133–135 form a particularly modern-looking information architecture without requiring modern terminology: require evidence for damaging claims, do not retransmit rumours as knowledge, and do not create incentives for scandal to spread.

Units 136 and 139 also make privacy unusually concrete. The ethical unit is not an abstract preference for secrecy but **control over access to personal space**, including within the family.
