# E3 Unit 101 — Qur'an 11:85
## Honest Exchange and Non-Deprivation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 11:85, within the account of Shu'ayb, commands full measure and weight with justice, prohibits depriving people of what is theirs, and rejects corrupt conduct.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Use honest standards in exchange.**
- **Do not obtain advantage by giving another person less than is due.**
- **Commercial deception is not merely private dishonesty; it damages social order.**
- **Economic transactions should remain intelligible and fair to both parties.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Leviticus 19:35–36 | Honest weights and measures are explicitly required. | A | Do not cheat through measurement. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 25:13–16 | False weights and measures are prohibited. | A | Commercial standards must be honest. |
| Buddhism — Right Livelihood | Livelihood should not depend on deception or harm. | B | Do not profit through dishonest exchange. |
| Hinduism — commercial dharma | Fraudulent measures are condemned in legal traditions. | A/B | Trade through reliable measures. |
| Sikhism — kirat karo | Honest earning is a central practical principle. | B | Wealth should be earned without fraud. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthiness sustains exchange and social order. | B | Economic relationships require reliability. |
| African Traditional | Market rules and sanctions against cheating occur across many societies, though practices vary. | B/C | Shared exchange depends on recognised fair standards. |

### Conclusion
The repetition of this principle across Qur'anic narratives is retained as evidence of emphasis: mundane market integrity is repeatedly treated as morally consequential.

# E3 Unit 102 — Qur'an 11:116–117
## Social Responsibility to Resist Corruption

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 11:116–117 laments the scarcity of people who restrain corruption and contrasts destructive wrongdoing with communities whose people are acting constructively.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **A stable society requires people willing to resist destructive conduct.**
- **Private virtue alone may be insufficient where corruption becomes systemic.**
- **Communities are more resilient when some members actively preserve functioning order.**
- **Collective destruction should not be attributed merely to difference where people are engaged in reform.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:13–16 | Individuals and communities are called to exert preserving moral influence. | B | Ethical responsibility includes positive influence on the surrounding community. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:17; prophetic traditions | Wrongdoing is to be confronted rather than silently normalised. | A/B | Community members share responsibility for resisting wrong. |
| Buddhism — sangha discipline | Community standards require correction of harmful conduct. | B | Ethical communities maintain conditions for wholesome conduct. |
| Hinduism — dharma and preservation of order | Right action contributes to maintenance of social/cosmic order. | B | Resist conduct that degrades legitimate order. |
| Sikhism — resistance to oppression | Ethical responsibility can require active opposition to injustice. | B | Do not remain passive before destructive wrong. |
| Confucianism — remonstrance | Officials and cultivated persons may have duties to correct rulers and disorder. | A/B | Responsible members should challenge corrupt conduct. |
| African Traditional | Elders, councils, and communal sanctions often act to preserve social order. | B | Community stability can require active correction. |

### Conclusion
The source contributes an anti-passivity principle: preventing corruption is itself a social function, not merely the absence of personal wrongdoing.

# E3 Unit 103 — Qur'an 12:23–35
## Resisting Sexual Coercion and Abuse of Power

### Qur'anic Source
The Joseph narrative in Qur'an 12:23–35 depicts a person resisting sexual pressure from someone with substantial household power, followed by manipulation, social pressure, and imprisonment despite evidence favouring his account.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Sexual access should not be obtained through coercive power.**
- **A subordinate person's refusal should be respected.**
- **Status must not convert desire into entitlement.**
- **Institutions can compound an initial abuse when they punish the resisting party to protect reputation or power.**
- **Evidence should matter more than the social standing of the parties.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Genesis 39 | The parallel Joseph narrative depicts refusal of sexual pressure and subsequent false accusation. | A | Power does not make coerced sexual access legitimate. |
| Judaism — Genesis 39 | Direct textual parallel. | A | Resist sexual wrongdoing despite personal cost. |
| Buddhism — Third Precept | Sexual misconduct is prohibited. | B | Sexual conduct should not exploit or harm others. |
| Hinduism — sexual restraint and non-harm | Abuse of sexual power conflicts with self-restraint and dharma. | B | Desire does not justify coercion. |
| Sikhism — restraint from lust and equality | Lust should not dominate conduct or become exploitation. | B | Govern desire and respect others' dignity. |
| Confucianism — propriety and restraint | Sexual/familial conduct is constrained by role and propriety. | B/C | Power and desire should remain disciplined. |
| African Traditional | Sexual norms and authority structures vary greatly; no uniform textual equivalent is asserted. | C | Coercive abuse of relational power is socially destructive. |

### Discussion
This is narrative-derived rather than a compact command. It is included because the ethical structure is separable and unusually clear: coercion, refusal, evidentiary assessment, reputation management, and institutional injustice are all visible within the account.

### Conclusion
The unit expands the corpus's anti-coercion theme into sexual and institutional power.

# E3 Unit 104 — Qur'an 12:54–55
## Competence and Trustworthiness in Public Responsibility

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 12:54–55 depicts Joseph being entrusted with authority after his reliability becomes evident; he identifies himself as capable of safeguarding resources and possessing relevant knowledge.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Public responsibility should consider both trustworthiness and competence.**
- **Good intentions alone do not substitute for relevant capability.**
- **Capability without trustworthiness is likewise insufficient for fiduciary office.**
- **Roles involving shared resources require people able to preserve and understand them.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Acts 6:3 | Administrators are selected for both good character and relevant wisdom. | A | Appoint trustworthy and capable people. |
| Judaism — Exodus 18:21 | Officials are selected for capability, integrity, and resistance to dishonest gain. | A | Public office requires character and competence. |
| Buddhism — qualities of righteous leadership | Moral character and practical wisdom are both valued. | B | Leadership should combine ethics with judgement. |
| Hinduism — rajadharma / Arthashastra traditions | Officials are assessed for capability and integrity. | A/B | Entrust responsibility to competent, reliable agents. |
| Sikhism — service and responsible leadership | Leadership is tied to service and moral discipline. | B | Authority should be exercised competently for common good. |
| Confucianism — merit and virtue in office | Government should employ capable and virtuous persons. | A | Select officials for competence and character. |
| African Traditional | Selection of elders/office-holders often considers experience, reputation, and social trust, with wide variation. | B | Entrusted roles require recognised capability and integrity. |

### Conclusion
This unit yields a strong governance principle: **trust and competence are complementary requirements of stewardship**.

# E3 Unit 105 — Qur'an 12:58–92
## Power After Injury: Restraint and Forgiveness

### Qur'anic Source
Later in the Joseph narrative, Joseph gains substantial power over brothers who had seriously wronged him. After testing and disclosure, he ultimately declines vengeance and forgives them.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Possessing power over a former wrongdoer does not create an obligation to retaliate.**
- **The moral test of forgiveness can become most significant when retaliation is finally possible.**
- **Past victimhood does not automatically justify future abuse of power.**
- **Reconciliation can follow truth and recognition rather than denial of the original wrong.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Genesis 45; 50:15–21 | Joseph forgives his brothers in the parallel narrative. | A | Do not use later power merely to avenge earlier injury. |
| Judaism — Genesis 45; 50 | Direct textual parallel. | A | Power can be used for reconciliation rather than vengeance. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 3–5 | Hatred is not ended by hatred. | A | Break retaliatory cycles. |
| Hinduism — forgiveness as virtue | Restraint after injury is praised. | B | Power need not be used vindictively. |
| Sikhism — absence of enmity | Justice need not become personal vengeance. | B | Resist hatred even when strong enough to retaliate. |
| Confucianism — principled response to injury | Conduct should follow rightness rather than uncontrolled resentment. | B | Do not let old grievance capture present power. |
| African Traditional | Restorative reconciliation after serious wrong exists in many traditions, though mechanisms vary. | B | Reintegrate where truth, repair, and circumstances permit. |

### Conclusion
The narrative supplies a particularly strong case of anti-vengeance because forgiveness occurs from a position of power rather than helplessness.

# E3 Unit 106 — Qur'an 13:22
## Patience, Generosity, and Answering Harm With Good

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 13:22 praises patient perseverance, giving privately and publicly, and repelling or countering bad conduct with good.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Persevere under difficulty without abandoning ethical conduct.**
- **Give both privately and publicly where each is appropriate.**
- **Where possible, interrupt harmful reciprocity by responding constructively.**
- **Do not assume that every wrong must be mirrored back to its source.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 12:17–21 | Evil is not to be repaid with evil; good is used to overcome evil. | A | Counter wrongdoing without reproducing it. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 25:21–22 | An enemy in need should be fed and given drink. | A | Respond to hostility without abandoning beneficence. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 3–5 | Hatred is not ended by hatred. | A | Break negative reciprocity. |
| Hinduism — forgiveness and non-harm | Ethical discipline restrains retaliatory harm. | B | Do not mirror wrongdoing automatically. |
| Sikhism — overcoming anger and enmity | Good conduct should not be surrendered to hatred. | B | Answer hostility without becoming governed by it. |
| Confucianism — Analects 14.34 | Injury is answered with uprightness rather than simple retaliation; direct “good for evil” equivalence is qualified. | B/C | Let principle, not imitation, determine response. |
| African Traditional | Reconciliation practices can interrupt feud cycles, but traditions vary. | B/C | Replace retaliatory escalation with restoration where possible. |

### Conclusion
This is another high-convergence non-retaliation source and will be important when source frequency is separated from distinct-principle count.

# E3 Unit 107 — Qur'an 13:25
## Covenant-Breaking, Severed Social Bonds, and Corruption

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 13:25 condemns breaking binding commitments, severing relationships that ought to be maintained, and spreading corruption.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not break legitimate commitments without sufficient cause.**
- **Do not needlessly sever relationships whose maintenance serves legitimate social order.**
- **Relational fragmentation can become a form of wider social corruption.**
- **Stable systems depend partly on reliable bonds between persons and groups.**

| Buddhism — harmonious community | Divisive speech and schism are ethically serious. | B | Do not needlessly fragment cooperative relationships. |
| Hinduism — relational dharma | Duties arise through legitimate social relationships. | B | Maintain rightful relational obligations. |
| Sikhism — sangat and truthful living | Reliable fellowship and truth support community. | B | Do not undermine legitimate bonds through deceit. |

| African Traditional | Kinship and communal bonds often create durable reciprocal obligations. | A/B | Do not casually sever socially sustaining ties. |

### Conclusion
The source links promise-keeping to social topology: repeated breach and severance can degrade the connective structure on which cooperative society depends.

### Qur'anic Source

# E3 Unit 109 — Qur'an 16:91–92
## Do Not Use Commitments as Instruments of Deception

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 16:91–92 commands fulfilment of covenants and condemns breaking solemn commitments after confirmation, including the use of oaths deceptively because one group is stronger or more advantageous than another.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Keep deliberately confirmed commitments.**
- **Do not make promises merely to obtain temporary cooperation before defecting.**
- **A more powerful or profitable alternative does not automatically justify betrayal.**
- **Contracts lose social function when commitment language becomes a strategic deception.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:37 | Speech should be reliably truthful. | A/B | Do not weaponise promises through deception. |
| Judaism — Numbers 30:2 | A deliberate vow is binding. | A | Honour confirmed commitments. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Deliberate deception is prohibited. | B | Do not induce reliance through false words. |
| Hinduism — satya and vows | Truthfulness constrains strategic false promises. | B | Keep legitimate solemn undertakings. |
| Sikhism — truthful living | Word and action should correspond. | B | Do not manipulate trust through false commitment. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthiness is indispensable to stable cooperation. | A | Do not abandon faith for immediate advantage. |
| African Traditional | Oaths and agreements often carry strong customary force. | B | A stronger alternative does not erase an existing obligation automatically. |

### Conclusion
The source is more specific than generic promise-keeping: it prohibits **strategic commitment followed by opportunistic defection**.

# E3 Unit 110 — Qur'an 16:125
## Persuasion Through Wisdom and Respectful Argument

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 16:125 instructs invitation through wisdom, good counsel, and argument conducted in the best manner.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Persuade through reasons and constructive communication rather than needless hostility.**
- **The manner of disagreement is ethically significant.**
- **Good ends do not make abusive argumentative methods automatically acceptable.**
- **Communication should be adapted toward understanding rather than humiliation.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 1 Peter 3:15–16 | Defence of belief should be given with gentleness and respect. | A | Argue for conviction without contempt. |
| Judaism — rabbinic disputation traditions | Strong disagreement can coexist with disciplined argument and preservation of relationship. | B | Dispute through reasons rather than degradation. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Speech should be truthful, beneficial, timely, and non-harmful. | A/B | Persuasion should avoid harmful speech. |
| Hinduism — traditions of philosophical debate | Formal debate distinguishes reasoned engagement from destructive quarrel. | B | Use disciplined argument to test disagreement. |
| Sikhism — truthful speech and interreligious engagement | Conviction should be expressed without slander or egoic hostility. | B | Speak truth without degrading others. |
| Confucianism — remonstrance and cultivated speech | Correction should be principled and properly expressed. | B | Manner matters when challenging another. |
| African Traditional | Deliberative councils and proverb-based persuasion occur in many traditions, though no single model is universal. | B/C | Use socially constructive deliberation where possible. |

### Conclusion
The verse adds a method ethic to freedom of belief: where persuasion is sought, the preferred tools are wisdom, good counsel, and respectful argument rather than coercive or degrading communication.

# Interim Findings: Units 101–110

| Cluster | Units |
|---|---|
| Honest exchange and non-deprivation | 101 |
| Active resistance to corruption | 102 |
| Sexual coercion and institutional abuse of power | 103 |
| Competence plus trustworthiness in office | 104 |
| Forgiveness from a position of power | 105 |
| Countering harm without mirroring it | 106 |
| Covenant and relationship maintenance | 107 |
| Justice plus positive beneficence | 108 |
| Anti-deceptive commitment / anti-opportunistic defection | 109 |
| Respectful persuasion and argument | 110 |

Several independently generated themes become more structurally precise here.

Unit 104 distinguishes **ethical intent from operational competence**. The anchor text does not treat trustworthy character alone as sufficient for resource stewardship; knowledge and capability also matter.

Unit 109 sharpens promise-keeping into an **anti-defection principle**: commitment must not be used as a temporary instrument until a stronger or more profitable partner appears.

Units 103, 105, and 110 each constrain power in a different domain: sexual/household power, retaliatory power, and persuasive power. Across all three, possession of an ability does not itself establish ethical permission to use it without restraint.

Unit 108 is also notable because it explicitly places **beneficence beyond justice**: avoiding wrong and meeting minimum fairness do not exhaust positive ethical responsibility.

These observations remain provisional until completion and audit of the Qur'anic anchor corpus.
