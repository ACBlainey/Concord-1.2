# E3 Unit 371 — Qur'an 42:37
## Anger Can Be Governed Without Denying Injury

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 42:37 praises people who, when angered, forgive.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Anger can be acknowledged without being obeyed.
- Forgiveness can be chosen after genuine injury.
- Emotional activation and behavioural response should remain separable.
- Systems that create space between injury and retaliation can reduce escalation.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Colossians 3:13 | Forgiveness after grievance is explicit. | A | Injury need not determine retaliation. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 19:11 | Overlooking offence is associated with prudence. | A/B | Restraint can exceed retaliation. |
| Buddhism | Anger is treated as a destructive state requiring disciplined response. | A | Do not act from hatred. |
| Hinduism | Forgiveness and control of anger strongly converge. | A | Govern anger. |
| Sikhism | Anger is a major vice; forgiveness strongly converges. | A | Do not become ruled by anger. |
| Confucianism | Self-command and measured response are central. | B | Emotion should remain bounded by right conduct. |
| African Traditional | Forgiveness norms vary. | B/C | Reconciliation can interrupt cycles of retaliation. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 161 and anger-restraint cluster.**

# E3 Unit 372 — Qur'an 42:38
## Shared Affairs Should Be Conducted Through Consultation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 42:38 praises those whose affairs are conducted through consultation among themselves.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Decisions affecting a group should not default to unilateral preference.
- Consultation can distribute information and agency.
- Participation improves legitimacy when it has real influence.
- Deliberation is an institutional practice, not merely personal politeness.
- Shared governance requires mechanisms for hearing affected parties.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Acts 15 | Collective deliberation provides a strong functional parallel. | A/B | Shared matters benefit from consultation. |
| Judaism | Council and deliberative traditions strongly converge. | B | Collective decisions should hear relevant voices. |
| Buddhism — sangha procedure | Communal decisions are institutionally deliberated. | A | Shared affairs require communal process. |
| Hinduism | Councils and assemblies provide historical parallels. | B | Governance can be deliberative. |
| Sikhism — collective deliberative traditions | Strong functional convergence. | A/B | Shared authority should include consultation. |
| Confucianism | Remonstrance and consultative administration constrain rulers. | B | Authority should hear informed counsel. |
| African Traditional | Council governance is widespread but diverse. | A/B | Shared matters can be decided deliberatively. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 158 and consultation/governance cluster.**

# E3 Unit 373 — Qur'an 42:40–43
## Legitimate Redress, Forgiveness, and Patient Restraint Can Coexist

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 42:40–43 recognises proportionate response to injury while praising forgiveness and reconciliation and denying blame to those who defend themselves after being wronged.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Victims retain legitimate agency after injury.
- Justice need not require compulsory forgiveness.
- Redress should remain proportionate.
- Forgiveness is especially valuable when freely chosen.
- Systems should distinguish self-defence from aggression.
- Patient restraint can be morally superior without erasing the right to protection.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Forgiveness is strongly emphasised, while traditions differ on defensive force. | A/B | Prefer forgiveness without trivialising injury. |
| Judaism — proportional justice | Proportionate redress is strongly developed. | A | Bound response to the wrong. |
| Buddhism | Non-harm and forgiveness strongly converge, though retaliatory rights are less direct. | B | Restraint is preferable to vengeance. |
| Hinduism | Duty, proportional response, and forgiveness provide strong functional parallels. | B | Justice and restraint can coexist. |
| Sikhism | Defence against oppression is recognised alongside restraint. | A/B | Protect without becoming oppressive. |
| Confucianism | Ordered justice constrains private retaliation. | B | Redress should remain governed by rightness. |
| African Traditional | Restorative and retaliatory systems vary. | B/C | Repair and proportion can restrain feud. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 159 and victim-agency/proportionality cluster.**

# E3 Unit 374 — Qur'an 49:6
## Verify Consequential Reports Before Acting

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:6 warns against acting on a report from an unreliable source without verification, lest people be harmed through ignorance and regret follow.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Information quality should be checked before consequential action.
- Source reliability matters.
- Verification thresholds should rise with potential harm.
- Regret after preventable misinformation is not an adequate safeguard.
- Information systems need friction between report receipt and coercive response.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Proverbs 18:17 | Initial testimony should be examined rather than automatically accepted. | A/B | Verify before judging. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 19:15–18 | Evidence and inquiry precede serious judgement. | A | Investigate consequential claims. |
| Buddhism | Discernment and avoidance of false speech strongly converge. | B | Test information before harmful action. |
| Hinduism — pramana | Valid knowledge requires reliable means. | A/B | Match action to warranted belief. |
| Sikhism | Truth and rejection of slander strongly converge. | B | Do not act on unreliable accusation. |
| Confucianism | Careful investigation and trustworthy reporting support good governance. | B | Verify before decision. |
| African Traditional | Evidentiary practices vary. | C | Serious action should not rest on untested report. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 163 and verification cluster.**

# E3 Unit 375 — Qur'an 49:9–10
## Intervene in Internal Conflict to Restore a Just Peace

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:9–10 directs intervention between fighting groups, resistance to the aggressing party where necessary, and fair reconciliation once aggression ceases.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Neutrality between aggressor and victim is not always justice.
- Intervention should aim at restoration rather than permanent domination.
- Peace without fairness can preserve the conditions of renewed conflict.
- Once aggression ends, adjudication should return to impartiality.
- Community membership creates responsibilities to repair internal conflict.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:9 | Peacemaking strongly converges, though coercive intervention is less direct. | B | Work actively toward peace. |
| Judaism | Judicial and communal duties to restrain wrongdoing provide functional parallels. | B | Peace should be joined to justice. |
| Buddhism | Reconciliation strongly converges; coercive restraint is more tradition-dependent. | B/C | Restore peace without hatred. |
| Hinduism | Duty to restrain wrongdoing and restore order provides strong parallels. | B | Intervention should restore just order. |
| Sikhism | Defence against oppression and commitment to justice strongly converge. | A/B | Restrain aggression and restore peace. |
| Confucianism | Governance should correct disorder and re-establish harmony. | B | Peace requires legitimate order. |
| African Traditional | Mediation and restorative mechanisms vary widely. | B | Community can bear responsibility for reconciliation. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 164 and conflict-resolution cluster.**

# E3 Unit 376 — Qur'an 49:11
## Do Not Build Social Hierarchy Through Ridicule and Degrading Labels

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:11 prohibits ridicule between groups, mutual defamation, and insulting nicknames, while warning that the mocked person may be better than the mocker.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Status humiliation is a form of social harm.
- Group identity should not become permission for contempt.
- Labels can freeze persons into degrading categories.
- Perceived superiority is epistemically uncertain.
- Social equality requires control of everyday humiliation, not only formal law.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 2:1–4 | Status partiality is rejected. | A/B | Do not demean persons through social rank. |
| Judaism | Public humiliation is treated seriously in rabbinic ethics. | A/B | Protect dignity in speech. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Abusive and divisive speech is constrained. | A | Do not use speech to degrade. |
| Hinduism | Non-harm and disciplined speech provide strong convergence. | B | Avoid humiliating speech. |
| Sikhism | Human equality and rejection of caste superiority strongly converge. | A | Do not construct superiority through identity. |
| Confucianism | Respectful address and propriety strongly constrain humiliation. | A/B | Preserve dignity in social interaction. |
| African Traditional | Status and speech norms vary. | C | Humiliation can fracture community. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 165 and dignity/speech cluster.**

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:12 warns against excessive suspicion, spying, and backbiting.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Suspicion should not automatically become surveillance.
- Privacy has ethical value even within a community.
- Information acquisition itself can be wrongful.

- Trusting societies require limits on intrusive monitoring and corrosive gossip.

# E3 Unit 378 — Qur'an 49:13
## Human Diversity Does Not Establish a Hierarchy of Inherent Worth

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:13 addresses humanity as originating from a common human pair and describes peoples and tribes as existing for mutual recognition rather than inherent superiority.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Group difference is not evidence of intrinsic human rank.
- Diversity can serve recognition rather than domination.
- Status systems based purely on ancestry are ethically unstable.
- Shared humanity precedes many social classifications.
- Evaluation should focus on conduct rather than inherited category.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Galatians 3:28 | Major inherited/status distinctions are denied ultimate moral hierarchy. | A/B | Human worth is not fixed by group category. |
| Judaism | Common human creation provides a strong universal dignity strand, alongside particular covenant identity. | B | Shared humanity constrains hierarchy. |
| Buddhism | Caste superiority is rejected in multiple texts. | A | Conduct, not birth, determines moral standing. |
| Hinduism | Traditions contain both hierarchical and universalist strands, so equivalence is mixed. | C | Some strands affirm deeper equality beyond status. |
| Sikhism | Caste hierarchy is explicitly rejected. | A | Birth does not determine human worth. |
| Confucianism | Moral cultivation is broadly available, though social roles remain differentiated. | B | Worth is not reducible to inherited category. |
| African Traditional | Kinship/status systems vary substantially. | C | No single pan-African equivalence asserted. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 167 and human-equality cluster.**

# E3 Unit 380 — Qur'an 55:7–9
## Shared Measures Must Not Be Manipulated

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 55:7–9 invokes balance and commands that measure be maintained justly without deficiency.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Shared standards are infrastructure for fairness.
- Manipulating measurement creates asymmetric advantage.
- Justice requires consistency in the metrics used to allocate or exchange.
- Standards themselves should be auditable.
- A system cannot remain fair if actors can privately alter its measuring instruments.

### Comparative Analysis

|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Leviticus 19:35–36 | Honest measures are explicit. | A | Preserve accurate common standards. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 25:13–16 | False weights and measures are prohibited. | A | Do not manipulate metrics for gain. |
| Buddhism — Right Livelihood | Fraudulent trade is unethical. | B | Exchange requires honest standards. |
| Hinduism | Commercial ethics strongly constrain false measurement. | A/B | Use reliable measures. |
| Sikhism | Honest earning provides strong convergence. | B | Do not profit through manipulated standards. |
| Confucianism | Trustworthiness supports stable shared standards. | B | Reliable metrics support social trust. |
| African Traditional | Measurement systems vary. | B/C | Shared standards should not be privately distorted. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Unit 169 and measurement-integrity cluster.**
