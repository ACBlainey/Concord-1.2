# E3 Unit 061 — Qur'an 4:94
## Verify Before Acting Against Another Person

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:94 instructs believers travelling or acting in conflict not to deny another person's peaceful profession or status merely in pursuit of worldly gain, and explicitly commands verification.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Verify relevant facts before taking harmful action.**
- **Do not disregard evidence of peaceful intent because doing so is materially convenient.**
- **Material incentive can corrupt judgement and should be recognised as a source of bias.**
- **When life and safety are at stake, uncertainty requires caution rather than opportunistic presumption.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 7:1–5; John 7:24 | Judgement should not rest on superficial or hypocritical assessment. | B | Investigate fairly before condemning. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 19:15–20 | Serious accusations require evidence and careful inquiry. | A/B | Verify claims before imposing serious consequences. |
| Buddhism — Right View and non-harm | Misperception should not become a basis for harmful action. | B | Examine before acting harmfully. |
| Hinduism — dharmic adjudication | Judgement requires attention to evidence and circumstance. | B | Do not punish on careless assumption. |
| Sikhism — justice and truthful conduct | Action should be grounded in truth rather than greed. | B | Do not let material interest distort judgement. |
| Confucianism — careful judgement and rectification | Responsible officials should investigate rather than act through arbitrary impulse. | B | Establish facts before consequential action. |
| African Traditional | Community adjudication often relies on hearing parties and witnesses, though procedures vary. | B/C | Serious judgement requires social verification. |

### Conclusion
This unit reinforces the information-governance strand but raises its stakes: verification is a safeguard against irreversible harm and motivated misclassification.

# E3 Unit 062 — Qur'an 4:114
## Private Deliberation Is Justified by Its Purpose

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:114 criticises much secret conversation while making explicit exceptions for deliberation directed toward charity, recognised good, or reconciliation between people.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Secrecy is not ethically neutral; its purpose matters.**
- **Private coordination is legitimate when needed for beneficence or reconciliation.**
- **Do not use hidden deliberation primarily for manipulation or wrongdoing.**
- **Confidentiality can serve ethical ends without becoming an excuse for unaccountable conduct.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 18:15; 6:3–4 | Some correction and charity are appropriately private. | A/B | Privacy can protect restoration and dignity. |
| Judaism — speech ethics and private rebuke traditions | Avoid public humiliation and harmful speech. | B | Use discretion where publicity would create needless harm. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Speech is judged by truth, benefit, timing, and intention. | B | Communication ethics depend on purpose and effect. |
| Hinduism — disciplined speech | Speech should be truthful, beneficial, and non-harmful. | B | Private or public speech should serve proper ends. |
| Sikhism — truthful speech and seva | Communication should support truth and service rather than intrigue. | B | Do not use speech to facilitate wrongdoing. |
| Confucianism — discretion and harmony | Sensitive correction may be shaped by relationship and role. | B | Deliberation should preserve propriety and constructive purpose. |
| African Traditional | Councils may deliberate confidentially in mediation or adjudication, but forms vary. | C | Confidentiality can support legitimate communal resolution. |

### Conclusion
The verse does not simply condemn secrecy. It evaluates secrecy functionally: hidden coordination is acceptable when serving charity, good conduct, or reconciliation.

# E3 Unit 063 — Qur'an 4:127
## Justice Toward Vulnerable Women and Orphans

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:127 returns to obligations concerning orphaned women and vulnerable children, condemning denial of what is prescribed for them and commanding justice toward orphans.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not use guardianship or social vulnerability to deny legitimate rights.**
- **Apply justice to people whose dependency makes enforcement difficult.**
- **The absence of power to demand one's rights does not weaken those rights.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 1:27 | Orphans and vulnerable persons receive explicit moral concern. | A/B | Protect people who lack ordinary social power. |
| Judaism — Exodus 22:22–24; Deuteronomy 24:17 | Justice must not be distorted against widow or orphan. | A | Vulnerability heightens rather than diminishes protection. |
| Buddhism — compassion and non-stealing | Vulnerable people should not be exploited. | B | Do not convert another's weakness into advantage. |
| Hinduism — protection and dana traditions | Dependants and vulnerable persons fall within duties of care. | B | Strength carries protective responsibility. |
| Sikhism — seva and defence of the vulnerable | Service and opposition to oppression are recurring duties. | B | Use strength to protect rather than exploit. |
| Confucianism — benevolent government | Humane governance attends especially to those lacking support. | B | Institutions should protect weak parties. |
| African Traditional | Extended kinship can create protective obligations toward orphaned children, though systems vary. | B/C | Community bears duties toward vulnerable dependants. |

### Conclusion
This repeats a major Qur'anic pattern: vulnerability is treated as a reason for stronger safeguards, not weaker claims.

# E3 Unit 064 — Qur'an 4:128
## Negotiated Settlement in Relationship Conflict

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:128 addresses fear of neglect or estrangement within marriage and permits mutually agreed settlement, explicitly describing reconciliation or settlement as good.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Parties in a deteriorating relationship may negotiate a mutually acceptable settlement.**
- **Compromise can be ethically preferable to unresolved hostility.**
- **Agreement should address real interests rather than preserve a failing appearance.**
- **Human self-interest is a predictable factor that negotiation must account for.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:25; 18:15 | Reconciliation and early settlement of disputes are encouraged. | A/B | Seek resolution before conflict hardens. |
| Judaism — peshara/compromise traditions | Compromise can be preferred in adjudication where appropriate. | A/B | Fair settlement can be better than prolonged dispute. |
| Buddhism — concord and dispute settlement | Reconciliation is preferable to continuing hostility. | B | Resolve disputes through peaceful agreement. |
| Hinduism — mediation and compromise traditions | Settlement is recognised within dispute resolution. | B | Use negotiated resolution where just. |
| Sikhism — reconciliation and collective deliberation | Conflict should be resolved without cultivating enmity. | B | Seek fair peace between parties. |
| Confucianism — mediation | Harmonious negotiated settlement has historically been strongly valued. | A/B | Restore workable relationship through fair mediation. |
| African Traditional | Restorative settlement through kin or elders is widespread though diverse. | A/B | Prefer relationship-restoring settlement where possible. |

### Conclusion
The source adds negotiated compromise to the Qur'anic dispute-resolution architecture, complementing earlier bilateral arbitration.

# E3 Unit 065 — Qur'an 4:129
## Fair Treatment Despite Unequal Affection

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:129 recognises that perfect emotional equality between wives may be impossible but prohibits total inclination toward one that leaves another effectively suspended.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Ethical obligation concerns controllable conduct even where feelings cannot be made equal.**
- **Unequal affection does not justify abandonment of fair treatment.**
- **Do not leave a dependent partner in prolonged relational limbo.**
- **Distinguish internal states from actions for which a person can reasonably be held responsible.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — duties of marital care | Conduct toward a spouse remains ethically constrained despite changing emotion. | B | Feelings do not erase relational duties. |
| Judaism — marital maintenance obligations | Material and relational duties can remain enforceable regardless of affection. | B | Obligation is not identical to preference. |
| Buddhism — intention and disciplined action | Mental states are recognised while conduct is trained and restrained. | B | Difficult feelings do not excuse harmful action. |
| Hinduism — duty over attachment | Dharma can require right action independent of preference. | B | Fulfil legitimate duties despite fluctuating desire. |
| Sikhism — self-mastery and marital responsibility | Personal impulse should not dominate ethical responsibility. | B | Govern conduct through duty rather than preference alone. |
| Confucianism — role obligation | Proper conduct is not reducible to spontaneous feeling. | B | Relational duties persist beyond emotion. |
| African Traditional | Household obligations may continue despite personal conflict, though structures vary. | C | Personal preference does not automatically cancel responsibility. |

### Conclusion
The unit introduces a useful responsibility distinction: ethics can demand fairness in controllable behaviour without demanding impossible equality of involuntary feeling.

# E3 Unit 066 — Qur'an 4:135
## Impartial Justice Against Self-Interest

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:135 commands standing firmly for justice and truthful testimony even when doing so is against oneself, parents, or close relatives, and warns against allowing wealth, poverty, desire, or avoidance to distort judgement.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Apply justice even when it disadvantages yourself or your allies.**
- **Kinship must not override truthful testimony.**
- **Neither wealth nor poverty should determine the justice of a case.**
- **Recognise personal desire as a source of bias.**
- **Do not distort or withhold testimony to evade justice.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 2:1–9 | Partiality based on wealth and status is condemned. | A | Do not bend judgement according to social advantage. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:15; Deuteronomy 16:19 | Judges must favour neither poor nor powerful and must not distort justice. | A | Apply law impartially. |
| Buddhism — impartiality/upeksha | Equanimity resists attachment and aversion in judgement. | B | Do not let preference corrupt moral assessment. |
| Hinduism — dharma of impartial judgement | Rulers and judges are expected to decide according to dharma rather than private attachment. | A/B | Justice outranks personal interest. |
| Sikhism — justice and equality | Status and personal advantage should not determine moral standing. | B | Stand for justice without favouritism. |
| Confucianism — righteousness over familial/private advantage, with relational complexities | Public duty can require principled conduct, though Confucian role ethics complicate direct equivalence. | B/C | Personal interest should not simply capture public judgement. |
| African Traditional | Councils often impose impartiality ideals while kinship systems vary strongly. | B/C | Adjudicators should not turn office into private favour. |

### Conclusion
This is one of the clearest Qur'anic formulations of anti-capture ethics encountered so far: justice must resist self-interest, kinship, wealth, poverty, desire, and evidentiary manipulation.

# E3 Unit 067 — Qur'an 4:140
## Do Not Legitimate Degrading Conduct Through Passive Participation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:140 instructs believers not to remain in a gathering where divine signs are being denied and mocked until the subject changes.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Participation can communicate endorsement even without direct action.**
- **Withdraw from settings where remaining would materially legitimise degrading conduct.**
- **Association creates responsibilities as well as freedoms.**
- **Non-participation can be an ethical response short of coercion.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 1 Corinthians 5:11; Ephesians 5:11 | Participation in wrongful conduct can be refused. | A/B | Do not lend participation to wrongdoing. |
| Judaism — Psalm 1:1; association ethics | Avoid joining counsel or conduct identified as wicked. | A/B | Association can shape or legitimise conduct. |
| Buddhism — avoidance of unwholesome companionship | Company affects conduct and mental formation. | B | Withdraw from influences that cultivate wrongdoing. |
| Hinduism — satsanga / avoidance of harmful company | Association influences character. | B | Choose company with ethical consequences in mind. |
| Sikhism — sangat and influence of company | Company is treated as morally formative. | B | Association can strengthen or weaken ethical life. |
| Confucianism — friendship and moral cultivation | Companions should aid moral development. | B | Do not normalise degrading conduct through company. |
| African Traditional | Communal participation often carries signalling and responsibility effects. | C | Presence can imply social recognition of conduct. |

### Conclusion
This unit extends the complicity principle from active facilitation to legitimising participation, while prescribing withdrawal rather than forced suppression.

# E3 Unit 068 — Qur'an 4:148–149
## Public Accusation, Wrongdoing, and Forgiveness

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 4:148–149 discourages public expression of harmful speech while recognising an exception for a person who has been wronged, and then praises doing good openly or privately and forgiving wrong.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not spread harmful public speech without legitimate reason.**
- **Victims retain moral space to name wrongdoing done to them.**
- **Privacy is not a rule requiring victims to conceal abuse.**
- **Forgiveness is praiseworthy but does not erase the recognition that wrongdoing occurred.**
- **Good may be done publicly or privately; context matters.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 18:15–17; Ephesians 4:31–32 | Wrongdoing can be named through escalating processes while forgiveness is encouraged. | A/B | Address wrong without making destructive speech an end in itself. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:16–18 | Tale-bearing is prohibited while rebuke and non-vengeance are also commanded. | A | Distinguish harmful gossip from legitimate confrontation of wrong. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Speech should be truthful, beneficial, timely, and non-malicious. | A/B | Truth does not eliminate responsibility for manner and purpose. |
| Hinduism — satya and ahimsa in speech | Truthfulness and non-harm jointly constrain speech. | B | Speak truth without gratuitous injury. |
| Sikhism — truthful speech, rejection of slander, forgiveness | Truth should not become malicious defamation. | B | Name wrong truthfully without cultivating hatred. |
| Confucianism — remonstrance and propriety | Legitimate correction is distinguished from uncontrolled denunciation. | B | Criticism should serve correction and proper order. |
| African Traditional | Mediation systems often distinguish legitimate complaint from destructive accusation, though practices vary. | B/C | Victims may bring grievances into recognised processes. |

### Conclusion
The source creates a nuanced speech rule: harmful publicity is constrained, but the wronged person is not ethically silenced.

# E3 Unit 070 — Qur'an 5:2
## Cooperation for Good, Non-Cooperation with Wrong, and Restraint Toward Former Opponents

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:2 contains ritual-specific provisions but also commands that hostility toward people who previously obstructed the believers must not provoke transgression, and instructs cooperation in righteousness rather than cooperation in wrongdoing and aggression.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not let prior hostility license present injustice.**
- **Cooperate with others in beneficial and righteous action.**
- **Do not cooperate in wrongdoing or aggression.**
- **The ethics of collaboration depend partly on the ends being jointly pursued.**
- **Past injury does not create unlimited permission for retaliation.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 12:17–21; Ephesians 5:11 | Do not repay evil for evil and do not participate in harmful works. | A | Refuse both retaliation and complicity in wrong. |
| Judaism — Exodus 23:4–5; Leviticus 19:18 | Duties can extend even toward an enemy; vengeance is constrained. | A | Prior hostility does not cancel ethical obligation. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 3–5 | Hatred is not ended by hatred. | A | Do not let hostility reproduce wrongdoing. |
| Hinduism — non-attachment, dharma, and disciplined action | Action should follow duty rather than personal hatred. | B | Do not make enmity the criterion of conduct. |
| Sikhism — justice without hatred; seva | Collective action should serve justice rather than oppression. | B | Cooperate for good without cultivating enmity. |
| Confucianism — righteousness and principled association | Cooperation is evaluated through righteousness, not advantage alone. | B | Do not join collective action merely because it benefits your side. |
| African Traditional | Communal cooperation and reconciliation are widespread but locally structured. | B/C | Collective action should support community rather than feud escalation. |

### Discussion
This unit contributes a particularly clear ethics of **association and collective action**. It neither treats cooperation as automatically good nor treats refusal to cooperate as automatically virtuous. The moral status of collaboration depends upon what participants are jointly enabling.

It also repeats a significant restraint principle: even remembered hostility does not justify transgression against the former opponent.

### Conclusion
The passage combines non-retaliation with a general complicity rule: **cooperate in good; withhold cooperation from wrongdoing and aggression**.

This group further develops several patterns generated by the anchor corpus.

**Information ethics** becomes more differentiated: Unit 061 requires verification before harmful action; Unit 062 distinguishes legitimate confidentiality from wrongful secrecy; Unit 068 constrains harmful public speech without silencing victims.

**Complicity** also develops beyond the earlier facilitation rule. Unit 067 concerns legitimising participation, while Unit 070 explicitly distinguishes cooperation for good from cooperation in wrongdoing.

**Justice under bias** reaches an especially explicit formulation in Unit 066. Self-interest, kinship, economic status, desire, and manipulation of testimony are all identified as pressures capable of corrupting judgement.

Finally, Unit 065 contributes a useful distinction between internal state and controllable action: ethical systems may demand fair conduct even where equal feeling cannot be commanded.
