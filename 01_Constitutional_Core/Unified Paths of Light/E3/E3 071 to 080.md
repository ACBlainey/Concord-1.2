# E3 Unit 071 — Qur'an 5:8
## Justice Toward Those One Dislikes

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:8 commands steadfast justice and explicitly warns that hatred of a people must not cause departure from justice.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Apply justice to opponents as well as allies.**
- **Hatred is a predictable source of adjudicative bias.**
- **Moral standards remain binding when the beneficiary is disliked.**
- **Fairness is tested most strongly where impartiality is personally costly.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:44; Romans 12:17–21 | Ethical duties extend toward enemies. | A/B | Hostility does not cancel moral obligation. |
| Judaism — Exodus 23:4–5; Leviticus 19:15 | Duties extend even toward an enemy; judgement must be impartial. | A | Do not let hostility distort justice. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 3–5; equanimity | Hatred should not govern response. | A | Do not reproduce hostility through biased conduct. |
| Hinduism — equanimity and dharma | Right action should not be determined by attachment or aversion. | B | Apply duty despite personal dislike. |
| Sikhism — justice without enmity | Opposition to wrongdoing need not become hatred of persons. | B | Uphold justice without vindictiveness. |
| Confucianism — righteousness over personal advantage | Judgement should follow rightness rather than private feeling. | B | Principle should constrain partiality. |
| African Traditional | Reconciliation/adjudication systems often seek to restrain feud bias, though forms vary. | B/C | Enmity should not capture judgement. |

### Conclusion
This is an unusually explicit anti-bias rule: justice is required precisely where hatred creates pressure to abandon it.

# E3 Unit 072 — Qur'an 5:13
## Forgiveness Despite Betrayal

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:13 describes betrayal within a covenantal context yet instructs pardon and overlooking.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Betrayal does not always require maximal retaliation.**
- **Forgiveness can remain ethically available after serious breach of trust.**
- **Responses to wrongdoing may distinguish protection from vengeance.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 18:21–22 | Repeated forgiveness is explicitly commended. | A | Wrongdoing need not generate endless retaliation. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:18 | Vengeance and grudge-bearing are prohibited toward one's people. | A/B | Do not perpetuate grievance indefinitely. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 3–5 | Hatred does not end through hatred. | A | Break retaliatory cycles. |
| Hinduism — forgiveness as virtue | Forbearance and forgiveness are praised. | B | Restrain vengeance after injury. |
| Sikhism — overcoming enmity | Forgiveness and absence of hatred are valued. | B | Do not let betrayal create permanent hatred. |
| Confucianism — upright response rather than vengeance | Injury should be answered through principle. | B | Respond proportionately rather than vindictively. |
| African Traditional | Restorative practices often seek reintegration after acknowledgement/repair. | B/C | Betrayal may be answered through restoration rather than endless feud. |

### Conclusion
The unit reinforces forgiveness but in the more difficult context of violated trust.

# E3 Unit 073 — Qur'an 5:32
## Sanctity of Life and the Social Significance of Saving Life

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:32, while explicitly recounting a prescription given to the Children of Israel, presents wrongful killing of one person in exceptionally grave terms and saving a life in correspondingly expansive terms.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Human life has very high moral value.**
- **Wrongful killing is a grave social and moral harm.**
- **Saving life is a major positive ethical act.**
- **The treatment of an individual life can carry significance beyond that individual case.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:21–22; Good Samaritan tradition | Killing is condemned and active preservation of endangered life is praised. | A/B | Protect life rather than destroy it. |
| Judaism — Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 and biblical homicide law | The passage has a close Jewish textual/legal parallel concerning the value of a single life. | A | A single life possesses immense moral significance. |
| Buddhism — First Precept | Taking life is prohibited. | A | Refrain from killing living beings. |
| Hinduism — ahimsa | Non-harm is a major ethical principle. | A/B | Preserve life and minimise wrongful harm. |
| Sikhism — protection of the innocent | Sikh ethics combine respect for life with defence against oppression. | B | Protect innocent life. |
| Confucianism — ren / humane government | Humane conduct rejects wanton destruction of people. | B | Human life should be treated with benevolent concern. |
| African Traditional | Life is commonly understood as deeply embedded in family/community continuity, though traditions vary. | B/C | Wrongful destruction of life damages the wider community. |

### Conclusion
Protection and preservation of life are among the strongest areas of cross-traditional convergence.

# E3 Unit 074 — Qur'an 5:38–39
## Wrongdoing, Accountability, and the Possibility of Reform

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:38–39 prescribes a specific legal punishment for theft, then immediately recognises repentance and reform after wrongdoing.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Theft is a serious violation of property and social trust.**
- **Wrongdoing can justly trigger accountability.**
- **A wrongdoer is not necessarily reducible forever to the wrong committed.**
- **Genuine reform matters after an offence.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 19:8–9 | Zacchaeus links repentance with restitution and changed conduct. | A/B | Reform should include concrete correction of wrong. |
| Judaism — Exodus 22; teshuvah | Theft triggers restitution; repentance permits moral return. | A | Accountability and reform can coexist. |
| Buddhism — karmic responsibility and ethical reform | Harmful action has consequences, but conduct and intention can change. | B | Past wrong does not prevent future ethical cultivation. |
| Hinduism — karma, expiation, and reform | Wrongdoing may require consequence and corrective practice. | B | Accountability can be followed by restoration. |
| Sikhism — transformation through truthful living | Ethical change remains possible after wrongdoing. | B | A person can turn from harmful conduct. |
| Confucianism — correction of faults | The exemplary person corrects faults rather than concealing them. | B | Moral development requires reform after error. |
| African Traditional | Restorative systems frequently combine sanction, compensation, and reintegration. | B | Accountability can aim at restored social membership. |

### Discussion
The specific corporal penalty is tradition-specific and is not converted into a universal ethic. The cross-comparative principle is the coexistence of accountability with a recognised path of reform.

### Conclusion
This unit contributes a non-permanent model of moral status: wrongdoing matters, but reform also matters.

# E3 Unit 075 — Qur'an 5:42
## Impartial Adjudication Even When Jurisdiction Is Optional

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:42 addresses cases brought for judgement and states that if judgement is undertaken, it should be rendered with justice.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Once authority accepts a dispute, it must adjudicate fairly.**
- **Discretion over whether to act does not create discretion to act unjustly.**
- **Procedural authority carries corresponding duties of impartiality.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 2:1–9 | Partiality in judgement is condemned. | A/B | Do not let status corrupt judgement. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 16:18–20 | Judges must judge justly. | A | Adjudication must be impartial. |
| Buddhism — righteous adjudication traditions | Fair and non-harmful judgement is associated with righteous rule. | B | Authority should decide without corrupt preference. |
| Hinduism — rajadharma | Judges/rulers are constrained by dharma. | A/B | Public judgement is a duty, not private privilege. |
| Sikhism — justice and equality | Legitimate authority should uphold justice. | B | Adjudicate without favouritism. |
| Confucianism — righteous government | Office requires morally responsible judgement. | B | Authority should serve right order. |
| African Traditional | Elders/courts are expected to hear disputes according to recognised norms. | B | Accepted jurisdiction entails fair hearing. |

### Conclusion
The unit reinforces the principle that authority and responsibility are inseparable.

# E3 Unit 076 — Qur'an 5:44–50
## Do Not Distort Judgement for Fear, Gain, or Preference

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:44–50 repeatedly condemns replacing or distorting revealed standards for worldly gain and culminates in a warning against following people's desires in judgement.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not sell judgement for material advantage.**
- **Do not alter standards merely to satisfy powerful interests or preferences.**
- **Adjudication should be rule-governed rather than desire-governed.**
- **A judge's fear or appetite is a corruption risk.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 2:1–9 | Social preference must not corrupt judgement. | A/B | Do not bend standards toward favoured parties. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 16:19 | Bribery and distortion of judgement are explicitly prohibited. | A | Do not corrupt adjudication for gain. |
| Buddhism — avoidance of greed, hatred, delusion in judgement | Ethical decisions should not be governed by corrupt mental roots. | B | Keep greed and aversion from determining judgement. |
| Hinduism — judicial dharma | Judges should decide according to law/dharma rather than private gain. | A/B | Rule by standard rather than appetite. |
| Sikhism — truthful and just conduct | Authority should resist corruption and falsehood. | B | Do not sell justice for advantage. |
| Confucianism — righteousness over profit | Profit should not override rightness. | A/B | Public duty outranks private gain. |
| African Traditional | Anti-bribery and impartiality expectations occur in many customary systems, though evidence is diverse. | B/C | Adjudication should not be purchased. |

### Conclusion
This sequence reinforces an anti-capture architecture: judgement is vulnerable to fear, profit, social pressure, and desire, and therefore requires standards external to the decision-maker's immediate interest.

# E3 Unit 077 — Qur'an 5:54
## Humility Toward Allies and Courage Against Hostile Pressure

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:54 praises a community characterised by humility toward fellow believers, firmness in the face of opposition, and lack of fear of hostile blame.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not dominate those within your own cooperative community.**
- **Courage may require tolerating social disapproval.**
- **Humility and firmness are compatible rather than opposites.**
- **Moral conduct should not be governed entirely by fear of criticism.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Philippians 2:3; Acts 5:29 | Humility toward others coexists with principled courage. | A/B | Be humble without surrendering moral conviction. |
| Judaism — Micah 6:8; prophetic courage | Humility and principled resistance to wrongful authority coexist. | A/B | Walk humbly while standing for right. |
| Buddhism — humility/non-attachment and steadfast practice | Ego is restrained while discipline remains firm. | B | Strength need not depend on self-exaltation. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 16:1–3 | Humility, courage, and steadfastness appear among virtues. | A/B | Combine humility with moral courage. |
| Sikhism — sant-sipahi ideal | Spiritual humility is joined to courage in defence of justice. | A/B | Be humble yet prepared to resist oppression. |
| Confucianism — junzi | The exemplary person can remain principled despite social pressure. | B | Do not exchange principle for approval. |
| African Traditional | Courage and humility are valued differently across traditions. | C | Community standing need not eliminate principled courage. |

### Conclusion
The source produces a paired virtue structure: gentleness toward one's cooperative community and firmness against coercive pressure.

# E3 Unit 078 — Qur'an 5:89
## Oaths, Accountability, and Repair

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:89 distinguishes careless oaths from deliberate oaths, imposes expiation for broken deliberate commitments, and commands care in the making and keeping of oaths.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Distinguish careless expression from deliberate commitment.**
- **Deliberately undertaken promises create accountability.**
- **When a serious commitment is broken, repair may be required.**
- **Do not make solemn commitments casually.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:33–37 | Oaths should not become a substitute for reliable ordinary truthfulness. | A/B | Make words dependable and avoid careless solemnisation. |
| Judaism — Numbers 30:2; Deuteronomy 23:21–23 | Deliberate vows are binding. | A | Honour deliberate commitments. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Speech should be intentional and truthful. | B | Do not create reliance through careless falsehood. |
| Hinduism — satya and vow traditions | Vows and truthful speech carry moral weight. | B | Deliberate promises create duties. |
| Sikhism — truthful living | Word and conduct should correspond. | B | Integrity requires reliable commitment. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthiness requires keeping one's word. | A | Commitments should be dependable. |
| African Traditional | Oaths and sworn undertakings often carry communal force. | B | Solemn promises generate responsibility. |

### Conclusion
The verse adds repair to promise-keeping: accountability after breach is not exhausted by admitting the promise was broken.

# E3 Unit 079 — Qur'an 5:90–91
## Avoid Practices That Generate Addiction, Conflict, and Impaired Judgement

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:90–91 prohibits intoxicants and gambling within a religious framework and identifies among their effects hostility, hatred, and diversion from higher responsibilities.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Avoid practices whose harms include impaired judgement and destructive dependency.**
- **Do not normalise systems that predictably generate interpersonal hostility through compulsive gain/loss cycles.**
- **Evaluate practices partly by their effects on agency, relationships, and responsibility.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Ephesians 5:18 | Drunkenness is explicitly condemned. | A | Do not surrender judgement to intoxication. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 20:1; 23:29–35 | Excessive alcohol use and its harms are warned against. | A/B | Avoid intoxication that destroys judgement. |
| Buddhism — Fifth Precept | Abstention from intoxicants causing heedlessness is explicit. | A | Protect awareness and agency from intoxication. |
| Hinduism — traditions vary regarding intoxicants and gambling; Mahabharata depicts gambling's destructive effects | Harmful loss of self-command is strongly illustrated. | B | Do not allow compulsive appetite to destroy judgement and relationships. |
| Sikhism — Sikh discipline rejects intoxicants | Intoxicating substances are prohibited in normative Sikh discipline. | A | Preserve disciplined agency. |
| Confucianism — moderation and ritual restraint | Excess that destroys proper self-command is disfavoured. | B | Appetite should remain governed. |
| African Traditional | Alcohol and gaming norms vary substantially; no pan-African prohibition can be asserted. | C/D | Evaluate specific practices within their social consequences. |

### Conclusion
Convergence is strong concerning impaired judgement from intoxication, but the precise treatment of alcohol and gambling varies significantly between traditions.

# E3 Unit 080 — Qur'an 5:100
## Quantity and Popularity Do Not Determine Moral Quality

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 5:100 states that bad and good are not equal even where the abundance of the bad is impressive or attractive.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not infer moral quality from abundance.**
- **Popularity or prevalence does not by itself make conduct good.**
- **Evaluate options by their qualities and consequences rather than simple numerical dominance.**
- **More is not automatically better.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 7:13–14 | Majority uptake is not treated as proof of rightness. | A/B | Popularity does not establish moral truth. |
| Judaism — Exodus 23:2 | One must not follow a multitude into wrongdoing. | A | Majority participation does not justify wrong. |
| Buddhism — Kalama Sutta | Mere tradition, hearsay, or social transmission is not sufficient grounds for accepting a claim; consequences matter. | A/B | Evaluate rather than merely follow prevalence. |
| Hinduism — discernment traditions | Ethical judgement requires discrimination between beneficial and harmful, not simple imitation. | B | Use discernment rather than crowd-following. |
| Sikhism — truthful living over social convention | Truth is not reducible to popular approval. | B | Social prevalence does not define rightness. |
| Confucianism — junzi versus conformity for advantage | The exemplary person follows righteousness rather than merely popular gain. | B | Do not substitute conformity for moral judgement. |
| African Traditional | Communal consensus is often important, so no uniform anti-majoritarian formulation is appropriate. | C | Community prevalence alone need not settle every moral question. |

### Discussion
This source is structurally important because it supplies an epistemic and evaluative warning against **quantity as a proxy for quality**. It does not imply that majority judgement is always wrong; it says abundance itself is insufficient evidence of goodness.

### Conclusion
The unit adds a clear anti-popularity heuristic: numerical prevalence, material abundance, or social attraction should not replace substantive evaluation.

This group contains several especially clear constraints on distorted judgement.

Unit 071 identifies **hatred** as a source of injustice. Unit 076 identifies **fear, gain, and preference** as corruption pressures. Unit 080 identifies **abundance and popularity** as unreliable proxies for moral quality.

The combined result is not yet a final theory, but the anchor corpus is independently producing a repeated requirement that decisions remain resistant to predictable distortions in human judgement.

Unit 074 also strengthens a separate pattern: accountability does not necessarily imply permanent moral exclusion. Wrongdoing can be condemned while a genuine path of reform remains open.

These observations remain provisional pending completion of the Qur'anic anchor corpus.
