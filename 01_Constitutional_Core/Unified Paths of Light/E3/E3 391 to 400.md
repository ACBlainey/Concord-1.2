# E3 Unit 391 — Qur'an 68:10–13
## Persistent Slander, Obstruction, and Aggression Are Relevant to Entrusted Influence

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 68:10–13 warns against following a person characterised by habitual oath-taking, contempt, slander, gossip, obstruction of good, aggression, and harshness.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Repeated conduct is relevant to whether a person should be trusted with influence.
- Slander and malicious information transfer are governance risks.
- Obstruction of beneficial action can itself be ethically significant.
- Status should not neutralise evidence of persistent destructive behaviour.
- Character assessment should focus on observable patterns rather than prestige.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 7:16–20 | Character is assessed by fruits or conduct. | A | Evaluate influence through behavioural evidence. |
| Judaism | Slander, false witness, and corrupt leadership are strongly constrained. | A/B | Persistent harmful conduct undermines trust. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Slander, harshness, and divisive speech are prohibited. | A | Harmful speech is relevant to character. |
| Hinduism | Truthfulness and non-harm constrain aggressive and deceptive conduct. | B | Entrusted influence requires disciplined conduct. |
| Sikhism | Slander, ego, and oppression are strongly criticised. | B | Status cannot substitute for integrity. |
| Confucianism | Office requires cultivated character and trustworthy conduct. | A/B | Role fitness includes moral character. |
| African Traditional | Leadership criteria vary. | C | Repeated antisocial behaviour can undermine legitimate trust. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Units 182 and 311.** This is recurrence evidence for trustworthy-agent selection.

# E3 Unit 392 — Qur'an 68:17–33
## Coordinated Ownership Should Not Be Used to Exclude the Vulnerable

### Qur'anic Source
The garden narrative in Qur'an 68:17–33 depicts owners planning to harvest before poor people can receive anything, followed by recognition of their error.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Collective planning can coordinate exclusion as well as cooperation.
- Property control does not eliminate wider ethical responsibility.
- Vulnerable persons can be excluded through timing and procedure rather than explicit prohibition.
- Owners should not design access rules solely to defeat social obligations.
- Recognition of wrongdoing can open a path to correction.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 12:16–21 | Hoarded abundance without wider responsibility is criticised. | A/B | Ownership does not erase duties to need. |
| Judaism — gleaning laws | Landholders must preserve access for vulnerable people. | A | Property use carries distributive duties. |
| Buddhism — dana | Hoarding and attachment conflict with generosity. | B | Do not organise resources solely around exclusion. |
| Hinduism — dana | Wealth carries duties of giving. | B | Possession should not extinguish social responsibility. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing directly counters exclusionary accumulation. | A | Share rather than close resources around owners. |
| Confucianism | Benevolence and humane government constrain elite indifference. | B | Advantage carries social responsibility. |
| African Traditional | Resource-sharing institutions vary. | B/C | Ownership can remain embedded in communal obligations. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Units 183 and 312.** Preserve as recurrence of anti-collusive exclusion.

# E3 Unit 393 — Qur'an 70:24–25
## Need Can Generate a Recognised Claim on Resources

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 70:24–25 praises people who recognise within their wealth a due share for the petitioner and the deprived.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Social provision can be framed as obligation rather than optional generosity.
- Vulnerable people who do not ask must not disappear from allocation systems.
- Need can create a legitimate claim on those with resources.
- Assistance mechanisms should not rely exclusively on successful self-advocacy.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Strong duties toward poor people converge, though rights-language varies. | B | Need creates serious obligations. |
| Judaism — tzedakah / gleaning | Assistance is strongly duty-based. | A | Vulnerable persons have recognised material claims. |
| Buddhism — dana | Giving is central but usually framed through giver practice rather than recipient right. | B | Resources should reach need. |
| Hinduism — dana | Giving is strongly valued and often duty-like. | B | Wealth carries distributive obligations. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing earnings is integral to ethical life. | A/B | Build sharing into ordinary resource use. |
| Confucianism | Humane government bears responsibility for subsistence. | B | Social order includes material provision. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocal entitlements vary. | B/C | Need can generate communal claims. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Units 184 and 313.** Preserve the rights-like formulation.

# E3 Unit 396 — Qur'an 76:8–9

- Assistance can cross adversarial or captive status.

- Vulnerability should not be exploited for reputational gain.
- Non-transactional support protects recipient agency.

# E3 Unit 397 — Qur'an 83:1–3
## Reciprocal Standards Must Apply to What One Gives and Receives

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 83:1–3 condemns demanding full measure when receiving while giving less when measuring for others.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- One standard for self and another for others is structurally unfair.
- Reciprocity requires symmetry in claims and duties.
- Measurement systems should prevent self-serving asymmetry.
- Commercial ethics can generalise into rule-design ethics: standards should work in both directions.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 7:12 | Reciprocal standards strongly converge. | A | Apply to others the standard claimed for oneself. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 25:13–16 | Unequal weights are explicitly prohibited. | A | Do not manipulate reciprocal standards. |
| Buddhism | Non-stealing and right livelihood oppose asymmetric extraction. | B | Do not take more than one fairly gives. |
| Hinduism | Honest exchange strongly converges. | A/B | Maintain symmetry in exchange. |
| Sikhism | Honest earning opposes exploitative standards. | B | Do not profit through asymmetry. |
| Confucianism — reciprocity | Reciprocal moral reasoning is central. | A | Standards should operate both ways. |
| African Traditional | Exchange norms vary. | B/C | Fair exchange requires consistent standards. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Units 189 and 319.**

### Qur'anic Source

- Inheritance can become a site of exploitation.
- Communities bear responsibility not only to give but to encourage provision.

- Protection of low-power persons is a test of economic legitimacy.
