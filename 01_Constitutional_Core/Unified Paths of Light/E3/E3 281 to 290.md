# E3 Unit 281 — Qur'an 28:77
## Use Resources for Good Without Neglecting Legitimate Worldly Needs

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 28:77 instructs the possessor of resources to seek higher good through what has been given while not forgetting a legitimate share of worldly life, to do good, and not to seek corruption.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Resource ethics need not demand total self-denial.
- Personal benefit and social responsibility can coexist.
- Wealth should be directed toward constructive ends rather than corruption.
- Sustainable ethics integrates present life with wider obligation.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Stewardship and generosity coexist with legitimate provision for household needs. | B | Use resources responsibly without treating all possession as wrong. |
| Judaism | Material life and charity obligations are integrated. | A/B | Enjoy legitimate goods while fulfilling duties. |
| Buddhism — Middle Way | Rejects both indulgence and destructive self-denial. | A | Balance use with restraint. |
| Hinduism — householder dharma | Material responsibility can coexist with spiritual duty. | A/B | Integrate worldly provision with ethical purpose. |
| Sikhism — householder ideal | Honest earning, sharing, and ordinary life are integrated. | A | Use resources without renouncing responsibility or service. |
| Confucianism | Material welfare is legitimate when subordinated to righteousness. | B | Prosperity should remain ethically governed. |
| African Traditional | Material and communal obligations vary but are often integrated. | B | Personal resources exist within wider social responsibility. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with equilibrium and stewardship cluster.**

# E3 Unit 282 — Qur'an 29:8
## Family Loyalty Has Ethical Limits

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 29:8 commands good treatment of parents while refusing obedience where they pressure a person toward a fundamental violation of conscience.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Respect does not require unlimited obedience.
- Relational duties can conflict with higher-order ethical commitments.
- Boundary-setting need not erase continued care.
- Authority derived from family relationship is real but bounded.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Acts 5:29 / family teachings | Higher moral obligation can override human command. | A/B | Respect authority without granting unlimited obedience. |
| Judaism | Parental honour does not require violation of higher law. | A | Family authority is bounded. |
| Buddhism | Family duties coexist with independent ethical responsibility. | B | Kinship does not erase moral agency. |
| Hinduism | Family dharma is strong but exists within wider dharma. | B | Role obligations can conflict and require hierarchy. |
| Sikhism | Family respect does not override commitment to truth. | B | Loyalty has principled limits. |
| Confucianism | Filial duty is strong, but remonstrance can be required. | A/B | Respectful disagreement can coexist with filial duty. |
| African Traditional | Kin authority varies widely. | C | Relational authority need not be absolute. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a strong bounded-authority principle.

# E3 Unit 283 — Qur'an 29:46
## Disagree With Other Communities in the Best Manner

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 29:46 directs argument with People of the Book in the best manner, while distinguishing those engaged in wrongdoing.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Intergroup disagreement should default to respectful discourse.
- Shared ground can be acknowledged without erasing real differences.
- Conduct can matter more than category when deciding how to engage.
- Civility is compatible with boundaries against active wrongdoing.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 1 Peter 3:15 | Give reasons with gentleness and respect. | A | Disagree without contempt. |
| Judaism | Structured argument and debate provide functional parallels. | B | Disagreement can remain relational. |

| Hinduism | Formal debate traditions distinguish constructive and destructive argument. | B | Use disciplined disagreement. |
| Sikhism | Respect for other faiths and truthful dialogue provide strong convergence. | B | Preserve conviction and respect together. |
| Confucianism | Harmony without sameness provides a strong conceptual parallel. | A/B | Difference need not become enmity. |
| African Traditional | Intergroup dialogue traditions vary. | C | Maintain workable relations across difference. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with Unit 247 and pluralism cluster.**

# E3 Unit 284 — Qur'an 31:14–15
## Care for Parents While Preserving Conscience

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 31:14–15 joins gratitude and care toward parents with a refusal to obey them in a fundamental violation, while still requiring good worldly companionship.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Disagreement need not cancel care.
- Authority can be bounded without becoming contempt.
- Conscience and relationship can be preserved simultaneously.
- Mature autonomy includes maintaining humane conduct toward people whose demands one rejects.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Family respect is important but subordinate to ultimate moral commitment. | B | Reject wrongful demands without abandoning humane treatment. |
| Judaism | Parental honour is bounded by higher law. | A | Respect and principled refusal can coexist. |
| Buddhism | Compassion can continue despite disagreement. | B | Maintain care while preserving ethical agency. |
| Hinduism | Role duties can conflict within wider dharma. | B | Balance filial duty with higher obligation. |
| Sikhism | Truth commitment and family responsibility can coexist. | B | Conscience does not require hostility. |
| Confucianism — remonstrance | A child may correct a parent respectfully. | A | Filial respect includes principled disagreement. |
| African Traditional | Kinship authority varies. | C | Relationship can persist despite bounded refusal. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with Unit 282.** The explicit continuation of good companionship after refusal is especially useful.

# E3 Unit 285 — Qur'an 31:18–19
## Humility Includes Voice, Bearing, and Social Presence

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 31:18–19 warns against contemptuous social bearing, arrogant walking, and unnecessarily loud speech.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Humility is behavioural, not merely internal.
- Status signalling can degrade others without explicit insult.
- Volume and physical bearing can become tools of domination.
- Civil interaction benefits from proportionate presence and speech.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Philippians 2:3 | Humility and regard for others are explicit. | A | Do not perform superiority. |
| Judaism — Micah 6:8 | Humble conduct is strongly valued. | A | Walk humbly. |
| Buddhism | Ego restraint and right speech strongly converge. | A/B | Do not use self-display to dominate. |
| Hinduism — humility / disciplined speech | Strong functional convergence. | A/B | Restrain pride in speech and bearing. |
| Sikhism — rejection of ego | Haumai is a central ethical/spiritual problem. | A | Resist status-driven self-exaltation. |
| Confucianism — propriety/modesty | Controlled speech and bearing are central to cultivated conduct. | A | Social presence should be proportionate. |
| African Traditional | Etiquette and elder/status conventions vary. | C | Social bearing is ethically regulated. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain/merge with humility cluster.** It usefully makes embodied dominance visible as an ethical variable.

# E3 Unit 286 — Qur'an 33:53
## Hospitality Does Not Cancel the Host's Privacy

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 33:53 includes historically specific household rules but contains a separable ethic: guests should not enter prematurely, linger unnecessarily after a meal, or impose on a host who may be reluctant to ask them to leave.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Hospitality does not create unlimited access.
- Guests have duties to notice burdens they impose.
- Politeness can prevent hosts from expressing discomfort directly, so guests should not exploit silence.
- Consent and boundaries remain relevant after an invitation has served its purpose.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — hospitality traditions | Hospitality is valued, but guests remain subject to relational consideration. | B | Invitation is not unlimited entitlement. |
| Judaism — hospitality etiquette | Host and guest duties are both recognised. | B | Guests should not exploit hospitality. |
| Buddhism — monastic/lay hospitality rules | Guests and monastics are subject to etiquette and burden limits. | B | Receive hospitality considerately. |
| Hinduism — guest ethics | Hospitality is strong, accompanied by reciprocal propriety. | B | Guest status carries responsibilities. |
| Sikhism | Hospitality and communal eating imply reciprocal respect. | B | Shared provision should not be exploited. |
| Confucianism — li | Guest-host relations are highly structured by propriety. | A/B | Invitation remains bounded by role and context. |
| African Traditional | Hospitality norms are strong in many societies but diverse. | B | Guests should respect household boundaries. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** It adds a subtle consent principle: permission can be purpose-limited rather than indefinite.

# E3 Unit 287 — Qur'an 33:58
## Do Not Harm People Through Unsupported Accusation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 33:58 condemns harming believing men and women for things they did not commit, characterising such conduct as slander and manifest wrongdoing.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- False attribution can itself be a serious harm.
- Reputation deserves protection from invented wrongdoing.
- Burdening innocent people with suspicion is not ethically neutral.
- Information systems should distinguish allegation from established fact.

### Comparative Analysis

|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — false witness prohibition | Directly equivalent. | A | Do not falsely accuse. |
| Judaism — Exodus 20:16 | False witness is prohibited. | A | Protect people from fabricated guilt. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | False harmful speech is prohibited. | A | Do not use falsehood to injure. |
| Hinduism — satya | Truthfulness directly constrains false accusation. | A | Attribute conduct truthfully. |
| Sikhism | Truthful living and rejection of slander converge strongly. | A/B | Do not damage others through false claims. |
| Confucianism — xin | Trustworthy speech is foundational. | B | Speech should correspond to reality. |
| African Traditional | Defamation norms vary. | C | False accusation damages social standing and trust. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: merge with Units 153, 186, 213, and 267.**

# E3 Unit 288 — Qur'an 39:18
## Listen Broadly and Follow the Best-Supported Course

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 39:18 praises those who listen to what is said and follow the best of it.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Exposure to information should precede selective judgement.
- Listening does not require accepting every claim.
- Discernment requires comparison among alternatives.
- Good reasoning is neither closed-mindedness nor indiscriminate acceptance.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|

| Buddhism — Kalama Sutta | Claims should be examined rather than accepted solely on authority. | A | Evaluate before accepting. |
| Hinduism | Philosophical debate and pramana traditions strongly support discernment. | A/B | Compare claims using valid means of knowledge. |
| Sikhism | Reflective listening and truthful discernment provide convergence. | B | Hear and distinguish truth from error. |
| Confucianism — learning/reflection | Broad learning must be joined to reflection. | A/B | Listen, compare, and judge. |
| African Traditional | Deliberative traditions vary. | C | Good judgement can require hearing multiple voices. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a strong plural-input epistemic principle.

### Qur'anic Source

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Response strategy can change the future relationship.
- Mirroring harm is not the only available option.

# E3 Unit 290 — Qur'an 42:38
## Collective Affairs Should Include Consultation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 42:38 praises a community whose affairs are conducted through mutual consultation.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- Collective decisions should not depend solely on unilateral preference.
- People affected by shared affairs should have structured opportunities for input.
- Consultation is a governance process, not merely a personal virtue.
- Distributed information can improve collective judgement.
- Consultation does not by itself specify a complete constitutional system, but it constrains pure autocracy.

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Acts 15 | Major communal disputes are addressed through collective deliberation. | A/B | Shared affairs can require deliberation. |
| Judaism — council/rabbinic traditions | Collective legal deliberation is deeply developed. | A/B | Governance benefits from multiple informed voices. |
| Buddhism — sangha procedures | Communal decisions use deliberative procedures. | A | Shared affairs require process. |
| Hinduism — assemblies/councils | Historical traditions include deliberative institutions. | B | Authority can incorporate counsel. |
| Sikhism — sarbat khalsa / collective deliberation | Strong historical functional parallel. | A/B | Community affairs can be decided collectively. |
| Confucianism — ministerial remonstrance | Consultation is valued though not egalitarian democracy. | B | Rulers should receive informed counsel. |
| African Traditional | Consensus councils are prominent in many, though not all, societies. | B | Collective affairs often require deliberation. |

### Conclusion
**Audit disposition: retain.** This is a major governance principle and should be explicitly represented in final synthesis.
