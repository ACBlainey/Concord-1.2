# E3 Unit 161 — Qur'an 42:37
## Control Anger and Forgive

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 42:37 praises those who avoid grave wrongdoing and who forgive when angered.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Anger need not determine action.**
- **Forgiveness is especially meaningful when one has actually been provoked.**
- **Emotional experience and chosen conduct should be distinguished.**
- **Self-command can prevent temporary anger from becoming lasting harm.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Ephesians 4:26, 31–32 | Anger is distinguished from sinful action and forgiveness is required. | A | Do not let anger become destructive conduct. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 16:32 | Self-control is praised above conquest. | A/B | Govern anger rather than be governed by it. |
| Buddhism — restraint of anger | Anger is a major unwholesome state to be overcome. | A | Do not act from hatred. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 2:62–63 | Anger is portrayed as a pathway to destructive loss of judgement. | A | Interrupt anger before it captures reason. |
| Sikhism — krodh | Anger is one of the major vices to overcome. | A | Restrain anger. |
| Confucianism — self-mastery | Emotion should be expressed in proper measure. | B | Do not let anger destroy propriety. |
| African Traditional | Anger-management and reconciliation norms vary. | B/C | Control anger to preserve relationship and order. |

### Conclusion
Anger regulation is a strong cross-traditional convergence point.

# E3 Unit 162 — Qur'an 47:4
## Restraint and Release After Active Combat

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 47:4 addresses active battle and then, once opponents are subdued and captured, shifts to restraint, allowing release either graciously or through ransom until the war lays down its burdens.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Rules governing active combat should not continue unchanged after an opponent is captured.**
- **A person who is no longer fighting should enter a different moral category.**
- **Captivity should have pathways toward release.**
- **Victory does not create unlimited permission over defeated persons.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — later just-war/humanitarian traditions | Persons hors de combat receive distinct protection in developed Christian ethics. | B | Defeated combatants remain human beings with claims to restraint. |
| Judaism — warfare laws | Biblical and later traditions contain constraints, though contexts differ substantially. | C | Combat status can affect permissible treatment. |
| Buddhism | Strong non-harm commitments provide general restraint but no close canonical prisoner-of-war analogue. | C | Reduce harm once threat has ended. |
| Hinduism — dharma-yuddha traditions | Epic norms distinguish combatants and constrain treatment of defeated opponents. | B | Do not continue combat against those no longer fighting. |
| Sikhism — disciplined warfare traditions | Historical codes emphasise restraint toward noncombatants and defeated persons. | B | Martial necessity does not erase humane limits. |
| Confucianism — humane warfare ideals | Benevolent rulers should avoid cruelty after victory. | B | Victory should not become licence for abuse. |
| African Traditional | Captivity practices varied enormously. | D/C | No responsible universal equivalence asserted. |

### Conclusion
The transferable principle is status transition: once immediate combat ceases, the ethical rules governing treatment should change accordingly.

# E3 Unit 163 — Qur'an 49:6
## Verify Reports Before Acting on Them

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:6 instructs verification when consequential information arrives from an unreliable source, lest people be harmed in ignorance and regret follow.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Source reliability matters.**
- **Verify consequential reports before acting.**
- **False information becomes especially dangerous when converted rapidly into coercive action.**
- **Procedural delay can be protective when evidence is uncertain.**
- **Regret after irreversible harm is inferior to verification beforehand.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 1 Thessalonians 5:21; Proverbs 18:17 | Claims should be tested and competing evidence heard. | A | Verify before concluding. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 13:14; 17:4 | Investigation and diligent inquiry precede serious judgement. | A | Investigate consequential reports carefully. |
| Buddhism — Kalama Sutta | Do not accept claims merely on report. | A | Examine information before relying on it. |
| Hinduism — pramana traditions | Reliable knowledge requires valid means. | A/B | Source and evidence quality matter. |
| Sikhism — truthful living | False reports should not govern action. | B | Act on truth rather than rumour. |
| Confucianism — investigation | Learning and judgement require careful examination. | B | Verify before acting. |
| African Traditional | Testimony and council procedures vary, but serious claims commonly undergo social verification. | B/C | Consequential action should not rest on unchecked report. |

### Conclusion
This is one of E3's clearest information-governance principles and has direct institutional applicability.

# E3 Unit 164 — Qur'an 49:9–10
## Intervene in Conflict Impartially and Restore Peace

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:9–10 instructs intervention when two groups fight, calls for resistance against the party that persists in aggression, and requires fair reconciliation once aggression stops.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not treat violent internal conflict as ethically neutral.**
- **Mediation should distinguish aggression rather than impose false equivalence.**
- **Once aggression ceases, restoration should return to fairness rather than revenge.**
- **Peacebuilding may require both restraint and enforcement.**
- **Reconciliation should aim at restored relationship, not merely temporary silence.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:9; 18:15–17 | Peacemaking and structured conflict correction are valued. | B | Intervene toward just reconciliation. |
| Judaism — pursuit of peace and adjudication | Peace and justice are jointly valued. | B | Reconcile without abandoning justice. |
| Buddhism — sangha dispute procedures | Formal procedures exist for resolving communal conflict. | A/B | Restore concord through fair process. |
| Hinduism — mediation and dharmic adjudication | Conflict settlement should restore rightful order. | B | Peace should be compatible with justice. |
| Sikhism — defence against aggression plus reconciliation | Oppression may be resisted without making perpetual hostility the goal. | B | Stop aggression, then restore peace fairly. |
| Confucianism — harmony and rectification | Harmony should rest on proper relations rather than conceal disorder. | B | Correct disorder to restore genuine harmony. |
| African Traditional | Mediation, councils, compensation, and reconciliation are prominent in many traditions. | A/B | Resolve conflict through restoration and recognised justice. |

### Conclusion
The source offers a sophisticated peace rule: impartiality does not mean ignoring which party is continuing aggression.

# E3 Unit 165 — Qur'an 49:11
## No Ridicule, Degrading Labels, or Status Humiliation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:11 prohibits groups from ridiculing other groups, warns that the mocked may be better than the mockers, and prohibits insulting or degrading labels.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not humiliate people for group entertainment or status.**
- **Mockery is an unreliable judgement of worth.**
- **Degrading labels can become instruments of social exclusion.**
- **Do not assume visible status reveals moral quality.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:22; James 3 | Contemptuous speech is morally serious. | A/B | Do not degrade others through speech. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19:17–18; humiliation ethics | Public humiliation and harmful speech are strongly constrained. | A/B | Protect others from degrading treatment. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Harsh and harmful speech should be avoided. | A | Do not use speech to humiliate. |
| Hinduism — non-injurious speech | Speech should not unnecessarily agitate or injure. | B | Avoid degrading communication. |
| Sikhism — equality and rejection of slander | Social hierarchy and insulting speech conflict with equal dignity. | A/B | Do not establish superiority through humiliation. |
| Confucianism — respect and propriety | Social interaction should preserve appropriate dignity. | B | Do not use ridicule to destroy respect. |
| African Traditional | Ridicule can have both corrective and harmful roles depending on context; no uniform rule. | C | Avoid humiliation that fractures communal dignity. |

### Conclusion
The verse directly addresses status aggression performed through language.

# E3 Unit 166 — Qur'an 49:12
## Avoid Suspicion, Spying, and Backbiting

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:12 warns against excessive suspicion, prohibits spying, and condemns backbiting.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not convert unsupported suspicion into assumed guilt.**
- **Respect informational privacy.**
- **Do not seek private information without sufficient justification.**
- **Do not damage absent people through corrosive speech.**
- **Privacy, epistemic restraint, and reputation are interconnected.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 7:1–5; James 4:11 | Rash judgement and speaking against others are constrained. | A/B | Do not presume guilt or malign others. |
| Judaism — lashon hara and privacy traditions | Harmful speech and improper intrusion are strongly regulated. | A | Protect reputation and private life. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Slander and divisive speech are prohibited. | A | Do not harm absent people through speech. |
| Hinduism — truthful/non-harmful speech | Harmful gossip conflicts with disciplined speech. | B | Avoid corrosive talk and baseless judgement. |
| Sikhism — rejection of slander | Backbiting conflicts with truthful living. | A/B | Do not degrade others behind their backs. |
| Confucianism — careful judgement and speech | The cultivated person avoids reckless accusation. | B | Restrain suspicion and gossip. |
| African Traditional | Privacy and gossip norms vary substantially. | C | Unchecked suspicion and reputational speech can fracture community. |

### Conclusion
This unit is unusually comprehensive in linking three stages of an information-harm pipeline: suspicion, intrusive acquisition, and harmful dissemination.

# E3 Unit 167 — Qur'an 49:13
## Human Equality Across Peoples and Tribes

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 49:13 addresses humanity as originating from a common male and female and describes peoples and tribes as existing for mutual recognition rather than inherent superiority, with moral quality—not lineage—as the relevant measure before God.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Ethnic, tribal, or inherited identity does not establish intrinsic human superiority.**
- **Difference can serve recognition rather than domination.**
- **Shared human origin undercuts claims of absolute lineage superiority.**
- **Moral evaluation should focus on conduct and character rather than ancestry.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Galatians 3:28; Acts 17:26 | Shared humanity relativises ethnic and social hierarchy. | A | Ancestry does not determine fundamental human worth. |
| Judaism — Genesis 1:27; common descent traditions | All humanity shares creation in the divine image, alongside particular covenantal identity. | A/B | Common humanity precedes many social distinctions. |
| Buddhism — anti-caste teachings | Spiritual/moral worth is not determined by birth. | A | Birth does not determine ethical worth. |
| Hinduism | Traditions contain both hierarchical and egalitarian strands; direct equivalence is therefore mixed. | C | Some philosophical schools relativise inherited status. |
| Sikhism — explicit rejection of caste hierarchy | Human equality is strongly affirmed. | A | Birth does not establish superior worth. |
| Confucianism — common moral potential | Moral cultivation matters more than inherited nobility in important strands. | B | Character outranks ancestry. |
| African Traditional | Strong lineage identities coexist with traditions of shared personhood; no single continental rule. | C | Group difference need not imply absolute human hierarchy. |

### Conclusion
This is one of the Qur'an's clearest anti-hereditary-superiority principles.

# E3 Unit 168 — Qur'an 53:32
## Do Not Claim Moral Purity for Yourself

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 53:32 warns people not to declare themselves pure or morally superior.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not appoint yourself as the final judge of your own virtue.**
- **Moral confidence should remain compatible with self-scrutiny.**
- **Public claims of purity can conceal blind spots and hypocrisy.**
- **Humility is epistemically relevant because people have incomplete access even to their own motives and effects.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 18:9–14 | Self-righteousness is directly criticised. | A | Do not equate self-approval with righteousness. |
| Judaism — humility traditions | Self-exaltation is repeatedly restrained. | A/B | Leave room for self-correction. |
| Buddhism — conceit | Claims of superiority are obstacles to development. | A | Do not build identity around moral superiority. |
| Hinduism — humility | Absence of pride is a virtue. | A/B | Cultivate humility regarding one's virtue. |
| Sikhism — rejection of haumai | Egoic self-exaltation is strongly criticised. | A | Do not make virtue an instrument of ego. |
| Confucianism — self-examination | The cultivated person continually examines faults. | A/B | Moral development requires self-scrutiny. |
| African Traditional | Humility before community and elders is common but varied. | B | Do not assume one's own judgement exhausts moral evaluation. |

### Conclusion
The verse adds an anti-self-certification rule: ethical systems should preserve mechanisms for correction even when actors believe themselves righteous.

# E3 Unit 169 — Qur'an 55:7–9
## Maintain Fair Balance and Do Not Manipulate Measures

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 55:7–9 invokes balance and commands that people not transgress it, requiring just weight without deficiency.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Shared measures should remain fair.**
- **Do not manipulate standards to obtain hidden advantage.**
- **Balance functions as both a moral and transactional ideal.**
- **Fair systems require reliable calibration rather than actor-specific standards.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Leviticus 19:35–36 | Honest weights and measures are required. | A | Use fair measurement. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 25:13–16 | Unequal weights are prohibited. | A | Do not manipulate standards. |
| Buddhism — Right Livelihood | Deceptive trade is constrained. | B | Do not profit through hidden distortion. |
| Hinduism — commercial dharma | False measures are condemned. | A/B | Exchange requires reliable standards. |
| Sikhism — honest earning | Wealth should not be acquired through fraud. | B | Earn honestly. |
| Confucianism — trustworthiness | Shared standards support trustworthy exchange. | B | Fairness requires reliable measures. |
| African Traditional | Measurement systems and market rules vary. | B/C | Shared exchange standards should not be manipulated. |

### Conclusion
Another repeated economic-integrity source strengthens frequency evidence without creating a separate final principle.

# E3 Unit 170 — Qur'an 57:7, 10–11
## Wealth as Stewardship Rather Than Absolute Possession

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 57 repeatedly urges spending from resources over which humans have been made successors or trustees and praises voluntary contribution.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Possession can be understood as stewardship rather than unlimited moral entitlement.**
- **Resources held by one generation are embedded in wider temporal and social systems.**
- **Material control creates opportunities and responsibilities for beneficial use.**
- **Generosity can be understood as responsible deployment of entrusted resources.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — stewardship parables | Property is often framed as entrusted rather than metaphysically absolute. | A/B | Use resources as a responsible steward. |
| Judaism — land and wealth obligations | Ownership is constrained by sabbatical, gleaning, and charity duties. | A/B | Possession carries obligations beyond the possessor. |
| Buddhism — non-attachment and dana | Wealth is impermanent and can be used generously. | B | Do not treat possession as absolute identity or entitlement. |
| Hinduism — dana and trusteeship-like interpretations | Wealth is subject to dharmic use. | B | Resources should serve legitimate duties. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Earn honestly and share what is held. | A/B | Possession creates sharing responsibilities. |
| Confucianism — righteous use of wealth | Wealth is subordinate to righteousness. | B | Material control remains morally conditioned. |
| African Traditional | Communal and lineage claims often qualify individual control of resources. | B | Resource possession is embedded in reciprocal obligations. |

### Conclusion
The source contributes a stewardship model that limits the idea of ownership as unrestricted moral sovereignty.
