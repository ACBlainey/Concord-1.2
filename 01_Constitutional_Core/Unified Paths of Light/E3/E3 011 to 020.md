## Introduction to Units 011–020

This file continues the Qur'an-first extraction in canonical order. Source units are admitted because they contain separable ethical instruction, not because they reproduce principles identified in the earlier Psalms study.

Evidence grades remain:

- **A — Direct textual equivalent**
- **B — Strong functional equivalent**
- **C — Inferred / corollary equivalent**
- **D — No clear equivalent identified**

The comparison seeks functional ethical equivalence while preserving important differences of context, theology, law, and social structure.

# E3 Unit 011 — Qur'an 2:195

## Ethics of Generosity and Avoiding Self-Destructive Conduct

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:195 instructs spending for a good cause, warns against contributing to one's own destruction, and commands excellence in conduct.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Use resources constructively** | Direct material resources toward beneficial purposes rather than selfish retention alone. |
| **Avoid self-destructive conduct** | Do not knowingly pursue conduct that brings serious avoidable harm upon yourself or your community. |
| **Practise excellence in doing good** | Ethical conduct should aim beyond minimal compliance toward active beneficence. |

### Comparative Analysis — Avoid Self-Destructive Conduct

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — 1 Corinthians 6:19–20; prudential teachings | The body and life are treated as entrusted goods rather than things to abuse recklessly. | B | Do not treat your own life and body with destructive disregard. |
| **Judaism** — Deuteronomy 4:9, 4:15; preservation-of-life tradition | Guarding oneself and preserving life are established duties. | B | Take reasonable care to preserve life and wellbeing. |
| **Buddhism** — Middle Way and first precept | Avoid extremes and conduct producing needless suffering or destruction. | B | Do not cultivate harmful conduct toward self or others. |
| **Hinduism** — ahimsa and disciplines of bodily restraint | Non-harm and self-discipline apply to one's conduct and treatment of embodied life. | B | Avoid needless harm and destructive excess. |
| **Sikhism** — disciplined living and rejection of self-destructive vice | Ethical life requires mastery of impulses that degrade the person. | B | Do not surrender agency to destructive appetites. |
| **Confucianism** — Analects and filial duties concerning preservation of the body | One's embodied life is received through family and should not be treated carelessly. | B | Exercise responsible care over oneself. |
| **African Traditional** — communal conceptions of personhood and life | Reckless self-destruction also damages family and community obligations. | C | Personal preservation carries communal significance. |

### Discussion

The verse joins expenditure, preservation, and beneficent conduct. The precise meaning of the warning is context-sensitive, but the ethically conservative extraction is that one should not knowingly bring ruin upon oneself through wrongful or reckless conduct.

### Conclusion

Responsible self-preservation has broad functional parallels, while the verse also links ethical responsibility to constructive use of resources.

# E3 Unit 012 — Qur'an 2:204–206

## Ethics of Sincerity, Destructive Conduct, and Responsiveness to Correction

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:204–206 describes a person whose attractive speech conceals hostility, who spreads corruption and destruction, and whose pride prevents correction.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Judge character by conduct as well as speech** | Persuasive moral language does not compensate for destructive behaviour. |
| **Do not spread corruption or destroy livelihoods and life** | Avoid conduct that deliberately damages social and material conditions needed for flourishing. |
| **Accept legitimate moral correction** | Pride should not prevent a person from reconsidering wrongdoing. |

### Comparative Analysis — Conduct Over Persuasive Appearance

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 7:15–20 | People are to be recognised by their fruits rather than appearances alone. | A | Evaluate moral claims through resulting conduct. |
| **Judaism** — prophetic criticism of hypocrisy | Religious or moral profession cannot substitute for just conduct. | B | Moral language without moral action is insufficient. |
| **Buddhism** — Dhammapada 9–10, 19–20 | External religious appearance or recitation without corresponding conduct is empty. | A | Practice matters more than moral appearance. |
| **Hinduism** — Bhagavad Gita 3:6 | Outward restraint combined with inward fixation is condemned as hypocrisy. | A | External moral presentation should correspond to inner and actual conduct. |
| **Sikhism** — Guru Granth Sahib's criticism of empty religious display | External piety without truthful living lacks moral value. | A | Judge spirituality through lived conduct. |
| **Confucianism** — Analects, emphasis on sincerity and conduct | Fine words without corresponding virtue are treated with suspicion. | B | Speech should be supported by character. |
| **African Traditional** — proverbial ethics concerning deeds and communal reputation | Character is established through conduct within the community. | B | Actions provide stronger evidence of character than claims. |

### Discussion

This unit closely resembles a recurring cross-traditional suspicion of hypocrisy. The Qur'anic passage adds a civic dimension: concealed hostility becomes ethically significant because it results in destructive conduct, and pride then blocks correction.

### Conclusion

The priority of conduct over persuasive moral appearance is strongly convergent across the comparison traditions.

# E3 Unit 013 — Qur'an 2:215

## Ethics of Directed Generosity

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:215 directs charitable expenditure toward parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, and travellers in need.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Generosity should meet real need** | Direct resources toward people who require support. |
| **Family obligations matter** | Charity does not erase duties toward parents and close relatives. |
| **Protect socially vulnerable people** | Orphans, poor people, and travellers without resources deserve assistance. |

### Comparative Analysis — Direct Resources Toward Those in Need

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 25:35–40; James 1:27 | Feeding, clothing, welcoming, and caring for vulnerable people are explicit duties. | A | Use resources to assist people in concrete need. |
| **Judaism** — Deuteronomy 15:7–11; 24:17–22 | The poor, stranger, widow, and orphan receive explicit material protection. | A | Do not harden yourself against vulnerable people. |
| **Buddhism** — dana and Sigalovada Sutta | Generosity and support for family and dependants are established virtues. | B | Share resources and fulfil relational duties. |
| **Hinduism** — dana traditions and duties of hospitality | Giving to those in need and caring for dependants are meritorious duties. | B | Wealth carries obligations of generosity. |
| **Sikhism** — vand chhakna and seva | Share what one has and serve others. | A | Resources should be shared rather than hoarded. |
| **Confucianism** — benevolence and family responsibility | Care begins in family relations and extends through humane social responsibility. | B | Material and relational duties begin with those for whom one bears responsibility. |
| **African Traditional** — extended-family reciprocity and communal support | Resources are commonly embedded within obligations to kin and vulnerable community members. | B | Prosperity carries reciprocal communal duties. |

### Discussion

This source overlaps earlier Unit 002 but is retained because source frequency and principle identity are separately tracked. It gives a particularly explicit hierarchy of recipients while maintaining a broad principle of need-directed generosity.

### Conclusion

Material care for kin and vulnerable people is one of the strongest areas of cross-traditional ethical convergence.

# E3 Unit 014 — Qur'an 2:219–220

## Ethics of Social Harm, Surplus Giving, and Care for Orphans

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:219–220 weighs intoxicants and gambling in terms of harm and benefit, addresses what should be spent, and instructs that improving the condition of orphans is good while warning against corruption of their interests.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Assess practices by harms as well as benefits** | A practice can contain benefits yet remain ethically problematic when its harms are greater. |
| **Give from what is beyond genuine need** | Surplus resources can be directed toward others rather than indefinite accumulation. |
| **Protect orphan interests** | Those controlling the affairs of vulnerable children should improve rather than exploit their condition. |
| **Distinguish reform from corruption** | Claimed guardianship should be evaluated by whether it actually benefits the vulnerable person. |

### Comparative Analysis — Protect Orphans from Exploitation

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — James 1:27 | Care for orphans in distress is explicitly commended. | A | Vulnerable children deserve active protection. |
| **Judaism** — Exodus 22:22–24 | Mistreatment of orphans and widows is explicitly prohibited. | A | Do not exploit those lacking ordinary protection. |
| **Buddhism** — compassion and duties toward dependants | Compassionate protection extends to vulnerable persons, though no identical orphan rule is central. | B | Vulnerability increases the duty of compassionate care. |
| **Hinduism** — dana and protective duties toward dependants | Ethical traditions commend support for vulnerable and dependent persons. | B | Those with resources should protect dependants rather than exploit them. |
| **Sikhism** — seva and defence of the vulnerable | Service and protection of those in need are important ethical themes. | B | Strength and resources should support vulnerable people. |
| **Confucianism** — Mencian benevolent government | Humane social order includes care for people lacking family support. | B | Society should protect people whose ordinary support structures are weak. |
| **African Traditional** — extended kinship care | Orphaned children are commonly incorporated into wider kin/community obligations. | B | Care of vulnerable children is a communal responsibility. |

### Discussion

This unit is ethically dense. Its treatment of intoxicants and gambling uses an explicit harm-benefit comparison rather than denying that benefits exist. Its orphan teaching also introduces a fiduciary principle: proximity to vulnerable people's property or affairs is permissible only where their welfare is genuinely protected.

### Conclusion

Protection of vulnerable children strongly converges across traditions. The passage also contributes an explicit harm-benefit reasoning principle to the emerging Qur'anic ethical catalogue.

# E3 Unit 015 — Qur'an 2:221

## Ethics of Moral Character in Intimate Partnership

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:221 gives specifically religious marriage restrictions and explicitly places faith and moral orientation above social desirability or status.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Character and core commitments matter in intimate partnership** | Do not evaluate a spouse solely by attraction, status, or social desirability. |
| **Shared foundational commitments affect household ethics** | Intimate partnership has consequences for moral direction and should be chosen with those consequences in view. |

### Comparative Analysis — Character Above External Desirability

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — 2 Corinthians 6:14; Proverbs 31:30 in the biblical tradition | Shared religious commitment and character are placed above superficial attraction. | B | Choose intimate partnership with attention to character and fundamental commitments. |
| **Judaism** — covenantal marriage traditions | Marriage historically carries religious and communal obligations beyond attraction alone. | B | Partnership has ethical and communal dimensions. |
| **Buddhism** — Sigalovada Sutta | Marriage is evaluated through fidelity, respect, responsibility, and reciprocal duties. | B | A good partnership is defined by conduct and responsibility. |
| **Hinduism** — grihastha and marriage duties | Marriage is an ethical/social institution involving shared dharma and reciprocal obligation. | B | Partnership involves shared duties, not attraction alone. |
| **Sikhism** — Sikh marriage ideal | Marriage is framed as a shared spiritual and ethical journey. | B | Partnership should unite ethical and spiritual direction. |
| **Confucianism** — family ethics | Marriage serves family and social order and therefore carries duties beyond individual desire. | B | Intimate choice has wider ethical consequences. |
| **African Traditional** — diverse kinship and marriage systems | Marriage commonly links persons, families, responsibilities, and communal continuity. | C | Partnership is socially consequential, though rules vary greatly. |

### Discussion

The specifically Islamic legal boundary in the verse does not have a clean universal equivalent and should not be transformed into one. The more generalisable ethical principle is narrower: intimate partnership should take account of character, foundational commitments, and downstream household consequences rather than external desirability alone.

### Conclusion

There is broad functional convergence around marriage as an ethically consequential relationship, but the specific religious eligibility rules remain tradition-specific.

# E3 Unit 016 — Qur'an 2:222–223

## Ethics of Intimacy, Bodily Conditions, and Relational Responsibility

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:222–223 addresses sexual relations during menstruation and marital intimacy within a specifically religious and legal framework.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Intimacy is subject to ethical limits** | Sexual access is not treated as ethically unlimited. |
| **Bodily condition matters in intimate conduct** | Physical wellbeing and prescribed boundaries should be considered rather than ignored. |
| **Intimate conduct carries responsibility beyond immediate desire** | Sexual behaviour is placed within a wider framework of responsibility. |

### Comparative Analysis — Intimacy Is Ethically Bounded

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — 1 Corinthians 7:3–5 | Marital sexuality is framed through reciprocal obligation rather than unilateral entitlement. | B | Intimacy should recognise the other partner's standing and needs. |
| **Judaism** — Leviticus 18:19 and later family-purity law | Menstrual sexual relations are specifically regulated. | A | Sexual conduct is bounded by bodily/religious conditions. |
| **Buddhism** — Third Precept | Sexual misconduct is prohibited. | B | Sexual desire does not override ethical restraint. |
| **Hinduism** — dharma traditions regulating sexual conduct | Sexuality is governed by duties and restraints rather than treated as unrestricted appetite. | B | Intimacy is subject to moral discipline. |
| **Sikhism** — restraint of lust and fidelity within marriage | Sexual desire is to be governed rather than allowed to dominate conduct. | B | Intimacy requires fidelity and self-control. |
| **Confucianism** — ritual and family ethics | Sexual/family relations are embedded in ordered reciprocal responsibilities. | C | Intimate life belongs within a framework of relational duties. |
| **African Traditional** — diverse customary sexual and marital norms | Many traditions regulate intimacy through kinship, bodily, and social rules, but no single universal formulation is justified. | C | Sexual conduct is commonly socially bounded. |

### Discussion

This unit requires caution. The detailed bodily and ritual rule is not universal, and E3 should not manufacture equivalence. The legitimate comparative level is that sexual conduct is treated across traditions as ethically regulated rather than exempt from moral restraint.

### Conclusion

The broad principle of ethically bounded intimacy converges, while the specific menstrual rule has a particularly direct Jewish parallel and remains otherwise tradition-dependent.

# E3 Unit 017 — Qur'an 2:224–225

## Ethics of Oaths, Reconciliation, and Moral Intention

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:224–225 warns against using oaths as an obstacle to doing good, acting rightly, and making peace between people, while distinguishing careless speech from what is genuinely intended.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Do not weaponise commitments against the good** | A formal promise should not be used as an excuse to avoid beneficent or reconciliatory conduct. |
| **Promote reconciliation** | Making peace between people is a positive ethical aim. |
| **Moral responsibility considers intention** | Ethical accountability should distinguish accidental or careless expression from deliberate commitment. |

### Comparative Analysis — Promote Reconciliation

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Matthew 5:9, 23–24 | Peacemaking and reconciliation are explicitly commended. | A | Actively repair broken relationships where possible. |
| **Judaism** — pursuit of shalom; rabbinic peacemaking traditions | Peace between persons is treated as a major moral good. | A | Seek reconciliation rather than needless division. |
| **Buddhism** — Right Speech and teachings against divisive speech | Speech should unite rather than create hostility. | A | Avoid division and cultivate concord. |
| **Hinduism** — ahimsa and social harmony | Non-harm and restraint support reconciliation over escalating hostility. | B | Prefer peaceful restoration to unnecessary conflict. |
| **Sikhism** — rejection of enmity and emphasis on truthful, compassionate conduct | Ethical life seeks justice without cultivating hatred. | B | Resolve conflict without sustaining enmity. |
| **Confucianism** — harmony governed by righteousness | Social harmony is an important good, though not at the price of abandoning principle. | B | Seek principled harmony in relationships. |
| **African Traditional** — restorative and reconciliation practices | Community repair commonly seeks restoration of social relationships. | A/B | Repair damaged relationships and restore communal peace. |

### Discussion

The distinctive feature is the priority given to substantive good over formalistic use of an oath. Rules and promises matter, but they should not be manipulated into barriers against righteousness and reconciliation.

### Conclusion

Peacemaking is highly convergent, and the unit also contributes a significant meta-ethical principle: formal commitments should not be exploited to defeat the moral purposes they are meant to serve.

# E3 Unit 018 — Qur'an 2:226–227

## Ethics of Time-Bounded Marital Separation and Deliberate Decision

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:226–227 addresses a husband's oath of sexual abstention from his wife, imposes a waiting period, and then requires movement toward reconciliation or divorce rather than indefinite suspension.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Do not leave intimate partners indefinitely suspended** | Relationship conflict should not be prolonged indefinitely through unilateral uncertainty. |
| **Allow time for reconsideration** | A cooling-off period can create space for reconciliation before irreversible action. |
| **Resolve serious relational decisions deliberately** | Where reconciliation fails, decisions should become explicit rather than remaining coercively ambiguous. |

### Comparative Analysis — Avoid Indefinite Relational Suspension

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — 1 Corinthians 7:5 | Temporary abstinence is framed as consensual and time-limited, followed by reunion. | A/B | Separation in intimacy should not become unilateral indefinite deprivation. |
| **Judaism** — ketubah and marital-obligation traditions | Marriage creates enforceable relational obligations and mechanisms addressing their breach. | B | A spouse cannot simply disregard continuing marital duties without consequence. |
| **Buddhism** — reciprocal spousal duties in Sigalovada Sutta | Marriage involves reciprocal responsibilities rather than unilateral domination. | B | Partners owe each other responsible conduct. |
| **Hinduism** — reciprocal household duties | The householder relationship carries defined responsibilities. | B | Partnership entails continuing duties. |
| **Sikhism** — marriage as reciprocal spiritual partnership | Marriage is not framed as unilateral possession but shared ethical life. | B | Partners bear reciprocal responsibilities. |
| **Confucianism** — ordered reciprocal family roles | Family relations carry mutual duties and should be governed rather than arbitrarily abandoned. | C | Relational authority carries obligation. |
| **African Traditional** — diverse marital mediation practices | Elders/families often mediate serious marital disputes, but systems vary. | C | Serious relationship breakdown should move toward mediated resolution rather than indefinite uncertainty. |

### Discussion

The specific legal mechanism is not universal. The defensible comparative principle is procedural: intimate conflict should not be used to hold another person indefinitely in unresolved dependence.

### Conclusion

Reciprocal marital responsibility is widely represented, while the Qur'anic four-month mechanism remains a tradition-specific legal form.

# E3 Unit 019 — Qur'an 2:228–230

## Ethics of Reciprocal Marital Rights and Fair Separation

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:228–230 regulates divorce, waiting periods, reconciliation, repeated divorce, and release. Within its historical legal framework, it states that women have rights corresponding to obligations according to what is recognised as fair, while also containing gender-differentiated provisions.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Relationships contain reciprocal rights and duties** | Ethical partnership imposes obligations on both parties rather than recognising duties on only one side. |
| **Reconciliation should be genuine** | Return to a relationship is justified by a real intention to repair it, not to prolong control. |
| **Separation should be fair** | Where a relationship ends, release should occur without exploitation or arbitrary recovery of what was given. |
| **Repeated procedural power should not become abuse** | Legal mechanisms governing separation should not be manipulated into indefinite control. |

### Comparative Analysis — Reciprocal Rights and Duties

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — 1 Corinthians 7:3–4; Ephesians 5:21 | Marriage includes reciprocal obligations, despite differing historical role frameworks. | A/B | Partners owe duties to one another. |
| **Judaism** — Exodus 21:10 and ketubah tradition | Marital law recognises material and relational obligations owed to a wife. | B | Marriage creates enforceable responsibilities. |
| **Buddhism** — Sigalovada Sutta | Husband and wife are assigned reciprocal duties of respect, fidelity, responsibility, and care. | A | Ethical marriage requires mutual obligations. |
| **Hinduism** — household dharma | Spouses carry interlocking duties within family life. | B | Partnership is structured through reciprocal responsibility. |
| **Sikhism** — marriage as shared spiritual union | Spouses are oriented toward a common ethical and spiritual path. | B | Marriage is a partnership rather than mere possession. |
| **Confucianism** — reciprocal role ethics | Family roles are differentiated but relational duties operate in both directions. | B | Authority and role are accompanied by obligation. |
| **African Traditional** — diverse kinship systems | Marriage generally creates reciprocal obligations between spouses and often between families, though structures vary. | B | Partnership establishes duties and claims on both sides. |

### Discussion

The source must be preserved in its historical and gendered legal context. E3 should neither erase those differences nor treat them as identical to modern legal equality. The cross-comparative ethical extraction is the narrower proposition that marital relationships create reciprocal claims and that divorce procedures should not be used simply as instruments of exploitation.

### Conclusion

Reciprocal marital obligation is widely shared, while the specific legal distribution of rights and roles differs significantly between traditions and historical contexts.

# E3 Unit 020 — Qur'an 2:231–232

## Ethics of Non-Abusive Divorce and Respect for Adult Relational Choice

### Qur'anic Source

Qur'an 2:231–232 instructs that a divorcing husband should either retain a wife on acceptable terms or release her on acceptable terms, explicitly prohibits retaining her in order to cause harm, and prohibits obstructing a divorced woman from remarrying where the parties mutually agree on acceptable terms.

### Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

| Principle | Ethical Core |
|---|---|
| **Do not use relationship status as a weapon** | Formal power within a relationship must not be used to deliberately injure or control another person. |
| **End relationships without deliberate abuse** | If reconciliation is not genuine, separation should occur on fair terms. |
| **Do not obstruct legitimate adult choice for purposes of control** | Third parties should not arbitrarily prevent competent adults from entering an agreed relationship. |
| **Legal rights should not be exercised maliciously** | Possessing procedural power does not ethically justify using it to cause harm. |

### Comparative Analysis — Do Not Use Relational Power to Cause Harm

| Tradition / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Ethical Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Christianity** — Ephesians 5:25–33; 1 Corinthians 7 | Marital authority and obligation are framed through care rather than deliberate harm; separation is regulated rather than a licence for abuse. | B | Intimate relationships should not become instruments of deliberate injury. |
| **Judaism** — Deuteronomy 24:1–4 and rabbinic divorce law | Divorce is formalised and bounded rather than left to informal abandonment. | B | Separation should follow recognised limits and protect parties from arbitrary manipulation. |
| **Buddhism** — Sigalovada Sutta | Spouses owe respect, fidelity, and responsible treatment. | B | Relational power must be exercised with respect. |
| **Hinduism** — ahimsa and household duties | Non-harm and duty constrain conduct within family life. | B | Family relationships do not excuse intentional harm. |
| **Sikhism** — equality before the divine and marital partnership | Domination and degradation conflict with the ideal of shared spiritual dignity. | B | Partnership should respect the standing of both persons. |
| **Confucianism** — reciprocal duties within family roles | Legitimate relational authority is bounded by responsibility and humane conduct. | B | Role-based authority does not remove duties toward the other. |
| **African Traditional** — diverse marriage and mediation systems | Communal mechanisms frequently regulate marital disputes and abuse of relational obligations, though norms vary substantially. | C | Relationship power is subject to community-recognised duties and limits. |

### Discussion

This unit supplies an unusually explicit anti-abuse rule: a legal or relational mechanism may not legitimately be used with the purpose of causing harm. It also limits interference with subsequent consensual remarriage.

The broader ethical principle is therefore not simply a rule about divorce. It is that **formal authority becomes ethically corrupt when exercised primarily as a mechanism of domination or injury**.

### Conclusion

The rejection of deliberate harm within intimate relationships has broad functional parallels. The Qur'anic passage adds a strong procedural dimension by constraining how legal relationship powers may be exercised.

A significant feature of Units 015–020 is the concentration of family and marital regulation. This reflects the canonical sequence of the anchor text rather than thematic selection. It should therefore be preserved even though it creates a temporarily uneven thematic distribution.

The comparison also demonstrates the importance of the evidence grades. Some broad principles—reciprocal responsibility, non-harm, reconciliation—travel readily across traditions, while specific Islamic legal mechanisms concerning marriage, menstruation, oaths, waiting periods, and divorce often do not. Those specific differences remain part of the data rather than being forced into artificial equivalence.
