# E3 Unit 031 — Qur'an 3:17
## Patience, Truthfulness, and Generosity

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 3:17 commends patience, truthfulness, devoted obedience, spending for good, and humility before moral failure.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Remain steadfast under difficulty.**
- **Be truthful.**
- **Use resources for the good of others.**
- **Recognise and correct moral failure rather than defending it through pride.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Ephesians 4:25; Romans 12:12–13 | Truth, endurance, and generosity are explicit virtues. | A | Be truthful, steadfast, and generous. |
| Judaism — Leviticus 19; Proverbs | Truthful dealing, endurance, and charity are established duties. | A/B | Integrity includes truth and material responsibility. |
| Buddhism — Eightfold Path; dana; patience | Right speech, generosity, and patient endurance are cultivated virtues. | A/B | Restrain falsehood, give, and endure without harmful reaction. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 16:1–3 | Truth, charity, self-restraint, and fortitude appear among divine qualities. | A | Cultivate truth, generosity, and steadiness. |
| Sikhism — truthful living, seva, vand chhakna | Truthful conduct, service, and sharing are central. | A | Live truthfully and share with others. |
| Confucianism — xin, ren, self-cultivation | Trustworthiness and humane conduct require disciplined character. | B | Character joins truthfulness with responsible action. |
| African Traditional | Truth, endurance, and reciprocal support recur across diverse communal traditions. | B/C | Reliable persons speak truth and sustain community. |

### Conclusion
This source combines several virtues that recur widely; later synthesis must keep their identities separate despite their co-location here.

# E3 Unit 032 — Qur'an 3:21
## Protection of Justice and Those Who Advocate It

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 3:21 condemns wrongful killing, including killing people who call for justice.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not kill without right.**
- **Do not violently suppress people for advocating justice.**
- **The pursuit of justice requires protection from coercive retaliation.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:10; 23:29–35 | Persecution of the righteous and killing of prophets are condemned. | A/B | Do not persecute people for righteousness. |
| Judaism — Exodus 20:13; prophetic justice tradition | Wrongful killing is prohibited; prophets repeatedly defend justice. | A/B | Protect life and permit moral correction of power. |
| Buddhism — First Precept | Intentional killing is prohibited. | B | Do not destroy life. |
| Hinduism — ahimsa and dharmic justice | Non-harm and justice constrain coercive power. | B | Do not use violence against rightful moral action. |
| Sikhism — defence of justice and the oppressed | Sikh ethics strongly value resisting persecution. | A/B | Protect people from violent oppression. |
| Confucianism — remonstrance traditions | Moral advisers may owe rulers truthful correction. | B | Authority should tolerate principled correction. |
| African Traditional | Councils, elders, and communal adjudication can constrain wrongdoing, though forms vary. | C | Legitimate authority should not silence justice through violence. |

### Conclusion
The anti-killing principle is highly convergent; protection of justice advocates is more specifically institutional and varies in formulation.

# E3 Unit 033 — Qur'an 3:92
## Generosity Involving What One Values

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 3:92 links moral excellence with giving from possessions one genuinely values.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Meaningful generosity requires real sacrifice.**
- **Do not define charity solely as disposal of what costs you nothing.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Mark 12:41–44 | The widow's costly gift is valued above larger but less sacrificial giving. | A | Generosity is partly measured by sacrifice. |
| Judaism — tzedakah traditions | Giving is a duty and can require surrender of valued resources. | B | Share resources even when doing so has real cost. |
| Buddhism — dana | Non-attached giving loosens possessiveness. | B | Give without clinging to possessions. |
| Hinduism — dana | Proper giving is a substantive moral discipline. | B | Generosity should be genuine rather than token. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Share what one earns with others. | B | Give from one's actual resources. |
| Confucianism — righteousness over profit | Material advantage should yield to moral obligation. | B | Do not make personal gain supreme. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocal sharing often involves valued resources needed by the group. | B/C | Genuine solidarity carries material cost. |

### Conclusion
The verse deepens the already recurring generosity cluster by adding sacrifice as a quality criterion.

# E3 Unit 034 — Qur'an 3:103

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 3:103 instructs the believing community to hold together and not divide, recalling transformation from enmity into fraternity.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not cultivate needless factional division.**
- **Preserve reconciliation after former enemies have made peace.**
- **Shared moral commitments can support social cohesion.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 12:18; Ephesians 4:3 | Peace and unity are explicitly encouraged. | A | Preserve peace where possible. |
| Judaism — Psalm 133; pursuit of shalom | Communal peace and unity are praised. | A/B | Seek peaceful communal relations. |
| Buddhism — sangha concord rules | Harmony within the community is a major disciplinary concern. | A/B | Avoid schism and cultivate concord. |
| Hinduism — social harmony and non-harm traditions | Cooperative order is ethically valued. | B | Avoid destructive division. |
| Sikhism — sangat and rejection of caste division | Shared community transcends divisive status boundaries. | B | Build solidarity around shared dignity. |
| Confucianism — harmony without sameness | Social harmony is a central good. | A/B | Maintain principled harmony without requiring identical views. |
| African Traditional — Ubuntu/communal reconciliation | Restored relationship is central in many communal ethics. | B | Personhood and flourishing depend on repaired community. |

### Conclusion
Communal reconciliation strongly converges, though E3 does not equate unity with suppression of legitimate disagreement.

# E3 Unit 035 — Qur'an 3:104

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 3:104 calls for a community that invites toward good, promotes what is right, and opposes what is wrong.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Ethical responsibility can be collective as well as individual.**
- **Communities should actively support beneficial conduct.**
- **Wrongdoing should not become socially immune from principled challenge.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 18:15–17; Galatians 6:1 | Communities have duties of correction and restoration. | B | Correct wrongdoing with a restorative aim. |

| Hinduism — dharma | Social roles include responsibilities to uphold right conduct. | B | Moral order requires active maintenance. |

| African Traditional | Elders/councils commonly mediate and sanction serious wrongdoing. | B | Community shares responsibility for maintaining norms. |

### Conclusion
Collective ethical accountability is widely represented, although mechanisms and legitimate authority differ substantially.

# E3 Unit 036 — Qur'an 3:113–115
## Do Not Erase Moral Differences Within an Out-Group

### Qur'anic Source
After criticism of some People of the Scripture, Qur'an 3:113–115 explicitly states that they are not all alike and identifies righteous members who do good.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not treat a criticised group as morally homogeneous.**
- **Judge good conduct as good even when performed by members of another community.**
- **Group identity should not erase evidence about individual or subgroup conduct.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 10:25–37 | The Good Samaritan makes an outsider the exemplary moral actor. | A/B | Moral worth cannot be inferred simply from group identity. |
| Judaism — Genesis 18:25; justice traditions | The righteous should not be indiscriminately treated as the wicked. | B | Distinguish persons according to conduct. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 393 | Status or birth does not alone make a person spiritually worthy; conduct matters. | A/B | Judge moral quality by conduct rather than label. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 5:18 | The wise are described as seeing with an equal vision across status categories. | B | Social category should not erase shared moral standing. |
| Sikhism — rejection of caste hierarchy | Birth category does not determine spiritual worth. | A/B | Evaluate persons beyond inherited group status. |
| Confucianism — Analects 15.23/related humane reciprocity | Ethical regard is grounded in cultivated humanity rather than crude factional stereotyping. | B/C | Do not let category substitute for moral judgement. |
| African Traditional | Because traditions are highly local, no single canonical out-group rule is asserted. | C | Many communal systems distinguish conduct within and across groups, but evidence must remain specific. |

### Conclusion
This is an important anti-generalisation principle generated directly by the anchor text: criticism of a group is itself qualified by recognition of righteous members.

# E3 Unit 037 — Qur'an 3:120

Qur'an 3:120 describes hostile rejoicing at the community's misfortune and responds by commending patience and moral restraint rather than reactive misconduct.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not let another person's hostility determine your own moral conduct.**
- **Exercise patience when provoked.**
- **Strategic restraint can be preferable to reactive escalation.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Romans 12:17–21 | Do not repay evil for evil; overcome evil with good. | A | Do not let hostility reproduce itself through you. |

| Confucianism — principled response to injury | Wrongdoing should be answered through uprightness rather than uncontrolled vengeance. | B | Respond according to principle, not rage. |
| African Traditional | Reconciliation traditions often seek to prevent feud escalation. | B/C | Restrain cycles of reciprocal hostility. |

### Conclusion
This source strongly converges with the earlier non-retaliation cluster while adding the dimension of maintaining ethical agency under deliberate provocation.

# E3 Unit 039 — Qur'an 3:133–136
## Generosity, Anger Restraint, Forgiveness, and Correction After Wrongdoing

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 3:133–136 commends spending in prosperity and adversity, restraining anger, forgiving others, and responding to one's own wrongdoing through acknowledgement and non-persistence.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Give in both ease and hardship.**
- **Control anger rather than allowing it to dictate action.**
- **Forgive others where appropriate.**
- **Acknowledge wrongdoing and do not knowingly persist in it.**

### Comparative Analysis — Restrain Anger and Correct Wrongdoing

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Ephesians 4:26, 31–32; repentance traditions | Anger is restrained, forgiveness commended, and wrongdoing calls for repentance. | A | Control anger, forgive, and turn from wrong. |
| Judaism — Proverbs 16:32; teshuvah | Self-control over anger and return from wrongdoing are major virtues. | A | Master anger and correct moral failure. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 221–223 | Abandon anger and overcome hostility through non-hostility. | A | Do not let anger govern conduct. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 2:62–63; 16:1–3 | Anger is treated as morally destructive; forgiveness and self-control are virtues. | A/B | Restrain anger and cultivate self-mastery. |
| Sikhism — krodh as a destructive vice | Anger is one of the major impulses to overcome. | A/B | Govern anger rather than be governed by it. |
| Confucianism — self-mastery and correction of faults | The cultivated person examines and corrects faults. | B | Moral development requires self-correction. |
| African Traditional | Reconciliation commonly requires acknowledgement, restraint, and restoration. | B/C | Repair wrongdoing rather than perpetuate it. |

### Conclusion
This is a dense virtue cluster with exceptionally broad convergence, but final synthesis must keep generosity, anger restraint, forgiveness, and repentance as distinct principles.

# E3 Unit 040 — Qur'an 3:159
## Gentleness, Forgiveness, and Consultation in Leadership

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 3:159 connects leadership with gentleness, forgiveness, seeking pardon for others, and consultation in collective affairs before decisive action.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Leadership should avoid unnecessary harshness.**
- **Forgiveness can preserve cohesion after failure.**
- **Consult affected people in collective affairs.**
- **Consultation should lead to decision rather than permanent paralysis.**

### Comparative Analysis — Consultative and Humane Leadership

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Mark 10:42–45; Acts 15 | Leadership is framed as service; early communal decisions include deliberation. | B | Authority should serve and listen rather than dominate. |
| Judaism — Exodus 18:17–26; council traditions | Moses is advised to distribute judgement and responsibility. | A/B | Good governance uses shared counsel and delegated responsibility. |
| Buddhism — sangha deliberative procedures | Community decisions use established collective procedures. | A/B | Shared institutions should deliberate rather than depend solely on arbitrary will. |
| Hinduism — counsel in statecraft/dharma traditions | Rulers are advised to consult ministers and govern according to duty. | B | Authority benefits from counsel and ethical restraint. |
| Sikhism — sangat/panthic deliberative traditions | Collective deliberation has an important place in community governance. | B | Shared affairs should involve collective counsel. |
| Confucianism — remonstrance and humane government | Rulers should hear principled advisers and govern humanely. | A/B | Authority requires counsel and humane conduct. |
| African Traditional | Councils of elders and consensus-oriented governance occur widely, though not universally. | B | Collective decisions should draw on communal deliberation. |

### Discussion

This source combines interpersonal virtue with governance procedure. Gentleness and forgiveness concern leadership character; consultation concerns institutional method. The verse therefore reinforces an emerging Qur'anic pattern in which ethical aims are supported by decision procedures rather than left solely to personal virtue.

### Conclusion
Humane, consultative leadership has substantial cross-traditional parallels. The combination of mercy, consultation, and subsequent decisiveness is a distinctive systems-level formulation.

Two features are especially important methodologically.

**First, Unit 036 emerged without being sought.** The anchor text itself interrupts a potentially broad criticism of an out-group by stating that its members are not all alike and recognising righteous conduct among them. That principle must therefore remain in the dataset even if it was not prominent in the earlier Psalm-derived architecture.

**Second, institutional ethics continues to recur.** Unit 035 treats ethical maintenance as a collective responsibility, while Unit 040 joins leadership character to consultation. These findings remain provisional and must not be promoted into final architecture before completion of the corpus.
