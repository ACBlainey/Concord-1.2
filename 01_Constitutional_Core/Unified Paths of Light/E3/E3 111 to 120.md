# E3 Unit 111 — Qur'an 16:126–127
## Proportionate Response and the Superiority of Restraint

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 16:126 permits response to injury only in proportion to the injury received, while immediately presenting patient restraint as better.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Retaliation, where permitted, must not exceed the original wrong.**
- **Possession of a right to respond does not require exercising it maximally.**
- **Restraint can be ethically preferable to proportionate retaliation.**
- **Justice establishes an upper bound; mercy can choose less.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:38–44 | The retaliation formula is followed by a stronger ethic of non-retaliation and enemy-love. | A | A right to answer injury can be voluntarily restrained. |
| Judaism — Exodus 21:23–25 | Lex talionis functions as a proportionality constraint in its legal context. | A | Punishment should not exceed the wrong. |
| Buddhism — Dhammapada 3–5 | Hatred is not ended by hatred. | A/B | Restraint is preferable to retaliatory escalation. |
| Hinduism — dharma and forgiveness | Proportionate justice coexists with praise of forbearance. | B | Response should be bounded and may be merciful. |
| Sikhism — disciplined defence | Force may be justified against oppression but is ethically constrained. | B | Necessary resistance should not become vengeance. |
| Confucianism — uprightness in response to injury | Response should be principled rather than excessive. | B | Do not let injury dictate uncontrolled retaliation. |
| African Traditional | Compensation and restorative settlement often limit feud escalation, though practices vary. | B/C | Bound response and restore relationship where possible. |

### Conclusion
The verse produces a two-level rule: proportionality defines the ceiling of justified response, while restraint remains ethically preferable where feasible.

# E3 Unit 112 — Qur'an 17:23–24
## Care and Humility Toward Ageing Parents

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:23–24 commands good treatment of parents, specifically prohibiting expressions of contempt when one or both reach old age and requiring respectful, humble speech and care.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Ageing dependency does not diminish a parent's dignity.**
- **Do not answer the burdens of care with contempt or verbal degradation.**
- **Family responsibility includes respectful treatment, not merely material provision.**
- **Power can reverse across generations; former dependants may acquire duties toward those who once cared for them.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Exodus 20:12; 1 Timothy 5:4 | Parents are to be honoured and family care reciprocated. | A | Honour and care for parents. |
| Judaism — Exodus 20:12; Leviticus 19:3 | Honour and reverence toward parents are explicit. | A | Treat parents with enduring respect. |
| Buddhism — Sigalovada Sutta | Children have reciprocal duties toward parents. | A | Repay parental care through responsible support. |
| Hinduism — filial duty | Care and reverence toward parents are strongly established. | A | Honour those who raised and supported you. |
| Sikhism — household responsibility and respect | Family duties remain part of ethical life. | B | Treat dependent family members with dignity. |
| Confucianism — xiao | Filial piety is foundational. | A | Care for parents respectfully, especially in dependency. |
| African Traditional | Respect and care for elders are highly prominent across many traditions. | A/B | Ageing should increase care, not social abandonment. |

### Conclusion
This is one of the strongest convergence points in family ethics, with particular emphasis on preserving dignity during age-related dependency.

# E3 Unit 113 — Qur'an 17:26–27
## Give What Is Due and Do Not Squander Resources

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:26–27 directs support toward relatives, the poor, and travellers while strongly condemning wasteful squandering.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Direct resources toward legitimate social obligations.**
- **Support people experiencing poverty or displacement.**
- **Do not dissipate resources through pointless excess.**
- **Generosity and stewardship are complementary rather than opposing principles.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 25:35; John 6:12 | Care for strangers and the needy coexists with avoidance of waste. | A/B | Share resources and do not waste them. |
| Judaism — tzedakah and bal tashchit | Charity and anti-waste principles are both established. | A | Direct resources toward need without needless destruction. |
| Buddhism — dana and moderation | Giving and restrained consumption are complementary. | A/B | Share rather than hoard or squander. |
| Hinduism — dana and moderation | Generosity and disciplined consumption are both valued. | B | Use surplus for legitimate benefit. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing resources is central; greed and excess are restrained. | A/B | Share what one has with others. |
| Confucianism — benevolence and frugality | Care for others is joined to restraint in expenditure. | A/B | Use resources humanely and without extravagance. |
| African Traditional | Kin support, hospitality, and communal resource obligations are widespread, though locally structured. | B | Resources carry reciprocal social duties. |

### Conclusion
The source rejects both hoarding and squandering: responsible stewardship includes positive distribution.

# E3 Unit 114 — Qur'an 17:28
## Decline Requests Without Degrading the Requester

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:28 addresses circumstances in which a person cannot provide requested assistance and instructs the use of gentle or gracious speech.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Inability to help materially does not justify humiliation.**
- **A refusal can preserve the dignity of the person asking.**
- **Kind communication is itself a form of ethical conduct.**
- **Resource limits should be communicated without contempt.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Colossians 4:6; James 2:15–16 | Speech should be gracious, though empty words cannot replace available material aid. | B | Speak respectfully even when material assistance is unavailable. |
| Judaism — tzedakah ethics | Charity should preserve recipient dignity. | A/B | Do not humiliate a person in need. |
| Buddhism — Right Speech | Speech should be kind, beneficial, and timely. | A | Refusal need not become verbal harm. |
| Hinduism — disciplined, non-agitating speech | Ethical speech should avoid unnecessary injury. | B | Communicate limits without cruelty. |
| Sikhism — respectful speech and seva | Service includes recognition of equal dignity. | B | Do not demean those seeking help. |
| Confucianism — li and ren | Courtesy and humaneness remain relevant when denying a request. | B | Preserve dignity through manner. |
| African Traditional | Hospitality norms often regulate how requests and refusals are handled, though forms vary. | B/C | Material inability need not become social rejection. |

### Conclusion
The verse separates two resources—material aid and respectful speech. A shortage of the first does not excuse withholding the second.

# E3 Unit 115 — Qur'an 17:29
## Neither Miserliness Nor Ruinous Generosity

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:29 uses a vivid image to reject both extreme withholding and uncontrolled expenditure that leaves the giver depleted.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not hoard resources through excessive miserliness.**
- **Do not give or spend so recklessly that legitimate future responsibilities become impossible.**
- **Sustainable generosity requires balance.**
- **An ethical transfer should consider both recipient need and giver capacity.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 2 Corinthians 8:12–14 | Giving is related to what a person has and to fair balance rather than impossible burden. | A/B | Give generously without ignoring capacity. |
| Judaism — graded tzedakah traditions | Charity is required but historically bounded to avoid self-impoverishment. | A/B | Generosity should be substantial yet sustainable. |
| Buddhism — Middle Way and dana | Giving is virtuous without requiring heedless self-destruction. | B | Avoid both attachment and reckless excess. |
| Hinduism — Bhagavad Gita 17:20–22 | Giving is evaluated by appropriateness, recipient, time, and motive. | A/B | Give in a fitting and responsible manner. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing is integrated with honest household life. | B | Share sustainably rather than hoard. |
| Confucianism — mean and frugality | Proper measure avoids excess and deficiency. | A/B | Balance generosity with responsible stewardship. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocal giving is often constrained by continuing family/community obligations. | B | Sharing should sustain rather than destroy reciprocal capacity. |

### Conclusion
The unit yields a sustainability rule: generosity should be real, but ethical systems need not equate virtue with resource exhaustion.

# E3 Unit 116 — Qur'an 17:31
## Do Not Destroy Dependants From Fear of Scarcity

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:31 prohibits killing children from fear of poverty and rejects anticipated scarcity as justification.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not destroy vulnerable dependants because future resources are feared to be insufficient.**
- **Forecast scarcity does not automatically justify irreversible harm.**
- **The weakest party should not bear the ultimate cost of economic anxiety.**
- **Fear about the future requires proportional response rather than destructive certainty.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Mark 9:36–37; protection of children | Children are treated as persons deserving care rather than disposable burdens. | B | Protect vulnerable children. |
| Judaism — prohibition of murder and family obligations | Child sacrifice is rejected within biblical tradition. | A/B | Economic or ritual interest does not license killing children. |
| Buddhism — First Precept | Killing is prohibited. | A | Do not kill vulnerable life. |
| Hinduism — ahimsa and family duty | Non-harm and care for dependants oppose destructive responses to scarcity. | B | Protect dependants rather than sacrifice them to fear. |
| Sikhism — family responsibility and protection of life | Vulnerability calls for protection. | B | Do not make the weak absorb the cost of hardship. |
| Confucianism — family responsibility | Parents have duties toward children and family continuity. | B | Scarcity does not erase parental obligation. |
| African Traditional | Children are central to lineage/community continuity in many traditions. | B | Economic difficulty should not convert dependants into disposable burdens. |

### Conclusion
This repeats the child-protection principle while adding an important decision rule: feared future scarcity is not sufficient justification for irreversible harm.

# E3 Unit 117 — Qur'an 17:32
## Do Not Approach Exploitative or Illicit Sexual Conduct

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:32 prohibits approaching zina, framing the rule not only around the final act but also around conduct leading toward it.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Sexual conduct is subject to ethical boundaries.**
- **Prevention can require attention to foreseeable pathways into serious wrongdoing, not only the terminal act.**
- **Desire does not eliminate duties created by existing relationships and social consequences.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:27–28 | Sexual ethics address both conduct and cultivated intention. | A | Sexual responsibility begins before the final harmful act. |
| Judaism — Exodus 20:14, 17 | Adultery and coveting another's spouse are prohibited. | A | Respect established relational boundaries. |
| Buddhism — Third Precept | Sexual misconduct is prohibited. | A | Avoid sexual conduct that exploits or harms. |
| Hinduism — brahmacharya / sexual restraint | Sexual desire is subject to disciplined ethical limits. | B | Govern sexual conduct rather than be governed by appetite. |
| Sikhism — rejection of uncontrolled lust | Lust is a vice when it captures conduct. | B | Sexual desire requires self-restraint. |
| Confucianism — propriety and family ethics | Sexual conduct is embedded in relational obligations. | B | Desire should not destabilise legitimate relationships. |
| African Traditional | Sexual norms vary greatly by society, so no uniform detailed equivalent is asserted. | C | Sexual behaviour is generally socially regulated rather than ethically consequence-free. |

### Conclusion
The source adds a preventative dimension to sexual ethics: systems can address high-risk pathways before harm becomes irreversible.

# E3 Unit 118 — Qur'an 17:33
## Sanctity of Life and Limits on Retaliation

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:33 prohibits wrongful killing, recognises a claim for the representative of a person unjustly killed, and then explicitly prohibits excess in retaliation.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not wrongfully take life.**
- **Victims and their representatives have legitimate claims to justice.**
- **Victimhood does not create unlimited permission for retaliation.**
- **Justice after severe harm remains bounded by proportionality.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 5:21, 38–44 | Killing is condemned and retaliation is further restrained. | A/B | Protect life and resist escalating vengeance. |
| Judaism — Exodus 21:23–25; Numbers 35 | Homicide law distinguishes culpability and limits retaliation through law. | A | Justice for killing should remain legally bounded. |
| Buddhism — First Precept | Taking life is prohibited. | A | Refrain from killing. |
| Hinduism — ahimsa alongside bounded justice traditions | Life is protected while justice remains contextual. | B | Severe wrong does not justify unlimited vengeance. |
| Sikhism — defence without hatred | Force can be justified under constraints but vengeance is not the goal. | B | Protect life and bound retaliatory force. |
| Confucianism — lawful punishment and humane rule | Punishment should serve order rather than private rage. | B | Justice should not become uncontrolled revenge. |
| African Traditional | Compensation and regulated retaliation historically constrain feud in many systems. | B | Victim claims should be recognised without endless escalation. |

### Conclusion
The verse joins victim recognition with anti-excess: acknowledging a grave wrong does not remove limits on the response.

# E3 Unit 119 — Qur'an 17:34
## Protect Orphan Property and Fulfil Commitments

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:34 prohibits approaching an orphan's property except in a manner that improves or protects it until maturity, and commands fulfilment of commitments.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **A guardian may interact with dependent property only for the beneficiary's legitimate interest.**
- **Temporary stewardship should terminate when the owner reaches sufficient maturity.**
- **Do not convert dependency into permanent dispossession.**
- **Honour commitments because agreements create accountability.**

### Comparative Analysis — Fiduciary Stewardship

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — stewardship traditions; James 1:27 | Vulnerable persons and entrusted resources require faithful care. | B | Manage another's interests for their benefit, not one's own. |
| Judaism — orphan protection and property law | Orphans receive strong protection from exploitation. | A/B | Dependency increases fiduciary duty. |
| Buddhism — non-stealing | Another's property cannot be appropriated because its owner is weak. | B | Vulnerability does not erase ownership. |
| Hinduism — guardianship/dharma traditions | Guardianship carries duties toward the dependent person's estate. | B | Custody should serve the beneficiary. |
| Sikhism — honest dealing and protection of the vulnerable | Exploitation of dependency conflicts with truthful conduct. | B | Do not enrich oneself from another's weakness. |
| Confucianism — benevolent guardianship | Authority toward dependants carries responsibilities. | B | Stewardship should serve the dependent person. |
| African Traditional | Kin guardianship often includes responsibilities toward inherited/dependent property, with substantial local variation. | B/C | Custody is not equivalent to unrestricted ownership. |

### Conclusion
The source repeats a particularly strong Qur'anic fiduciary pattern: **protect during incapacity, then return control when capacity arrives**.

# E3 Unit 120 — Qur'an 17:35
## Accurate Measurement and Transparent Exchange

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 17:35 commands full measure and correct weighing with an accurate balance.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Use accurate, shared standards in exchange.**
- **Do not hide theft inside measurement.**
- **Transparency and comparability reduce opportunities for exploitation.**
- **Reliable measurement is infrastructure for economic trust.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity / biblical tradition — Leviticus 19:35–36 | Honest measures are explicitly required. | A | Use accurate commercial standards. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 25:13–16 | Dishonest weights and measures are prohibited. | A | Do not manipulate measurement for gain. |
| Buddhism — Right Livelihood | Deceptive trade is incompatible with right livelihood. | B | Exchange without fraud. |
| Hinduism — commercial dharma | False measures are condemned. | A/B | Market standards should be honest. |
| Sikhism — honest earning | Legitimate livelihood excludes deceptive gain. | B | Earn without cheating. |
| Confucianism — trustworthiness | Stable exchange depends on reliable standards and conduct. | B | Make transactions trustworthy. |
| African Traditional | Market standards and sanctions against cheating occur widely though not uniformly. | B/C | Shared measurement should not be manipulated. |

### Discussion
This principle has appeared repeatedly in the Qur'anic corpus. Its recurrence should not inflate the number of distinct ethics, but it is important evidence about **frequency and emphasis**. The anchor text repeatedly treats economic measurement as a moral rather than merely technical matter.

### Conclusion
Accurate measurement is a small-scale institutional safeguard: common standards reduce information asymmetry and constrain opportunistic exploitation.

# Interim Findings: Units 111–120

| Cluster | Units |
|---|---|
| Proportionality plus restraint | 111 |
| Dignity and care of ageing parents | 112 |
| Social support plus anti-waste | 113 |
| Dignity-preserving refusal | 114 |
| Sustainable generosity | 115 |
| Protection of dependants under scarcity | 116 |
| Preventative sexual ethics | 117 |
| Life protection and bounded retaliation | 118 |
| Developmental fiduciary stewardship | 119 |
| Accurate measurement and economic trust | 120 |

A notable pattern in this group is **boundedness**.

Unit 111 bounds retaliation. Unit 115 bounds both withholding and giving. Unit 118 bounds the response even to wrongful killing. Unit 119 bounds guardianship by the beneficiary's maturity. Unit 120 bounds economic discretion through shared measurement.

This is not simply moderation as temperament. In several cases the anchor text creates **structural limits on otherwise available power**.

Units 112, 114, 116, and 119 also reinforce a second pattern: vulnerability does not reduce dignity or entitlement. Age, poverty, childhood, dependency, and lack of resources repeatedly generate additional responsibilities for the stronger party.

These observations remain provisional until completion and audit of the Qur'anic anchor corpus.
