## Method Note

Canonical Qur'an-first extraction continues into the short later surahs. Inclusion remains based on separable ethical content, not a requirement that every surah generate a unit. Grades: **A direct textual; B strong functional; C inferred/corollary; D no clear equivalent**.

# E3 Unit 191 — Qur'an 90:12–16
## Liberation and Aid During Severe Need

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 90:12–16 describes the difficult moral path through acts including freeing a person from bondage and feeding people during severe hunger, especially orphans and people in extreme poverty.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Use resources to reduce severe human dependency and deprivation.**
- **Liberation from bondage is a positive moral good.**
- **Aid is especially important during systemic scarcity or famine.**
- **Vulnerability should increase rather than decrease moral attention.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 25:35; liberation traditions | Feeding the hungry and care for the vulnerable are central. | A/B | Direct resources toward severe need. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 15; poor relief | Debt release, servant release, and aid to the poor are explicit. | A | Reduce dependency and material deprivation. |
| Buddhism — dana and compassion | Aid to suffering beings is strongly valued. | A/B | Relieve severe need. |
| Hinduism — dana | Feeding and charitable giving are major virtues. | B | Support people facing deprivation. |
| Sikhism — langar and seva | Feeding others and service are institutionalised. | A | Meet basic needs without status distinction. |
| Confucianism — humane government | Subsistence and relief are responsibilities of good governance. | B | Protect people in material distress. |
| African Traditional | Mutual aid during scarcity is prominent in many communities. | B | Severe need activates reciprocal support obligations. |

### Conclusion
The source links freedom and subsistence: both bondage and extreme deprivation are conditions from which ethical action should help release people.

# E3 Unit 192 — Qur'an 90:17
## Mutual Encouragement Toward Patience and Compassion

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 90:17 praises communities whose members encourage one another toward patience and mercy.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Ethical behaviour can be socially reinforced.**
- **Communities should encourage resilience without abandoning compassion.**
- **Mercy and perseverance are complementary rather than opposing virtues.**
- **Norm formation is a shared responsibility, not merely an individual task.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Hebrews 10:24 | Community members should spur one another toward love and good works. | A | Build social reinforcement for ethical conduct. |
| Judaism — communal exhortation traditions | Members share responsibility for encouraging right conduct. | B | Ethics is socially sustained. |
| Buddhism — sangha / spiritual friendship | Community supports persistence and compassion. | A/B | Mutual support strengthens ethical practice. |
| Hinduism — satsanga | Good company supports moral/spiritual development. | A/B | Community influences character. |
| Sikhism — sangat | Fellowship reinforces service and truthful living. | A | Ethical community is mutually formative. |
| Confucianism — friendship and cultivation | Friends assist one another's moral development. | A/B | Encourage virtue through relationship. |
| African Traditional | Communal formation of character is central in many traditions. | A/B | Virtue is socially cultivated. |

### Conclusion
This source explicitly recognises moral culture as a collective production.

# E3 Unit 193 — Qur'an 92:5–10
## Generosity and Miserliness Create Divergent Moral Pathways

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 92:5–10 contrasts giving and moral responsibility with miserliness and self-sufficiency, presenting them as trajectories toward different outcomes.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Repeated choices form behavioural pathways.**
- **Generosity tends to reinforce outward responsibility; miserliness reinforces isolation and self-sufficiency.**
- **Character can emerge from cumulative action rather than one isolated decision.**
- **Resource behaviour has effects on the giver as well as recipients.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — 2 Corinthians 9:6–8 | Generous giving is treated as formative and fruitful. | A/B | Generosity develops through practice. |
| Judaism — tzedakah / habit traditions | Repeated right action forms character. | A/B | Giving is both duty and cultivation. |
| Buddhism — dana | Giving weakens attachment and develops generosity. | A | Repeated generosity changes the giver. |
| Hinduism — dana | Giving forms part of disciplined ethical life. | B | Practice shapes disposition. |
| Sikhism — vand chhakna | Sharing is a recurring practice, not a one-time act. | A/B | Build generosity into ordinary life. |
| Confucianism — habituation through ritual/practice | Character is cultivated through repeated conduct. | A/B | Repeated action forms virtue. |
| African Traditional | Reciprocal giving often sustains identity and membership. | B | Generosity reinforces communal bonds. |

### Conclusion
The unit adds a developmental insight: ethics is not only rule compliance but pathway formation through repeated choices.

# E3 Unit 194 — Qur'an 93:9–10
## Do Not Oppress Orphans or Rebuke Those Who Ask

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 93:9–10 prohibits oppressing the orphan and rebuking the person who asks for assistance.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not exploit people who lack ordinary protection.**
- **Need does not justify humiliation.**
- **A request for help should not itself become grounds for verbal abuse.**
- **Even when assistance cannot be provided, dignity should be preserved.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 1:27; Matthew 5:42 | Orphans and requesters receive explicit moral concern. | A | Protect the vulnerable and respond humanely to need. |
| Judaism — Deuteronomy 24:17 | Orphans receive strong legal protection. | A | Do not exploit those lacking protectors. |
| Buddhism — compassion | Vulnerability calls for compassionate response. | B | Do not add humiliation to suffering. |
| Hinduism — dana and hospitality | Requesters and guests are recognised recipients of giving. | B | Treat people in need respectfully. |
| Sikhism — seva / langar | Aid is provided without status humiliation. | A/B | Meet need while preserving dignity. |
| Confucianism — benevolence | Humane treatment extends to vulnerable people. | B | Need should evoke care, not contempt. |
| African Traditional | Orphan care is often embedded in extended kin/community structures. | B | Protect those lacking immediate household protection. |

### Conclusion
The source pairs material vulnerability with reputational dignity.

# E3 Unit 195 — Qur'an 95:4–6
## Human Capacity for Dignity and Moral Development

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 95:4–6 describes humanity as created in an excellent form while also capable of decline, except through faith and constructive action.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Human beings possess significant capacity but are not guaranteed to use it well.**
- **Dignity and developmental potential coexist with moral fallibility.**
- **Status should depend less on assumed perfection than on continuing conduct.**
- **Ethical systems should account for both potential and failure.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Genesis 1:27 alongside fall traditions | Human dignity coexists with moral fallibility. | A/B | High human worth does not imply moral perfection. |
| Judaism — Genesis 1:27 and moral-choice traditions | Human beings possess dignity and responsibility. | A/B | Capacity creates responsibility. |
| Buddhism | Human birth is treated as a valuable opportunity for development, without a creator-based dignity claim. | B | Human life offers unusual developmental potential. |
| Hinduism | Human embodiment is often treated as a valuable opportunity for liberation. | B | Human capacity should be developed responsibly. |
| Sikhism | Human life is a significant opportunity for truthful/spiritual development. | B | Human potential requires right use. |
| Confucianism — moral potential | Humans possess capacities for cultivation that can be developed or neglected. | A/B | Potential becomes virtue through practice. |
| African Traditional | Conceptions of personhood often emphasise development through community. | B | Personhood can involve realised moral capacity. |

### Conclusion
This source supports a developmental rather than static account of human dignity.

# E3 Unit 196 — Qur'an 103:1–3
## Truth, Patience, and Mutual Ethical Reinforcement

### Qur'anic Source
Surah al-'Asr describes human loss except among those who combine faith, constructive action, mutual encouragement toward truth, and mutual encouragement toward patience.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Belief or stated values alone are insufficient without constructive action.**
- **Truth should be socially reinforced.**
- **Perseverance should be socially reinforced.**
- **Communities can help members sustain difficult ethical commitments over time.**
- **Ethical resilience is partly relational.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — James 2:17; Hebrews 10:24 | Faith without action is incomplete; community encourages good works. | A | Values should become action and mutual encouragement. |
| Judaism — study/action traditions | Ethical teaching is directed toward practice and communal reinforcement. | A/B | Truth should be lived. |
| Buddhism — sangha and practice | Community supports sustained right practice. | A/B | Ethical development requires action and perseverance. |
| Hinduism — karma yoga / satsanga | Right action and good association support development. | B | Practice values and reinforce them socially. |
| Sikhism — sangat and truthful living | Truth is lived collectively through action. | A | Truth should become practice. |
| Confucianism — cultivation through community | Learning must become action and be sustained through relationships. | A/B | Ethical knowledge requires practice. |
| African Traditional | Communal moral formation is widespread. | B | Community reinforces norms and perseverance. |

### Conclusion
This compact surah strongly converges with the project's emerging model of ethics as both individual conduct and social reinforcement.

# E3 Unit 197 — Qur'an 104:1–3
## Do Not Slander, Mock, or Treat Wealth as Security

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 104:1–3 condemns the habitual slanderer/mockery-maker who accumulates and repeatedly counts wealth as though it could provide enduring security.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Do not build status through humiliating others.**
- **Do not make wealth accumulation the primary measure of security or worth.**
- **Mockery and material obsession can reinforce one another as status behaviours.**
- **Possession does not make a person morally invulnerable.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Luke 12:15–21; James 3 | Wealth fixation and harmful speech are both condemned. | A | Do not seek security in accumulation or status through insult. |
| Judaism — harmful speech and charity traditions | Slander is constrained and wealth carries duties. | A/B | Wealth and speech remain morally accountable. |
| Buddhism — non-attachment / Right Speech | Attachment and harmful speech are both restrained. | A | Do not build self around possession and contempt. |
| Hinduism — non-attachment and disciplined speech | Wealth and speech should remain governed by dharma. | B | Do not let possession or contempt dominate character. |
| Sikhism — rejection of greed, ego, and slander | These are major obstacles to truthful life. | A | Reject greed and degrading speech. |
| Confucianism — righteousness over profit | Wealth does not outrank virtue. | A/B | Character matters more than accumulation. |
| African Traditional | Wealth often carries communal obligations; ridicule norms vary. | B/C | Material success does not erase reciprocal responsibility. |

### Conclusion
The source links two forms of status domination: verbal degradation and material accumulation.

# E3 Unit 198 — Qur'an 107:1–3
## Ethical Failure Includes Neglect of Orphans and the Poor

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 107:1–3 associates denial of moral accountability with repulsing the orphan and failing to encourage feeding the poor.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Ethical seriousness is tested by treatment of vulnerable people.**
- **Do not push away those lacking protection.**
- **Communities should actively encourage provision of basic needs.**
- **Failure can consist not only in direct harm but in refusal to mobilise care.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Judaism — Deuteronomy 24:17–22 | Orphans and poor people receive explicit protection. | A | Build vulnerability protection into social life. |
| Buddhism — compassion and dana | Care for suffering and generosity are central. | A/B | Respond to need. |
| Hinduism — dana | Feeding and supporting others are important duties. | B | Use resources to relieve deprivation. |
| Sikhism — langar and seva | Feeding and service are institutional practices. | A | Organise care rather than merely praise it. |
| Confucianism — humane government | Subsistence of the people is a governance concern. | B | A humane society attends to material need. |
| African Traditional | Extended-family and communal support often protect vulnerable members. | B | Community bears responsibility for people without ordinary support. |

### Conclusion
The source reinforces the distinction between passive non-harm and positive responsibility.

# E3 Unit 199 — Qur'an 107:4–7
## Reject Performative Morality That Withholds Practical Help

### Qur'anic Source
Qur'an 107:4–7 criticises worship performed heedlessly or for display while people withhold even ordinary acts of assistance.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Public moral performance is not a substitute for practical ethical conduct.**
- **Do not use visible piety or virtue signalling to conceal lack of care.**
- **Small acts of assistance matter, not only dramatic generosity.**
- **Ethics should be evaluated partly by what a person actually does for others.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity — Matthew 6:1–5; James 2:15–17 | Performative piety and faith without practical help are criticised. | A | Moral display cannot substitute for action. |
| Judaism — prophetic critiques of ritual without justice | Ritual divorced from ethical conduct is repeatedly criticised. | A | Worship does not excuse injustice. |
| Buddhism | Ritual alone is insufficient without right conduct. | A/B | Practice matters more than display. |
| Hinduism | Traditions distinguish genuine disciplined action from ostentatious religious performance. | B | Do not substitute display for ethical substance. |
| Sikhism — rejection of empty ritualism | Outward religiosity without truthful living is strongly criticised. | A | Live ethics rather than perform them. |
| Confucianism — sincerity in ritual | Ritual without sincerity and humane conduct loses its purpose. | A/B | Form should embody substance. |
| African Traditional | Ritual and social duty are often interconnected, though traditions vary. | B/C | Public observance should not replace reciprocal obligation. |

### Conclusion
This is a direct anti-performativity principle and strongly mirrors earlier Qur'anic warnings against saying what one does not practise.

# E3 Unit 200 — Qur'an 109:1–6
## Maintain Deep Religious Difference Without Forced Merger

### Qur'anic Source
Surah al-Kafirun rejects worship compromise while ending with a clear separation between the religious commitments of the parties.

### Extracted Ethical Core
- **Peaceful coexistence need not require doctrinal merger.**
- **A person may maintain firm conviction without pretending incompatible beliefs are identical.**
- **Pluralism can preserve boundaries rather than erase them.**
- **Difference does not by itself require coercive conversion or false consensus.**

### Comparative Analysis

| Tradition / Text | Equivalent | Grade | Reduced Core |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Christianity | Traditions vary between exclusivist conviction and religious-tolerance principles. | C | Conviction and coexistence can be distinguished. |
| Judaism | Strong covenantal particularity has historically coexisted with recognition that outsiders need not become Jews. | B | Distinct communities can retain distinct religious obligations. |
| Buddhism | Multiple paths and traditions often coexist without a single conversion mandate, though history varies. | B | Difference need not require forced uniformity. |
| Hinduism | Strong pluralist strands recognise multiple religious/philosophical paths. | A/B | Coexistence need not erase doctrinal difference. |
| Sikhism | Distinct Sikh identity coexists with strong respect for other faiths and conscience. | A/B | Preserve conviction while respecting others' paths. |
| Confucianism | Less directly analogous as a doctrinal religion; coexistence with multiple traditions is historically common. | C | Social harmony need not require identical metaphysics. |
| African Traditional | Religious plurality and layered practice are common in many contexts, but no single rule applies. | C | Different communities can preserve distinct sacred commitments. |

### Conclusion
The source offers a boundary-preserving form of pluralism: **no forced synthesis is required for coexistence**.
