# EKC-10 — Peaceful Relations Between Ethically or Institutionally Different Civilisations — U1–U8 Derivation

**Experiment:** V1.2a — U-Kernel Counterfactual Concord Experiment  
**Case:** EKC-10  
**Status:** VERSION 1.0 — FROZEN PRE-COMPARISON DERIVATION  
**Date:** September 2026  
**Comparison boundary:** V1.2 intercivilisational architecture remains sealed.

---

# 1. Starting Question

What does U1–U8 require when distinct civilisations must coexist without sharing a constitution, government, institutional design or complete ethical interpretation?

The central difficulty is that peaceful relations cannot depend on one civilisation becoming the other.

If coexistence requires internal uniformity, peaceful cooperation becomes conditional upon assimilation.

But if internal autonomy is treated as absolute, a civilisation can externalise harm or domination while invoking sovereignty.

The architecture must therefore preserve:

**difference without impunity**

and:

**interaction without absorption.**

---

# 2. Root Activation

## U1 — Protection From Unjustified Harm — Dominant

Civilisational boundaries do not make harm ethically invisible.

Actions originating inside one civilisation can affect participants, environments and systems outside it.

U1 therefore creates cross-boundary constraints where significant harm is exported.

But U1 alone does not justify intervention merely because another civilisation is different.

---

## U2 — Reality-Responsive Epistemic Integrity — Dominant

Different civilisations may describe the same event through incompatible conceptual systems.

Cross-civilisational interaction therefore requires methods for distinguishing:

- observation;
- interpretation;
- translation;
- inference;
- disagreement;
- uncertainty.

Neither civilisation should automatically treat its own conceptual vocabulary as a complete representation of the other's.

---

## U3 — Protected Agency and Non-Domination — Dominant

Civilisations, communities and participants may legitimately organise themselves differently.

U3 strongly resists forced assimilation and unilateral domination.

Capability asymmetry is especially dangerous because a stronger civilisation may turn interoperability into dependency or protection into control.

---

## U4 — Care, Reciprocity and Support Under Need — Active

Cross-civilisational relations are not exhausted by non-aggression.

Severe need, disaster, displacement or dependency can create reasons for assistance and reciprocal support.

But support should not become a route to political ownership.

---

## U5 — Responsible Resource Stewardship — Active

Shared environments, infrastructure and resources generate cross-civilisational stewardship problems.

A civilisation cannot treat a common system as exclusively internal where its use materially affects others.

---

## U6 — Accountability, Correctability and Repair — Dominant

Agreements, translations and institutions can fail.

Cross-civilisational wrongs require attribution, contestability, correction and repair.

Agreements must also remain revisable when conditions change.

---

## U7 — Capability-Sensitive Development — Active

Different civilisations may possess radically different capabilities.

Capability differences can affect what coordination mechanisms are feasible.

But superior capability does not create superior standing or general authority.

Developmental assistance may be legitimate; developmental domination is not.

---

## U8 — Adaptive Continuity of Cooperative Function — Strong

Relations may persist across institutional change, technological change and succession.

Cooperative functions should remain recoverable even where particular treaties, interfaces or institutions become obsolete.

U8 also supports preservation of peaceful alternatives when integration fails.

---

# 3. Internal Uniformity Is Not Required

Nothing in U1–U8 requires every civilisation to implement the roots through identical institutions.

Different architectures may satisfy similar ethical constraints.

Therefore:

> **Peaceful coexistence does not require constitutional or institutional sameness.**

This is a foundational result.

The ethical question is not:

**Are they organised like us?**

It is:

**How do their actions affect protected interests within the relationship and beyond their boundaries?**

---

# 4. The Minimum Intercivilisational Boundary

A minimal cross-civilisational relationship appears to require at least:

1. restraint from unjustified cross-boundary harm;
2. respect for the other's agency and non-domination;
3. sufficient truthfulness for safe interaction;
4. accountability for cross-boundary effects;
5. stewardship of genuinely shared resources or systems;
6. some capacity to revise or terminate arrangements without unnecessary violence.

This does not amount to common government.

It is a **relational ethical boundary**.

---

# 5. Internal Autonomy and the Externality Boundary

A civilisation has strong reason to control its own internal architecture.

But internal classification does not erase external consequences.

This generates:

## **Externality Boundary Principle**

> **The stronger and more direct an action's effects outside a civilisation's legitimate internal domain, the weaker the claim that the matter is exclusively internal.**

Conversely, disagreement about primarily internal arrangements does not by itself create jurisdiction for outsiders.

This provides a gradient rather than a binary sovereignty rule.

---

# 6. Recognition Is Not Endorsement

Civilisations need to recognise that another civilisation exists, possesses institutions, makes claims and has participants without endorsing every internal practice.

Thus:

**recognition ≠ ethical approval.**

This distinction is necessary for coexistence among genuinely different systems.

Without it, diplomacy becomes conditional upon ideological agreement.

---

# 7. Translation Before Unification

Where conceptual systems differ, immediate unification is epistemically dangerous.

A term such as:

- person;
- property;
- citizen;
- responsibility;
- death;
- consent;
- ownership;
- intelligence;

may not map cleanly across systems.

Therefore U2 generates a **Translation Layer**.

Its purpose is not to declare one vocabulary correct.

It is to identify:

- approximate correspondences;
- non-equivalences;
- uncertainty;
- consequences of mismatch;
- concepts with no direct translation.

This is semantic interoperability before institutional integration.

---

# 8. Translation Must Preserve Non-Equivalence

A dangerous translation system can create false agreement.

Therefore:

> **Where concepts do not map cleanly, the mismatch itself must remain visible.**

A translation that forces every foreign concept into domestic categories may erase precisely the difference that matters.

This produces a principle of **Loss-Aware Translation**.

---

# 9. Rights Recognition Across Different Ontologies

The hardest case occurs where one civilisation recognises a being as a rights-bearing participant while another classifies the same kind of being as property, tool or non-person.

The roots do not generate a trivial solution.

U1 and U3 prevent the first civilisation from simply ignoring credible severe harm.

But U2 warns against assuming certainty where personhood or experience is genuinely uncertain.

A cross-civilisational architecture therefore needs a way to preserve protective caution without requiring complete ontological agreement.

This generates:

## **Protective Recognition Without Ontological Merger**

Where there is sufficiently credible reason that an entity may possess protected interests, interaction rules can extend protective treatment for cross-civilisational purposes without requiring every civilisation to adopt an identical metaphysical theory.

---

# 10. Jurisdictional Gap

Different legal systems can create spaces where each assumes the other is responsible.

This produces a **Jurisdictional Gap**:

> a situation in which a participant, event or harmful effect falls between systems such that no competent authority accepts responsibility for addressing it.

Cross-civilisational architecture should detect such gaps.

The ethical objective is not necessarily to create a superior authority.

It is to prevent responsibility from disappearing between boundaries.

---

# 11. Jurisdictional Overlap

The opposite problem also occurs.

Several civilisations may simultaneously claim authority over:

- a participant;
- transaction;
- shared infrastructure;
- event;
- externality.

This creates **Jurisdictional Overlap**.

Overlap can become domination if the strongest party simply prevails.

Therefore the architecture needs ways to identify:

- legitimate interests;
- location;
- consent;
- causal responsibility;
- affected parties;
- prior agreements;
- shared systems.

No single universal jurisdiction rule follows directly from U1–U8.

---

# 12. Dispute Resolution Without Supremacy

Cross-civilisational disputes require resolution mechanisms.

But requiring one civilisation's courts or constitution to be supreme over the other contradicts U3 unless voluntarily accepted.

The roots therefore favour **mutually accepted dispute processes** where possible.

These may involve:

- negotiated resolution;
- jointly selected evaluators;
- reciprocal procedures;
- temporary case-specific mechanisms;
- agreed evidential standards.

The exact institution is underdetermined.

The important constraint is:

> **resolution does not require permanent constitutional supremacy.**

---

# 13. Peaceful Distance

Cooperation is not always maximised by integration.

Where values, institutions or risk tolerances differ substantially, the least harmful stable arrangement may be limited interaction.

U3 + U8 therefore generate:

## **Peaceful Distance**

A civilisation may legitimately prefer low interaction provided that distance does not itself impose unjustified harm or domination on others.

Peaceful distance is therefore not failure.

It is one valid point in the cooperation space.

---

# 14. Peaceful Distance Versus Hostile Exclusion

Distance remains peaceful where it primarily governs one's own participation.

It becomes ethically problematic where it involves:

- aggression;
- coercive containment of outsiders;
- exported harm;
- forced dependency;
- denial of others' independent existence;
- interference with their peaceful alternatives.

Thus:

**we choose not to integrate**

is categorically different from:

**you are not permitted to exist independently.**

---

# 15. Variable Cooperation

The roots do not generate a binary choice between isolation and union.

Cooperation can vary by domain.

Two civilisations may:

- trade but not share governance;
- share research but not infrastructure;
- share infrastructure but retain separate law;
- coordinate environmental stewardship but restrict migration;
- maintain communication while avoiding economic integration.

This generates **Domain-Scoped Cooperation**.

---

# 16. Interface Before Merger

Where cooperation occurs, the architecture should first ask:

> **What minimum interface is required for this function?**

rather than:

> **How do we make the two civilisations structurally identical?**

This generates an **Interface-Minimisation Principle**:

> **Use the least integrative shared architecture sufficient for the cooperative function.**

This protects autonomy and reduces unnecessary dependency.

---

# 17. Interoperability Without Common Government

If two systems can exchange:

- information;
- goods;
- participants;
- services;
- commitments;

through agreed interfaces, common government is not necessarily required.

Thus:

> **shared protocol does not imply shared sovereignty.**

This follows directly from U3 and functional decomposition.

---

# 18. Interface Contract

A generic cross-civilisational interface should make visible:

- participants;
- purpose;
- exchanged resources/information;
- rights or protections relevant at the interface;
- responsibilities;
- evidence standards;
- externalities;
- dispute pathway;
- termination conditions;
- continuity requirements.

This is a functional abstraction rather than a prescribed treaty form.

---

# 19. Capability Asymmetry

A much stronger civilisation can dominate without formally governing.

Dependency may arise through:

- infrastructure;
- security;
- compute;
- trade;
- knowledge;
- medicine;
- transport;
- communication.

Therefore U3 + U5 generate **Asymmetry Awareness**.

An agreement is not necessarily non-dominating merely because both parties formally consent.

The architecture must consider whether one party controls the other's practical alternatives.

---

# 20. Dependency Leverage

This generates a specific failure mode:

## **Dependency Leverage**

A civilisation provides a critical capability and later uses withdrawal or control of that capability to extract unrelated concessions.

This can transform cooperation into domination without formal annexation.

Cross-civilisational design should therefore consider:

- exit capacity;
- substitutability;
- redundancy;
- transition time;
- dependency concentration.

---

# 21. Assistance Without Annexation

U4 can justify aid where another civilisation experiences disaster or severe need.

But:

> **help does not create ownership.**

Assistance should not automatically generate permanent authority over the recipient.

This mirrors the participant-level distinction between support and paternalism at civilisational scale.

---

# 22. Shared Resources

Where civilisations affect the same resource or environment, purely internal decision-making becomes inadequate.

U5 generates shared stewardship proportional to:

- causal contribution;
- dependence;
- vulnerability;
- ability to mitigate;
- future impact.

The exact governance mechanism remains underdetermined.

---

# 23. Cross-Boundary Harm

A civilisation cannot make harmful conduct ethically invisible by locating the initiating act inside its own territory or systems.

Where consequences cross boundaries, affected parties acquire legitimate standing in relation to those effects.

This does not imply authority over unrelated internal matters.

It implies:

**external effect → external accountability.**

---

# 24. Migration and Changing Membership

Participants may move between civilisations.

The roots suggest several constraints:

- departure should not automatically erase legitimate obligations already incurred;
- entry need not imply acceptance of every demand by the entrant;
- migration rules should not become arbitrary domination;
- participants should understand which rules will apply;
- identity and rights should not disappear merely during jurisdictional transition.

The exact migration architecture is not root-determined.

---

# 25. Cross-Civilisational Participant Continuity

A participant crossing systems may be described differently by each.

Therefore some minimal **portable identity/provenance** may be necessary for safe interaction.

But forcing a universal identity system could itself become centralising or exclusionary.

The requirement is therefore functional:

> enough continuity of identity, consent, obligation and protection to prevent participants disappearing between incompatible systems.

Implementation remains open.

---

# 26. Mixed Projects

Joint projects create temporary shared domains.

The roots generate the need to specify:

- purpose;
- contribution;
- authority;
- participant protections;
- information handling;
- resource stewardship;
- dispute resolution;
- exit;
- succession;
- failure responsibility.

A joint project should not silently become a superior government over its founding civilisations.

---

# 27. Agreement Mortality

Agreements can become obsolete.

U6 + U8 therefore generate:

> **Cross-civilisational agreements should be amendable, supersedable and terminable through legitimate processes while preserving necessary transition and accrued responsibilities.**

Agreement continuity protects function.

It should not create treaty immortality.

---

# 28. Exit Without Collapse

An architecture that allows entry into cooperation but no practical exit creates domination risk.

Therefore cooperation should consider **Exit Continuity**:

> how can a party leave or reduce integration without causing avoidable catastrophic failure for itself, the other party or shared participants?

This may require transition periods, data/resource portability, replacement interfaces or staged separation.

The exact mechanisms are engineering choices.

---

# 29. Forking Relations

If one civilisation changes internally or divides into successors, prior relationships may need reassessment.

U8 suggests preserving legitimate cooperative function where possible without assuming that institutional identity remains unchanged.

Thus succession requires:

- provenance;
- affected-party identification;
- reassessment of consent;
- continuity of necessary functions;
- ability to renegotiate.

---

# 30. Unknown Civilisation Contact

Contact with an unknown intelligence creates extreme epistemic uncertainty.

The roots do not justify immediate trust or immediate hostility.

A cautious initial architecture would favour:

1. observation;
2. low-risk communication;
3. explicit uncertainty;
4. reversible interaction;
5. limited dependency;
6. progressive exchange;
7. monitoring for harm and misunderstanding;
8. increasing cooperation only as evidence supports it.

This can be called **Progressive Intercivilisational Trust**.

---

# 31. Unknown Ontology

An unknown civilisation may not share concepts such as:

- individual;
- government;
- property;
- death;
- consent;
- agreement.

Therefore initial contact architecture must avoid assuming that familiar categories are universal.

U2 strongly supports preserving unresolved interpretation rather than prematurely forcing equivalence.

---

# 32. Cooperation Gradient

The derivation produces a general continuum:

**Awareness  
→ Non-Aggression  
→ Communication  
→ Limited Exchange  
→ Domain Cooperation  
→ Shared Infrastructure  
→ Deep Interoperability  
→ Optional Institutional Integration**

Movement along this gradient should be functional and voluntary rather than treated as inevitable progress.

More integration is not automatically better.

---

# 33. Relationship Reversibility

Under uncertainty, cross-civilisational relationships should avoid unnecessary irreversible dependency.

This produces:

> **Prefer relationships that can deepen as trust grows and contract without catastrophic harm if assumptions prove wrong.**

This is analogous to reversibility under emergency uncertainty but applied to civilisation-scale relations.

---

# 34. Heterogeneity as Potential Civilisational Asset

Difference creates coordination difficulty.

But it may also preserve:

- alternative knowledge;
- different failure modes;
- different technologies;
- different epistemic approaches;
- independent experiments;
- alternative institutional solutions.

Therefore complete homogenisation may destroy option value.

U2 + U7 + U8 suggest that **civilisational heterogeneity may itself contribute to collective resilience and discovery**.

This is a hypothesis, not proof.

It connects with the wider V1.2a cognitive-heterogeneity finding.

---

# 35. Architecture Generated

U1–U8 collectively generate a:

# **Plural Coexistence and Interoperability Architecture**

Its purpose is:

> **Enable distinct civilisations to coexist, communicate and cooperate where beneficial without requiring internal uniformity or common sovereignty, while constraining exported harm, domination, jurisdictional gaps, dependency leverage and failures of translation.**

---

# 36. Architectural Layers

## Layer 1 — Mutual Existence Recognition
Recognise distinct systems without requiring endorsement.

## Layer 2 — Non-Aggression / Externality Boundary
Constrain unjustified cross-boundary harm.

## Layer 3 — Translation
Map concepts while preserving uncertainty and non-equivalence.

## Layer 4 — Protective Recognition
Prevent rights/protection gaps where ontologies differ.

## Layer 5 — Domain-Scoped Interface
Define the minimum shared architecture required for a function.

## Layer 6 — Asymmetry and Dependency Review
Detect domination hidden inside formally voluntary cooperation.

## Layer 7 — Shared Stewardship
Coordinate genuinely shared resources and externalities.

## Layer 8 — Dispute and Jurisdiction Handling
Address gaps, overlaps and cross-boundary wrongs without requiring permanent supremacy.

## Layer 9 — Exit and Peaceful Distance
Preserve legitimate low-contact and withdrawal options.

## Layer 10 — Agreement Adaptation
Allow amendment, succession and termination.

## Layer 11 — Relationship Learning
Preserve provenance, failures and corrections.

## Layer 12 — Progressive Trust
Permit deeper cooperation where evidence supports it without making integration mandatory.

### F3 — Translation Collapse
Different concepts are falsely treated as equivalent.

### F4 — Translation Capture
One civilisation's vocabulary becomes the mandatory interpretive standard.

### F5 — Recognition-as-Endorsement Error
Acknowledging another system is treated as approval of all its practices.

### F6 — Jurisdictional Gap
No system accepts responsibility.

### F7 — Jurisdictional Pile-Up
Multiple systems impose conflicting authority.

### F8 — Interface Sovereignty
A shared interface expands into unagreed superior government.

### F9 — Dependency Leverage
Critical services are used to extract unrelated concessions.

### F10 — Capability Imperialism
Superior capability is treated as entitlement to rule.

### F11 — Aid Capture
Support during vulnerability becomes permanent influence or ownership.

### F12 — Shared-Resource Free Riding
One civilisation externalises stewardship costs.

### F13 — Exit Trap
Integration becomes practically irreversible.

### F14 — Exit Shock
Withdrawal causes avoidable catastrophic dependency failure.

### F15 — Agreement Fossilisation
Historical agreements survive after their legitimate function changes.

### F16 — Agreement Opportunism
A party abandons commitments whenever compliance becomes inconvenient.

### F17 — Peaceful-Distance Misclassification
Low contact is wrongly treated as hostility.

### F18 — Hostility-as-Distance
Aggressive exclusion is falsely described as peaceful separation.

### F19 — Personhood Gap
A participant loses protection between incompatible ontologies.

### F20 — Mixed-Project Capture
A joint institution acquires authority beyond its purpose.

### F21 — Strong-Party Forum Capture
The stronger civilisation controls dispute procedure.

### F22 — Unknown-Ontology Projection
Foreign intelligence is forced into familiar categories prematurely.

### F23 — Premature Trust
Deep dependency forms before adequate evidence exists.

### F24 — Permanent Suspicion
Precaution prevents any relationship from developing despite accumulating evidence.

### F25 — Homogenisation Fragility
Uniformity removes independent approaches and correlated-failure protection.

### F26 — Relationship Memory Loss
Successors repeat avoidable cross-civilisational failures.

### F27 — Cross-Border Responsibility Laundering
Actors exploit jurisdictional differences to make accountability disappear.

### F28 — Integration Ratchet
Every successful cooperative layer is assumed to justify deeper integration.

# 41. Frozen Pre-Comparison Result

> **U1–U8 generates a Plural Coexistence and Interoperability Architecture. Distinct civilisations need not share internal institutions, a common government or complete ethical interpretation in order to coexist. Cross-civilisational constraints arise principally at interfaces: exported harm, shared resources, participant transitions, incompatible rights concepts, dependencies, joint projects and disputes. The architecture therefore favours translation before unification, recognition without compulsory endorsement, domain-scoped interfaces, peaceful distance as a legitimate outcome, mutually accepted dispute processes without automatic supremacy, protection against dependency leverage, and reversible cooperation that can deepen or contract as evidence and trust change. Internal autonomy is strong but not an ethical shield for exported harm. More integration is not automatically better. Heterogeneity may itself preserve resilience and discovery value.**
