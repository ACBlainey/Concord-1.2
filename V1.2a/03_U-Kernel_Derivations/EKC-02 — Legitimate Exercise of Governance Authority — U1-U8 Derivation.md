# 1. Derivation Question

> **Under what conditions may an actor or institution legitimately exercise consequential authority over other participants or shared systems?**

The derivation begins only from the frozen problem conditions and U1–U8.

---

# 2. U1–U8 Activation Map

## U1 — Protection From Unjustified Harm

**STRONGLY ACTIVE**

Governance can prevent harms that uncoordinated action cannot prevent, but governance can itself inflict harm.

Authority therefore cannot be justified merely by its existence, tradition, popularity or capability.

A consequential intervention requires a defensible relationship between the harm or shared problem addressed and the burdens imposed.

---

## U2 — Reality-Responsive Epistemic Integrity

**STRONGLY ACTIVE**

Governance acts under uncertainty and can be wrong.

Legitimate decision processes must therefore remain connected to evidence, preserve uncertainty where relevant, permit competing explanations, monitor consequences and remain corrigible.

Authority cannot transform an assertion into truth merely by declaring it official.

---

## U3 — Protected Agency and Non-Domination

**STRONGLY ACTIVE**

Governance is inherently capable of constraining agency.

U3 does not prohibit all authority, because unconstrained actions by participants can themselves dominate or harm others.

It does require authority to be justified by function, bounded in scope, non-arbitrary and structured so participants do not become merely objects of institutional will.

---

## U4 — Care, Reciprocity and Support Under Need

**ACTIVE**

Governance cannot assess legitimacy only by aggregate output.

Participants facing dependency, vulnerability or asymmetric exposure may require protection or support even when they possess little bargaining power.

Collective systems also create reciprocal obligations where participants depend upon shared infrastructure and cooperation.

---

## U5 — Responsible Resource Stewardship

**ACTIVE**

Governance controls or influences shared resources and consumes resources itself.

Authority over resources must therefore remain connected to legitimate claims, sufficiency, shared function, non-exploitation and future consequences.

Institutional appetite is not sufficient justification for resource control.

---

## U6 — Accountability, Correctability and Repair

**STRONGLY ACTIVE**

Consequential authority must be answerable for its decisions.

Governance therefore requires attribution, challenge, correction, review of consequences and routes to repair wrongful outcomes.

Persistent immunity from correction is incompatible with U6.

---

## U7 — Capability-Sensitive Development

**ACTIVE**

Participants differ in knowledge and capability.

Governance may legitimately assign specialised functions to those able to perform them, but capability does not automatically confer unrestricted standing over others.

Participation and responsibility may require capability-appropriate forms.

Institutions themselves can learn, improve, degrade or become obsolete.

---

## U8 — Adaptive Continuity of Cooperative Function

**STRONGLY ACTIVE**

Some shared functions must persist across personnel change and temporary disruption.

However, preserving cooperative function is not equivalent to preserving a particular institution.

U8 therefore protects necessary function while permitting structures to adapt, be replaced or terminate.

This distinction becomes central to the derivation.

---

# 3. First Derivation: Why Authority May Exist

U1, U4, U5 and U8 together establish that some problems create ethically relevant shared obligations.

Where:

- actions generate serious externalities;
- shared infrastructure requires coordination;
- vulnerable participants require protection;
- common resources require stewardship;
- or necessary cooperative functions would otherwise fail,

purely isolated choice may be insufficient.

Therefore some capacity for collective decision and implementation can be ethically justified.

But U3 and U6 immediately constrain this conclusion.

The existence of a collective problem justifies only the **minimum authority reasonably necessary to perform the legitimate function**, not a general entitlement to rule.

This produces the first major principle:

> **Authority is derivative from legitimate function, not inherent in office, identity, intelligence, status or institutional existence.**

---

# 4. Derived Ethical Requirements

## R1 — Authority Requires a Legitimate Function

Every consequential authority must be able to identify the ethically legitimate function that justifies its power.

**Roots:** U1, U3, U4, U5, U8.

## R2 — Scope Must Track Function

Authority should extend no further than reasonably required to perform the function that justifies it.

**Roots:** U1, U3, U6.

## R3 — Authority Must Remain Non-Arbitrary

Comparable cases require relevant reasons for different treatment. Decisions cannot legitimately rest only upon preference of the authority-holder.

**Roots:** U2, U3, U6.

## R4 — Affected Agency Must Remain Material

Participants substantially affected by governance require meaningful ways to express interests, supply evidence, challenge errors and influence or contest decisions.

This does not imply that every participant has an unconditional veto.

**Roots:** U2, U3, U6, U7.

## R5 — Consent Strengthens Legitimacy but Cannot Be the Sole Test

Voluntary agreement is important because it protects agency.

However, collective externalities can make unanimous individual consent impossible as a universal requirement.

Conversely, nominal consent cannot legitimate severe domination where meaningful alternatives are absent.

**Roots:** U1, U3, U4.

## R6 — Expertise Grants Epistemic Weight, Not General Moral Sovereignty

Greater capability can justify greater functional responsibility where relevant to the task.

It does not by itself grant authority over unrelated domains or superior intrinsic standing.

**Roots:** U2, U3, U7.

## R7 — Authority Must Be Epistemically Corrigible

Decision systems must be able to receive new evidence, identify uncertainty, evaluate failures and revise conclusions.

**Roots:** U2, U6.

## R8 — Consequential Authority Must Be Accountable

Decisions must be attributable enough for affected participants or legitimate reviewing processes to determine what was decided, why and by whom or what process.

**Roots:** U2, U6.

## R9 — Harm to Minorities Cannot Be Justified Solely by Majority Preference

Numerical support does not transform unnecessary severe harm or domination into ethical legitimacy.

**Roots:** U1, U3, U4.

## R10 — Local Autonomy Ends Where Unjustified External Domination Begins

A community may govern many internal matters, but cannot claim autonomy as a justification for imposing serious unjustified externalities upon others.

**Roots:** U1, U3, U5.

## R11 — Institutional Continuity Is Conditional

A necessary function may require continuity.

The particular institution performing it has no independent ethical claim to permanence.

**Roots:** U6, U8.

## R12 — Authority Must Be Removable, Revisable or Terminable When Its Justification Fails

If an institution persistently fails, becomes captured, exceeds its function or loses the problem that justified it, continued authority requires new justification.

**Roots:** U2, U3, U6, U8.

## R13 — Governance Must Consider Future Participants

Present participants cannot treat future cooperative capacity and shared resources as ethically irrelevant merely because future participants cannot presently object.

**Roots:** U5, U8, supported by U1.

## R14 — Governance Burden Must Be Proportionate

Oversight and participation consume resources. Ethical governance requires enough process to protect legitimacy and correction without assuming maximal procedure is always better.

**Roots:** U5, U7, U8.

# 5. Derived Constraints

### C01 — No authority without an identifiable legitimate function.

### C02 — No authority may automatically expand because broader power would make its task easier.

### C03 — No office, substrate, intelligence level or historical status creates unrestricted authority.

### C04 — Expertise must be relevant to the function for which epistemic weight is granted.

### C05 — Authority must remain challengeable at a level proportionate to its consequences.

### C06 — Persistent unaccountable power is incompatible with legitimate governance even when it has previously produced good outcomes.

### C07 — Majority preference cannot by itself legitimate severe unnecessary harm or domination of minorities.

### C08 — Local autonomy cannot legitimise serious unjustified externalities.

### C09 — Institutional persistence must not be confused with preservation of necessary function.

### C10 — When a function disappears, the authority attached to it presumptively contracts or terminates.

### C11 — When authority expands, justification must be renewed for the expanded function.

### C12 — Governance must preserve routes for epistemic correction.

### C13 — Governance architecture should contain means to detect capture, failure and mission drift.

### C14 — Participants must retain meaningful avenues of nonviolent challenge, exit, replacement or correction where compatible with the legitimate shared function.

### C15 — Greater coercive or infrastructural power requires stronger accountability.

# 6. Candidate Mechanisms

The ethical requirements permit multiple implementations.

Candidate mechanisms include:

## M1 — Explicit Authority Mandates

Every authority has a defined function, scope and affected domain.

## M2 — Scope Boundary

Powers outside the mandate require separate justification rather than implied expansion.

## M3 — Review and Challenge Channels

Affected participants can contest evidence, procedure, scope or consequences.

## M4 — Independent Oversight

High-power functions can be inspected by actors not dependent upon the authority being reviewed.

## M5 — Distributed or Layered Decision Rights

Different functions can be assigned to different levels or institutions so authority is not automatically concentrated.

## M6 — Function-Specific Expertise

Experts can receive decision weight or delegated responsibility where their competence is relevant, while remaining bounded by mandate and accountability.

## M7 — Sunset or Renewal Conditions

Authorities created for bounded problems can expire or require renewed justification.

## M8 — Replacement and Succession Procedures

Necessary functions can continue while failed personnel or institutions are replaced.

## M9 — Outcome Monitoring

Governance decisions can be reviewed against observed consequences rather than only procedural compliance.

## M10 — Minority Protection Constraints

Decision procedures can contain boundaries preventing majorities from authorising specified forms of severe harm or domination.

## M11 — Externality Review

Decisions nominally internal to one community can trigger wider review when material effects cross its boundary.

## M12 — Transparent Justification

Consequential decisions can record their function, evidence, uncertainty, scope and rationale, subject to legitimate confidentiality.

## M13 — Forking, Exit or Alternative Jurisdiction Where Feasible

Where disagreement need not impose serious externalities, participants may be allowed meaningful institutional diversity rather than forced uniformity.

This mechanism is not universally possible and is not yet a required constitutional form.

# 7. Counterfactual Architecture

The roots generate a recognisable governance pattern:

# Function-Bounded, Corrigible and Regenerative Authority

Its central proposition is:

> **Legitimate authority exists to perform ethically justified cooperative functions; its scope is bounded by those functions, its exercise remains accountable and corrigible, and its institutional form has no independent claim to permanence.**

---

# 8. Architectural Layers

## 8.1 Function Layer

Before authority is granted, identify the shared problem or cooperative function requiring collective action.

This prevents “governance” itself from becoming the justification for governance.

## 8.2 Mandate Layer

The authority receives only the powers reasonably required for that function.

Different functions need not be bundled into the same institution.

## 8.3 Epistemic Layer

Decision processes must distinguish evidence, uncertainty, prediction and value conflict.

Relevant expertise receives weight without becoming general sovereignty.

## 8.4 Participation Layer

Affected participants require meaningful channels to provide information, express interests and challenge consequential decisions.

The exact participation mechanism is underdetermined.

## 8.5 Constraint Layer

Authority is bounded by harm, agency, non-domination, stewardship and accountability requirements that cannot be overridden merely by popularity or administrative convenience.

## 8.6 Oversight Layer

The greater the power and consequence of an authority, the stronger the need for independent scrutiny, provenance and challenge.

## 8.7 Correction Layer

Failed policies, incorrect assumptions and wrongful decisions must be revisable.

Where harm has occurred, repair should be possible where feasible.

## 8.8 Mortality Layer

Institutions are presumptively mortal.

Their **functions** may persist; their organisational form need not.

Mandates can contract, expire, transfer or terminate.

## 8.9 Regeneration Layer

If a necessary institution fails, the architecture should preserve enough constitutional or procedural capability to restore the legitimate function without requiring preservation of the failed authority.

## 8.10 Heterogeneity Layer

Where multiple arrangements can coexist without serious unjustified externalities, governance should not impose uniformity merely for administrative simplicity.

This follows primarily from U3, supported by U1 and U5.

---

# 9. Edge-Condition Results

## A. Popular Harm

Majority support is insufficient. U1/U3 constrain the decision. A legitimate governance system must contain protections that do not disappear merely because a harmful policy is popular.

## B. Expert Disagreement

Expertise can increase epistemic weight and functional responsibility, but does not independently create general authority. The affected population's agency remains ethically relevant.

## C. Institutional Self-Expansion

Rejected unless the new function is independently justified and the expanded scope is proportionate.

## D. Successful but Unaccountable Authority

Past success does not justify permanent immunity from scrutiny. U2 and U6 require continued corrigibility and accountability.

## E. Persistent Minority

Repeated loss is not automatically illegitimate, but if the arrangement produces systematic domination or severe avoidable harm, the architecture must provide protection, accommodation, decentralisation, exit or another remedy where feasible.

## F. Local Externality

Local autonomy is constrained by material consequences imposed beyond the local boundary.

## G. Obsolete Institution

The institution presumptively loses authority when its legitimate function disappears, unless a new function is independently justified.

## H. Long-Term Decision

U5 and U8 require future cooperative capacity and resource consequences to enter present decision-making.

## I. Capability Difference

Greater capability can justify specialised responsibility. It cannot alone justify superior standing or unrestricted rule.

## J. Institutional Failure

The architecture should distinguish preservation of necessary function from preservation of the failed institution. Replacement, transfer or regeneration is ethically preferable to institutional immunity.
