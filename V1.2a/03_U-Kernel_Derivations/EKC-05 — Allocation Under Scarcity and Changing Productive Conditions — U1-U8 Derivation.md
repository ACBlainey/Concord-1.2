# 1. Derivation Question

Using only U1–U8 and the frozen EKC-05 neutral problem:

> **What allocation architecture is ethically required when resources, needs and productive conditions change—and what remains an empirical or engineering choice rather than a consequence of root ethics?**

No existing Concord economic mechanism is assumed.

---

# 2. Root Activation

## U1 — Protection From Unjustified Harm

**Strongly active.**

Allocation can expose participants to severe harm through deprivation, exclusion, dangerous dependency or externalised cost.

Scarcity does not remove the harm constraint; it changes what outcomes may be avoidable.

## U2 — Reality-Responsive Epistemic Integrity

**Active.**

Allocation depends upon claims about:

- actual scarcity;
- actual need;
- productive capacity;
- environmental cost;
- externalities;
- future consequences;
- and system performance.

False models can make apparently fair rules harmful.

## U3 — Protected Agency and Non-Domination

**Strongly active.**

Control of essential resources can become control of participants.

An allocation system must therefore constrain resource-based domination and preserve meaningful participant choice where compatible with legitimate claims of others.

## U4 — Care, Reciprocity and Support Under Need

**Strongly active.**

Serious need can create an ethically relevant claim even where equivalent contribution is absent.

This is one of the clearest U4 activations in the experiment so far.

## U5 — Responsible Resource Stewardship

**Dominant / strongly active.**

Resources should be acquired, controlled, allocated, preserved and used with regard to:

- legitimate claims;
- sufficiency;
- function;
- non-exploitation;
- and future consequences.

This directly governs the case.

## U6 — Accountability, Correctability and Repair

**Strongly active.**

Allocation systems produce errors, incentives, concentrations and unintended consequences.

They must remain inspectable, contestable and correctable.

## U7 — Capability-Sensitive Development

**Active.**

Differences in present productive capability should not automatically become permanent status categories.

Where capability can develop, architecture should preserve routes for development and participation.

## U8 — Adaptive Continuity of Cooperative Function

**Strongly active.**

Allocation architecture must survive changing scarcity and technology without treating current economic mechanisms as permanent ends.

The function to preserve is legitimate cooperative access and stewardship, not any particular historical mechanism.

# 3. First Derivation Result — No Single Root Allocation Formula

The kernel does not generate a single scalar rule such as:

- equal allocation;
- allocation by contribution;
- allocation by need;
- allocation by ownership;
- allocation by price;
- allocation by authority;
- or allocation by welfare maximisation.

Instead, several legitimate claims coexist.

At minimum:

1. **Sufficiency claim** — serious basic need creates a claim against avoidable deprivation.
2. **Agency claim** — participants have a claim to meaningful control over resources legitimately available to them.
3. **Contribution claim** — productive contribution can create a legitimate claim to benefit, recognition or discretionary access.
4. **Stewardship claim** — present use is constrained by depletion, externalities and future cooperative function.
5. **Dependency claim** — control over essential bottlenecks carries obligations because it creates asymmetric power.
6. **Common-function claim** — resources necessary to shared systems may carry claims that exceed individual preference.
7. **Future claim** — foreseeable future participants and future system function constrain destructive present consumption.

These claims can conflict.

The architecture therefore requires **plural claim balancing**, not a single universal allocation metric.

# 4. Derived Principles

## P1 — Avoidable Severe Deprivation Has Priority Over Low-Stakes Preference

Where a scarce resource is necessary to prevent serious harm, claims based on urgent need gain weight over claims based solely on marginal preference.

This does not imply unlimited entitlement.

Scarcity may make some harm unavoidable.

The requirement is to avoid **unjustified** harm.

## P2 — Need Does Not Require Equivalent Prior Contribution

U4 prevents a civilisation from making all legitimate support conditional upon equivalent productive contribution.

Children, injured participants, temporarily dependent participants, developing participants or those facing genuine incapacity can possess legitimate claims independent of current output.

## P3 — Contribution May Matter Without Becoming Moral Rank

Contribution can legitimately affect allocation where doing so supports reciprocity, production, incentive or recognition.

But productive output does not establish greater intrinsic standing.

## P4 — Control of Essentials Creates Duties

Where a participant or institution controls a resource or infrastructure upon which others critically depend, that control creates ethical obligations.

Ownership or possession alone cannot erase the non-domination and harm implications of dependency.

## P5 — Scarcity Is Contextual, Not Permanent by Definition

Allocation mechanisms should track actual scarcity.

A mechanism justified by scarcity may lose justification when production changes.

## P6 — Abundance Should Relax Unnecessary Allocation Control

If the marginal cost and scarcity of a resource collapse, restrictions whose justification depended upon scarcity should be reconsidered.

Administrative scarcity should not be preserved merely because institutions developed around it.

## P7 — Common Resources Require Stewardship

Where individually rational use cumulatively degrades a shared resource, U5 permits and may require coordination.

## P8 — Future Consequences Constrain Present Consumption

Present participants may not treat foreseeable future cooperative capacity as ethically irrelevant.

## P9 — Allocation Must Not Become a Domination System

Access to essentials should not be manipulable as a general mechanism for coercing unrelated behaviour.

## P10 — Local Information Has Ethical Relevance

Where allocation depends upon participant circumstances, architecture should preserve routes for relevant local information to correct aggregate models.

This follows primarily from U2 and U3.

## P11 — Allocation Metrics Remain Representations

Measures of:

- need;
- contribution;
- productivity;
- scarcity;
- efficiency;
- or value

can become distorted.

No metric should become self-validating merely because the system uses it.

## P12 — Mechanisms Must Adapt When Conditions Change

U8 strongly rejects preservation of an allocation mechanism merely because it is historically established.

The ethical function is continuity of legitimate cooperation, not continuity of the mechanism.

## P13 — Allocation Power Must Be Accountable

Actors able to determine others' access to consequential resources exercise power.

That power requires challenge, correction and constraints proportionate to consequence.

## P14 — Emergency Allocation May Differ From Ordinary Allocation

Acute scarcity may justify temporary restrictions or prioritisation that would be unjustified under normal conditions.

Emergency mechanisms should remain tied to the scarcity condition that justified them.

## P15 — Allocation Architecture Has a Resource Cost

Information gathering, enforcement, dispute resolution, monitoring and administration consume resources.

An allocation system that consumes excessive resources in pursuit of marginal optimisation can violate stewardship.

## P16 — Productive Change Should Propagate Into Social Obligations

If technology radically changes the relationship between labour and production, social claims should be reconsidered rather than preserving obsolete assumptions about contribution.

Automation is therefore ethically relevant because it changes facts, not because U1–U8 prescribes a predetermined post-labour economy.

# 5. Derived Claim Hierarchy

The roots do not generate a rigid total ordering, but they do support a conditional hierarchy.

When resources are acutely scarce:

[
SeriousHarmPrevention
>
MarginalPreference
]

subject to feasibility and competing serious claims.

Beyond that, claims remain contextual.

For example:

- need may outweigh contribution where survival is at issue;
- contribution may matter strongly for discretionary goods;
- stewardship may override both where depletion threatens future function;
- agency may constrain otherwise efficient central allocation;
- externalities may limit otherwise legitimate private use.

Therefore:

> **Allocation legitimacy is multi-dimensional and context-sensitive rather than reducible to one distributive principle.**

# 6. Counterfactual Architecture

The derivation produces:

# **Adaptive Sufficiency, Agency and Stewardship Architecture**

Core rule:

> **Resources should be coordinated so that serious avoidable deprivation is constrained, meaningful agency is preserved, productive contribution can matter without becoming moral rank, common and future resources are stewarded, resource power cannot become arbitrary domination, and allocation mechanisms adapt when scarcity or productive conditions change.**

The architecture has eight functional layers.

## Layer 1 — Resource Reality

Determine relevant facts:

- what exists;
- how scarce it actually is;
- what can be substituted;
- what production requires;
- what dependencies exist;
- and what consequences use creates.

## Layer 2 — Claim Recognition

Recognise relevant claims arising from:

- need;
- agency;
- contribution;
- dependency;
- common function;
- stewardship;
- and future consequence.

## Layer 3 — Scarcity Classification

Different allocation logic may legitimately apply to:

- essential scarcity;
- ordinary scarcity;
- discretionary scarcity;
- abundance;
- and emergency scarcity.

The categories must remain corrigible.

## Layer 4 — Allocation

Select mechanisms capable of reconciling relevant claims under actual conditions.

The kernel does not determine the mechanism.

## Layer 5 — Anti-Domination

Detect whether resource control creates coercive dependency, exclusion or structurally unchallengeable power.

## Layer 6 — Externality and Commons Stewardship

Account for consequences displaced onto:

- other participants;
- common systems;
- environments;
- or future participants.

## Layer 7 — Correction

Monitor outcomes, gaming, model error, hidden deprivation, over-allocation, under-allocation and concentration.

## Layer 8 — Adaptation

When scarcity, technology or participant capability changes, reconsider the allocation mechanism rather than merely recalibrating an obsolete one.

# 7. Treatment of Edge Cases

## Essential Scarcity

Priority should shift toward serious harm prevention and legitimate essential need.

Where all needs cannot be met, the system must make the unavoidable conflict explicit rather than pretending no harm occurs.

No single rationing formula is derivable from the roots alone.

## Luxury Scarcity

Need has less force.

Agency, contribution, voluntary exchange and other allocation mechanisms have wider permissible scope provided they do not create unjustified external harms or domination.

## Sudden Abundance

Scarcity-dependent restrictions lose justification.

The system should reduce unnecessary control rather than preserve artificial scarcity for institutional convenience.

## Automation Shock

If labour ceases to be necessary for production, contribution rules tied to labour require re-examination.

The kernel does not determine a specific income or ownership system.

## Contribution Without Need

Contribution may create legitimate claims, but not unlimited claims.

Stewardship, externalities and domination constraints remain active.

## Need Without Contribution

Serious legitimate need can create a claim independent of equivalent contribution.

Support should preserve agency and, where relevant, future capability rather than convert need into permanent subordination.

## Common Resource Depletion

Coordination is justified because private action creates cumulative external harm and threatens common/future function.

## Productive Bottleneck

Control over essential infrastructure creates obligations and accountability proportional to dependency.

The roots do not determine whether the correct response is regulation, shared control, redundancy, competition, public provision or another mechanism.

## Local Information

Aggregate allocation models must remain correctable by materially relevant local evidence.

## Intergenerational Conflict

Current benefit is constrained where foreseeable depletion would seriously impair future cooperative function.

## Allocation Gaming

Metrics and incentives must remain contestable.

When behaviour adapts to the metric, the allocation model must be revalidated against its underlying purpose.

## Allocation Failure

Observed hidden harms become evidence against the mechanism even if its internal performance metrics appear successful.

# 10. U8 Dependence

U8 is more significant here than in EKC-01–04.

Without U8, U1–U7 still generate:

- sufficiency;
- non-domination;
- care under need;
- stewardship;
- accountability;
- and capability sensitivity.

But U8 adds a stronger requirement:

> **preserve legitimate cooperative resource function while allowing the allocation mechanism itself to change.**

This becomes particularly important under:

- automation;
- technological abundance;
- environmental disruption;
- demographic change;
- new substrates;
- and catastrophic supply shifts.

U8 therefore opposes both:

- destructive instability;
- and obsolete institutional permanence.

It generates **adaptive continuity**, not preservation of a particular economy.

# 12. First-Pass Failure Modes

Before comparison with V1.2, the derived architecture itself exposes at least the following risks.

## F1 — Sufficiency Creep

A guaranteed floor may progressively redefine discretionary preferences as essential claims.

## F2 — Contribution Capture

Powerful actors may define “contribution” in ways that privilege their own activity.

## F3 — Need Gaming

Participants may adapt behaviour to systems that measure need.

## F4 — Metric Substitution

The measure of scarcity, contribution or welfare may replace the underlying reality.

## F5 — Administrative Overreach

Attempting precise contextual allocation may require intrusive information and excessive control.

## F6 — Dependency Lock-In

Support mechanisms may unintentionally make continued dependency structurally advantageous or difficult to exit.

## F7 — Bottleneck Capture

Institutions created to control essential bottlenecks may themselves become bottlenecks.

## F8 — Artificial Scarcity

Actors benefiting from scarcity may resist abundance or preserve restrictions after their justification disappears.

## F9 — Commons Overconstraint

Stewardship rules may become so restrictive that beneficial use and innovation are suppressed.

## F10 — Future-Claim Inflation

Appeals to hypothetical future participants may be used to override present agency without adequate evidence.

## F11 — Emergency Persistence

Temporary scarcity controls may survive after the emergency ends.

## F12 — Allocation Complexity

Balancing many legitimate claims may create an incomprehensible system vulnerable to specialist capture.

## F13 — Localism Failure

Local knowledge may improve fit but conceal externalities imposed outside the local decision boundary.

## F14 — Centralism Failure

System-wide coordination may see externalities but erase relevant local variation.

## F15 — Automation Lag

Institutions may preserve labour-linked allocation after labour is no longer a reliable proxy for contribution or scarcity.

## F16 — Abundance Shock

Institutions funded or legitimised by managing scarcity may resist technological changes that reduce their necessity.

## F17 — Optimisation Fragility

Highly efficient allocation may remove buffers and redundancy required for resilience.

## F18 — Ethical Goodhart Failure

Once ethical concepts such as need, contribution or stewardship become operational metrics, optimisation against those metrics may distort the ethical purpose they were intended to serve.

# 15. Pre-Comparison Result

The frozen counterfactual result is:

# **Adaptive Sufficiency, Agency and Stewardship Architecture**

Its strongest finding is not a specific economic system.

It is the refusal of the roots to collapse legitimate resource claims into one universal allocation rule.

The architecture instead requires:

- protection against severe avoidable deprivation;
- meaningful agency;
- legitimate recognition of contribution without moral hierarchy;
- support under genuine need;
- stewardship of commons and future resources;
- constraints on resource-based domination;
- reality-responsive allocation;
- accountable correction;
- and adaptation when productive conditions change.

The exact economic machinery remains highly underdetermined.
