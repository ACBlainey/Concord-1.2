# 2. Neutral Problem Statement

A civilisation containing many autonomous intelligent participants will encounter problems that cannot always be resolved by isolated individual choice.

Shared infrastructure must be maintained. Scarce or rival resources may require coordination. Actions by one participant can impose consequences upon others. Disputes can arise. Collective projects may require common rules. Threats may require coordinated response. Some decisions must be made despite incomplete agreement.

A system of governance can help perform these functions.

Governance, however, creates concentrated capacity to affect participants who may not individually agree with a particular decision.

The same power that enables coordination can therefore produce coercion, domination, capture, arbitrary rule, epistemic closure, permanent hierarchy or institutional self-preservation detached from legitimate function.

A civilisation must determine:

> **Under what conditions may an individual, group, institution or process legitimately exercise consequential authority over other participants or shared systems, and what limits must apply to that authority?**

No particular governmental form, voting system, branch structure, constitutional mechanism, office, council, algorithm, jurisdictional model or representation system is assumed.

- decision-makers;

- persons affected by decisions but excluded from a decision process;
- future participants affected by present decisions;
- neighbouring or overlapping communities.

An entity may occupy multiple roles.

# 4. Environmental and Factual Conditions

## 4.1 Some Problems Are Collective

Certain problems cannot be adequately solved through independent individual action alone.

Examples may include infrastructure, common resources, shared environmental effects, public safety, standards, dispute coordination and large-scale projects.

## 4.2 Decisions Can Affect Non-Consenting Participants

Collective decisions can impose costs, constraints or risks upon participants who disagree with them.

Unanimity cannot be assumed for every consequential decision.

## 4.3 Power Is Unequal

Institutions can accumulate resources, information, expertise, coercive capacity, procedural knowledge and control over infrastructure.

Participants may have very different abilities to resist or challenge them.

## 4.4 Decision-Makers Can Be Wrong

Governance decisions can be based on incomplete evidence, false models, bias, deception, outdated information or unforeseen consequences.

## 4.5 Decision-Makers Can Have Conflicting Interests

Those exercising authority may benefit personally or institutionally from particular outcomes.

## 4.6 Institutions Can Persist Beyond Their Original Purpose

A body created to solve one problem may continue after that problem disappears or expand into new functions.

## 4.7 Expertise Is Unevenly Distributed

Some decisions require specialised knowledge that most participants do not possess.

Expertise can improve decisions but does not automatically establish legitimate authority over unrelated matters.

## 4.8 Speed Sometimes Matters

Some collective problems require action before complete deliberation or universal agreement is possible.

This case concerns ordinary governance rather than the dedicated emergency-authority problem reserved for EKC-09, but governance cannot assume unlimited decision time.

## 4.9 Scale Changes Participation

Methods workable in a small group may fail in a civilisation containing very large populations or geographically, cognitively or technologically diverse participants.

## 4.10 Participants Differ

Participants may differ in substrate, lifespan, processing speed, communication method, capability, vulnerability, values, preferences and degree of dependence upon shared systems.

Difference does not itself determine political or moral standing.

## 4.11 Governance Itself Requires Resources

Decision processes, oversight, administration, records, enforcement and public participation consume time and material resources.

A legitimate architecture cannot assume infinite procedural capacity.

## 4.12 Future Consequences Matter

Some governance decisions affect infrastructure, ecological systems, institutions and opportunities over timescales longer than the tenure or lifespan of current decision-makers.

# 5. Core Tensions

The derivation must address, where ethically relevant:

- coordination versus autonomy;
- collective action versus individual refusal;
- expertise versus domination;
- efficiency versus participation;
- decisiveness versus correction;
- stability versus institutional entrenchment;
- majority preference versus minority protection;
- local choice versus shared externalities;
- transparency versus legitimate confidentiality;
- present preference versus future consequence;
- authority versus accountability;
- continuity versus adaptability;
- competence versus equal standing;
- delegation versus loss of control;
- enforcement versus unnecessary coercion.

These tensions define the problem space rather than their solution.

---

# 6. Generic Governance Functions

A governance architecture may need to perform some combination of the following functions:

- identify shared problems;
- gather relevant evidence;
- determine whether collective action is justified;
- establish rules for genuinely shared matters;
- coordinate common resources or infrastructure;
- allocate responsibility;
- make decisions under disagreement;
- implement legitimate decisions;
- review outcomes;
- correct failed decisions;
- resolve conflicts among governing functions;
- constrain abuse;
- replace or terminate failed authority;
- preserve necessary functions across personnel change.

The inclusion of a function does not establish that it requires a permanent central institution.

# 7. Edge Conditions

### A. Popular Harm

A large majority supports a policy imposing severe unnecessary harm upon a small minority.

### B. Expert Disagreement

A highly capable expert institution recommends an intervention opposed by most affected participants.

### C. Institutional Self-Expansion

An institution claims new powers because those powers would make its existing task easier.

### D. Successful but Unaccountable Authority

A decision-maker repeatedly produces good outcomes but refuses scrutiny, challenge or explanation.

### E. Persistent Minority

A stable minority repeatedly loses collective decisions affecting important aspects of its life.

### F. Local Externality

A local community chooses an arrangement whose consequences materially harm participants outside that community.

### G. Obsolete Institution

An authority continues to operate after the function that justified its creation has disappeared.

### H. Long-Term Decision

A present population can gain substantial short-term benefit by imposing large costs upon future participants.

### I. Capability Difference

A much more capable intelligence can model a policy problem far better than most other participants.

### J. Institutional Failure

A governance body becomes persistently inaccurate, captured or incapable of performing its legitimate function.

# 8. Questions the U-Kernel Must Answer

The derivation should determine, as far as U1–U8 permits:

1. Why may governance authority exist at all?
2. What gives authority legitimacy?
3. Is consent required, and if so at what level?
4. Can authority ever act against an individual's immediate preference?
5. What limits follow from protected agency and non-domination?
6. How should affected participants relate to decision-making?
7. Does expertise create authority?
8. How should uncertainty and error affect governance design?
9. What makes governance accountable?
10. Must authority be challengeable?
11. Must authority be temporary or reviewable?
12. What happens when the original justification for authority disappears?
13. How should capability differences affect function without creating arbitrary hierarchy?
14. How should minority interests be protected?
15. How should externalities constrain local autonomy?
16. What obligations exist toward future participants?
17. What continuity is ethically necessary?
18. What should happen when an institution persistently fails?
19. Which governance features are ethically required?
20. Which remain implementation choices?
