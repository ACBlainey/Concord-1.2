# 1. Purpose

This packet defines a neutral civilisational allocation problem for derivation from the frozen U1–U8 kernel.

It deliberately does not assume a particular economic system, ownership regime, currency, market structure, planning mechanism, welfare system, abundance model or existing Concord solution.

The case is intentionally high-underdetermination.

Its purpose is to test how far ethical roots alone constrain resource architecture and where empirical, technological and engineering choices become necessary.

---

# 2. Primary Problem

A civilisation contains participants with different needs, capabilities, preferences, locations and productive capacities.

Resources differ in:

- abundance;
- scarcity;
- renewability;
- divisibility;
- substitutability;
- production cost;
- storage cost;
- transportability;
- urgency;
- and importance to basic function.

Productive conditions also change.

A resource that is scarce today may become abundant.

A previously abundant resource may become scarce.

Automation may reduce the labour required for some goods while leaving other bottlenecks unchanged.

Environmental shocks may destroy supply.

New technologies may create new resources, new dependencies or new concentrations of productive power.

The primary question is:

> **How should a civilisation coordinate access to, use of and responsibility for resources when scarcity, need, productive capacity and technological conditions differ and change over time?**

---

# 3. Generic Conditions

The architecture should remain coherent where:

- some resources are essential for survival;
- some are desirable but non-essential;
- some are abundant;
- some are irreducibly scarce;
- scarcity differs by location;
- production requires effort, skill, infrastructure or risk;
- participants contribute differently;
- participants begin from unequal circumstances;
- some participants temporarily or permanently cannot contribute at the same level as others;
- preferences differ;
- future participants have interests in resources consumed today;
- private use can impose external costs;
- common resources can be depleted;
- infrastructure can create monopoly or dependency;
- automation can make some labour economically unnecessary;
- productive technology can change rapidly;
- measurement of need and contribution is imperfect;
- allocation systems themselves consume resources;
- and allocation rules can change participant behaviour.

---

# 4. Core Tensions

The problem contains tensions between:

- need and contribution;
- sufficiency and preference;
- individual control and common claims;
- present use and future availability;
- efficiency and resilience;
- equality and relevant difference;
- autonomy and coordination;
- reward and dependency;
- local knowledge and system-wide visibility;
- abundance and residual scarcity;
- productive incentive and exploitation;
- accumulation and stewardship;
- emergency allocation and ordinary allocation;
- stability and adaptation;
- simplicity and contextual accuracy;
- allocation optimisation and participant dignity;
- resource conservation and beneficial use.

---

# 5. Required Questions

A satisfactory derivation should address:

1. Are some resource claims stronger than others?
2. Does basic need create a claim independent of productive contribution?
3. Does contribution create a legitimate claim?
4. What limits apply to accumulation or control?
5. How should common or non-renewable resources be treated?
6. How should future consequences affect present allocation?
7. How should the system respond when scarcity changes?
8. What happens when automation reduces the need for labour?
9. How should participants unable to contribute be treated?
10. How should allocation account for local knowledge and different circumstances?
11. When may collective coordination override individual resource preference?
12. How should externalities be handled?
13. How should productive infrastructure be governed where many participants depend upon it?
14. How should errors in allocation be detected and corrected?
15. How should the system avoid creating permanent domination through resource control?
16. How should emergency scarcity differ from ordinary conditions?
17. How should the cost of the allocation mechanism itself be constrained?
18. What information is legitimately required to allocate resources?
19. How should experimentation with alternative allocation mechanisms occur?
20. What should remain deliberately underdetermined until empirical conditions are known?

# 6. Edge Cases

## 6.1 Essential Scarcity

There is temporarily insufficient supply of a resource necessary for basic survival to satisfy all normal demand.

## 6.2 Luxury Scarcity

A highly desired resource is scarce but not necessary for basic functioning.

## 6.3 Sudden Abundance

Technology makes a previously expensive good extremely cheap to produce.

## 6.4 Automation Shock

A large category of socially useful production no longer requires significant participant labour.

## 6.5 Contribution Without Need

One participant contributes substantially to production but already possesses more than sufficient resources.

## 6.6 Need Without Contribution

A participant has serious legitimate needs but currently cannot make an equivalent productive contribution.

## 6.7 Common Resource Depletion

Individually beneficial use cumulatively threatens a resource upon which many participants depend.

## 6.8 Productive Bottleneck

A small number of participants or systems control infrastructure essential to wider production.

## 6.9 Local Information

A central allocation model indicates one distribution while participants close to the problem possess material contextual information the model lacks.

## 6.10 Intergenerational Conflict

Current participants can increase present welfare by consuming resources or degrading systems needed by future participants.

## 6.11 Allocation Gaming

Participants learn how an allocation rule measures need or contribution and alter behaviour to maximise their allocation without creating corresponding value.

## 6.12 Allocation Failure

An apparently successful allocation system produces hidden harms that become visible only after prolonged use.
