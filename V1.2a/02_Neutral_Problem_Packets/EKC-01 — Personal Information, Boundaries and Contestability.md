# 2. Neutral Problem Statement

A civilisation composed of intelligent participants will generate, observe, infer, store and exchange information about those participants.

Some information may be supplied voluntarily. Some may arise from participation in shared systems. Some may be observed by others. Some may be inferred from behaviour or combined from otherwise innocuous data.

Information about a participant can be useful for legitimate cooperative purposes, including communication, coordination, safety, service provision, resource planning, research, dispute resolution and adaptation.

The same information can also create power over the person or entity to whom it relates.

Information may be inaccurate, incomplete, misleading, obsolete, decontextualised or correctly recorded but used for a purpose materially different from the purpose for which it was originally obtained.

Participants may differ greatly in their ability to understand information systems, inspect records, challenge decisions, predict downstream uses or resist organisations possessing much greater informational and computational capability.

A civilisational system must therefore determine:

> **Under what conditions may information about a participant be acquired, retained, inferred, combined, used, shared and acted upon, and what protections are required when such information can materially affect that participant?**

No particular ownership model, privacy model, data architecture, access system, correction process, institutional design or technical mechanism is assumed.

# 3. Relevant Agents

The problem may involve:

- the participant to whom information relates;
- other individual participants;
- voluntary associations;
- service providers;
- researchers;
- economic organisations;
- public or civil institutions;
- adjudicative bodies;
- safety or emergency functions;
- automated decision systems;
- artificial intelligences;
- custodians of shared infrastructure;
- future institutions receiving inherited records.

An entity may occupy several roles simultaneously.

# 4. Environmental and Factual Conditions

For this experiment, assume the following conditions.

## 4.1 Information Has Consequences

Information can affect access to opportunities, resources, services, relationships, reputation, mobility, participation, investigation, intervention and institutional decisions.

## 4.2 Information Can Be Wrong

Records and inferences can contain errors.

Even factually correct observations may support false conclusions when context is missing.

## 4.3 Information Persists

Digital information can survive long after the circumstances that produced it have changed.

Persistence can make obsolete information consequential.

## 4.4 Information Replicates

Once information has been copied or distributed, complete retrieval or deletion may be difficult or impossible.

## 4.5 Information Can Be Combined

Individually low-risk observations may become highly consequential when aggregated.

## 4.6 Information Can Be Inferred

A participant need not explicitly disclose a fact for another system to estimate or infer it.

## 4.7 Capability Is Unequal

Participants and institutions may differ dramatically in computational power, legal knowledge, technical literacy, resources, bargaining power and ability to detect misuse.

## 4.8 Some Shared Information Is Necessary

A complex civilisation cannot operate if every fact relating to a participant is unavailable to every other participant or institution.

Coordination therefore requires some legitimate information flows.

## 4.9 Some Non-Consensual Processing May Be Claimed Necessary

Situations may arise in which obtaining prior agreement is impossible, impractical or alleged to conflict with prevention of serious harm, adjudication, infrastructure operation or another legitimate function.

The ethical validity and boundaries of such claims are not predetermined.

## 4.10 Participants Change

Preferences, circumstances, capabilities, relationships and past behaviour may change over time.

A civilisation must therefore confront the possibility that persistent information can preserve an outdated representation of a changing participant.

## 4.11 Different Substrates May Produce Different Information Profiles

Biological, artificial, hybrid and other intelligences may generate very different quantities and kinds of observable data.

The problem must not assume that human-style personal information is the only relevant form.

## 4.12 Complete Secrecy and Complete Transparency Are Both Impractical

Absolute secrecy can obstruct necessary cooperation, accountability and safety.

Absolute transparency can expose participants to manipulation, coercion, exploitation and loss of meaningful autonomy.

The problem therefore concerns justified boundaries rather than either absolute.

# 5. Core Tensions

The counterfactual derivation must address, where ethically relevant:

- useful information versus informational power;
- coordination versus individual boundaries;
- consent versus necessary shared function;
- accuracy versus uncertainty and inference;
- persistence versus personal change;
- institutional memory versus obsolete representation;
- accountability versus confidentiality;
- safety versus surveillance;
- collective learning versus exploitation of participants;
- efficiency versus meaningful challenge;
- legitimate asymmetry versus domination;
- present use versus future reuse;
- individual interests versus consequences for others.

These tensions identify the problem space. They do not prescribe how they must be resolved.

---

# 6. Edge Conditions

The derivation should be capable of reasoning about at least the following generic situations without assuming a predetermined mechanism:

### A. False Information

A consequential record about a participant is materially wrong.

### B. Disputed Inference

The underlying observations are accurate but the participant disputes an inference drawn from them.

### C. Purpose Expansion

Information obtained for one legitimate purpose becomes useful for another purpose not originally anticipated.

### D. Power Asymmetry

An institution possesses enough information about a participant to predict or influence behaviour in ways the participant cannot reciprocally inspect.

### E. Serious-Harm Exception

Information normally unavailable to another actor appears necessary to prevent imminent serious harm.

### F. Historical Record

Accurate information about a participant's past remains available after the participant has substantially changed.

### G. Collective Effect

Restricting a particular information flow protects one participant but materially harms other participants or a shared system.

### H. Nonhuman Participant

An artificial or otherwise nonhuman intelligence generates persistent operational information that could reveal internal states, vulnerabilities, preferences or behavioural tendencies.

The derivation must not assume that substrate alone resolves whether protections apply.

---

# 7. Questions the U-Kernel Must Answer

The U1–U8 derivation should determine, as far as the kernel permits:

1. What ethically relevant interests exist in information about a participant?
2. Does a participant possess any presumptive boundary around such information?
3. What makes acquisition or use legitimate or illegitimate?
4. What role does consent play?
5. When, if ever, may another ethical requirement override refusal or lack of consent?
6. What obligations arise when information is uncertain or inferred?
7. What obligations arise when consequential information is wrong?
8. Must participants be able to know that consequential information exists?
9. Must they be able to challenge it?
10. Must decisions based on information be explainable or attributable?
11. Does purpose matter to legitimacy of use?
12. How should informational power asymmetries be constrained?
13. How should changing persons and persistent records interact?
14. What obligations attach to information preservation across time?
15. Does the kernel require different treatment for different substrates or capabilities?
16. Which conclusions follow directly from U1–U8, and which remain underdetermined?
17. What mechanisms are possible without treating any one mechanism as ethically mandatory unless the derivation actually establishes that necessity?

---

# 8. Explicit Non-Assumptions

This packet does **not** assume:

- that information is legally or morally “owned” by the participant;
- that consent is always sufficient;
- that consent is always necessary;
- a right to deletion;
- a right to access;
- a right to correction;
- a right to explanation;
- a particular privacy doctrine;
- a particular identity system;
- centralised or decentralised storage;
- blockchain or distributed ledgers;
- state custody;
- participant custody;
- institutional custody;
- a civilisational metrics architecture;
- a specific appeals process;
- a specific oversight body;
- that transparency is always preferable;
- that confidentiality is always preferable.

Any such feature must emerge from U1–U8 plus the stated conditions or remain underdetermined.

# 9. Contamination Note

The selected V1.2 comparison anchor concerns citizen data, access and contestable civilisational metrics. Its title necessarily reveals that the existing Concord contains architecture relevant to these concepts.

That knowledge cannot be removed.

To limit contamination, this packet has been framed from generic properties of information systems, agency, uncertainty, persistence, asymmetrical capability and civilisational coordination rather than by reproducing the existing Concord mechanism.

Any later similarity involving access, correction, contestability, purpose limitation or related concepts must therefore be assessed cautiously: some may be directly regenerated from U1–U8, while some may reflect prior conceptual familiarity.
