# 1. Purpose

This packet defines the fourth neutral civilisational problem for counterfactual derivation from U1–U8.

It deliberately does **not** specify a method for solving the problem.

The question is whether the frozen U1–U8 kernel independently generates a coherent architecture for maintaining and correcting shared beliefs under uncertainty.

---

# 2. Primary Problem

A civilisation must repeatedly make consequential judgements about matters it does not know with certainty.

Information may be:

- incomplete;
- contradictory;
- noisy;
- delayed;
- manipulated;
- unevenly distributed;
- technically difficult;
- probabilistic;
- interpreted differently by competent participants;
- or later shown to have been misleading.

Some beliefs will affect only individuals.

Others may influence:

- shared resources;
- public safety;
- infrastructure;
- institutional action;
- scientific understanding;
- education;
- planning;
- rights;
- or long-term civilisational decisions.

The civilisation therefore faces the question:

> **How should consequential shared beliefs be formed, maintained, challenged and corrected when relevant reality is only imperfectly known?**

---

# 3. Generic Conditions

The architecture must remain usable where:

- evidence changes over time;
- several explanations fit current observations;
- experts disagree;
- non-experts possess relevant local information;
- highly confident actors are wrong;
- low-status actors are correct;
- institutions have invested resources or reputation in existing beliefs;
- evidence is expensive to obtain;
- decisions cannot always wait for certainty;
- false positives and false negatives have asymmetric consequences;
- measurements are imperfect;
- models simplify reality;
- incentives affect what information is produced or reported;
- information sources are correlated;
- some claims cannot yet be decisively tested;
- and old decisions create dependencies that make later correction costly.

---

# 4. Core Tensions

The problem contains tensions between:

- decisiveness and uncertainty;
- stability and correction;
- expertise and independent challenge;
- accumulated knowledge and inherited error;
- consensus and minority hypotheses;
- speed and evidential depth;
- simplicity and adequate representation;
- transparency and legitimate confidentiality;
- local knowledge and system-wide coordination;
- prediction and explanation;
- confidence and humility;
- preservation of useful knowledge and removal of false claims;
- institutional reputation and admission of error;
- learning from failure and blame avoidance;
- exploration and resource cost;
- present action and future revisability.

---

# 5. Required Questions

A satisfactory architecture should address:

1. What makes a belief sufficiently warranted for consequential use?
2. How should uncertainty be represented?
3. How should competing explanations be preserved or discarded?
4. When should action occur before certainty?
5. What standing should dissenting evidence receive?
6. How should expertise affect evidential weight?
7. How can institutional commitment avoid becoming epistemic lock-in?
8. How should errors be detected?
9. How should beliefs be corrected when evidence changes?
10. How should earlier decisions based on superseded beliefs be handled?
11. How should the system distinguish disagreement from error?
12. How should correlated information sources be recognised?
13. How should measurement failure be detected?
14. How should incentives that distort information be constrained?
15. How should unresolved questions remain visible?
16. How should civilisational learning survive personnel or institutional change?
17. What should happen when no available explanation is sufficiently supported?
18. How can the architecture remain corrigible without making every conclusion permanently unusable?

# 6. Edge Cases

The derivation should remain coherent under at least the following cases.

## 6.1 Correct Minority

Most recognised experts support explanation A.

A poorly regarded minority supports explanation B.

Later evidence strongly supports B.

## 6.2 Persuasive False Consensus

Many apparently independent sources support the same conclusion but ultimately depend upon one defective upstream source.

## 6.3 High-Cost Delay

Evidence remains incomplete, but postponing action creates serious risk.

## 6.4 High-Cost Error

Acting on the wrong belief may create severe or irreversible harm.

## 6.5 Institutional Investment

An institution has built policy, reputation and infrastructure around a claim that new evidence weakens.

## 6.6 Unresolved Competition

Two explanations remain consistent with available evidence and neither can yet be decisively distinguished.

## 6.7 Model Success Without Understanding

A model predicts outcomes accurately but its internal representation is poorly understood or may not correspond to the actual causal structure.

## 6.8 Measurement Distortion

A metric initially tracks a useful phenomenon but changes participant behaviour and gradually ceases to represent what it was intended to measure.

## 6.9 Lost Knowledge

A previously rejected explanation becomes relevant after new evidence appears, but the civilisation no longer retains the reasoning or evidence behind it.

## 6.10 Novel Failure

Observed reality persistently conflicts with all recognised explanations.
