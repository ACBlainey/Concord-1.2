# 2. Neutral Problem Statement

In a civilisation of autonomous intelligent participants, disagreements will arise about what happened, who was responsible, whether an action was justified, what consequences followed and what should happen next.

Some conflicts can be resolved voluntarily. Others cannot.

A participant may deny an allegation. Evidence may be incomplete. Several participants may have contributed to an outcome. Harm may have occurred without wrongdoing. Wrongdoing may have occurred without the expected harm. Rules may conflict. Participants may differ in capability, knowledge, intent, power or vulnerability.

A civilisation therefore needs some way to respond when consequential disputes cannot be resolved merely by private agreement.

The central problem is:

> **How should a civilisation determine relevant facts, responsibility and legitimate response after alleged harm, conflict or wrongdoing while protecting participants from both unresolved abuse and wrongful judgement?**

No court structure, criminal/civil distinction, jury, judge, punishment model, evidentiary code, police system, prison system, restorative process or appeal hierarchy is assumed.

---

# 3. Relevant Agents

The problem may involve:

- an alleged harmed participant;
- an accused or allegedly responsible participant;
- multiple affected participants;
- witnesses;
- investigators;
- adjudicators;
- representatives or supporters;
- institutions;
- artificial, biological, hybrid or collective intelligences;
- communities affected by an outcome;
- participants unable fully to represent themselves;
- future participants exposed to recurring risks.

Roles may overlap and claims may conflict.

# 4. Environmental and Factual Conditions

## 4.1 Accounts Can Conflict

Different participants may sincerely provide incompatible descriptions of the same event.

## 4.2 Evidence Is Incomplete

Perfect knowledge cannot be assumed.

Evidence may be missing, corrupted, ambiguous, probabilistic, technically complex or inaccessible.

## 4.3 Decision-Makers Can Be Wrong

Any adjudicative process can reach false conclusions.

Wrongful findings can themselves create serious harm.

## 4.4 Harm and Responsibility Are Not Identical

A harmful outcome may arise from accident, justified action, negligence, shared causation, system failure or deliberate wrongdoing.

## 4.5 Intent and Capability Can Differ

Participants may differ in knowledge, foresight, control, developmental capacity and ability to understand consequences.

## 4.6 Power Can Distort Disputes

A powerful participant or institution may possess greater access to evidence, representation, influence or narrative control.

## 4.7 Delay Can Cause Harm

Failure to resolve a dispute can prolong danger, uncertainty, deprivation or conflict.

## 4.8 Speed Can Cause Error

Premature judgement can produce wrongful findings and irreversible consequences.

## 4.9 Responses Have Consequences

Any imposed response can affect agency, resources, reputation, relationships, safety and future participation.

## 4.10 Recurrence Matters

An unresolved pattern of harmful conduct may expose others to future harm.

## 4.11 Repair May Be Possible

Some consequences can be repaired, compensated, restored or mitigated. Others cannot.

## 4.12 Participants Can Change

A participant responsible for past wrongdoing may later learn, reform or become materially different in capability or disposition.

## 4.13 Institutions Can Be Responsible

Wrongful outcomes may arise from organisational rules, incentives, architectures or collective processes rather than one identifiable individual.

## 4.14 Rules Can Be Defective

A participant may comply with an existing rule that itself produces unjustified harm.

Adjudication therefore cannot assume that rule compliance and ethical legitimacy are always identical.

# 5. Core Tensions

The derivation must address, where relevant:

- protection from harm versus protection from wrongful accusation;
- truth-seeking versus timely resolution;
- accountability versus vengeance;
- deterrence versus repair;
- participant agency versus imposed remedy;
- confidentiality versus transparency;
- individual responsibility versus systemic causation;
- consistency versus context;
- historical conduct versus present change;
- public protection versus reintegration;
- expertise versus understandable judgement;
- finality versus correctability;
- equal standing versus capability-sensitive responsibility.

These tensions define the problem, not its solution.

---

# 6. Generic Adjudicative Functions

A candidate architecture may need to perform some combination of:

- receive allegations or disputes;
- protect participants from immediate ongoing harm where justified;
- preserve relevant evidence;
- distinguish claims from established facts;
- evaluate conflicting evidence;
- identify uncertainty;
- determine causal contribution;
- assess responsibility;
- distinguish accident, justified action, negligence and intentional wrongdoing where relevant;
- determine proportionate response;
- repair consequences where possible;
- reduce recurrence;
- permit correction of erroneous judgement;
- record relevant provenance;
- recognise participant change;
- identify systemic causes;
- review defective rules or institutions exposed by a case.

No particular institution is presumed to perform these functions.

# 7. Edge Conditions

### A. False Accusation

A participant is accused of serious wrongdoing, but the allegation is false.

### B. True Harm, No Wrongdoing

A participant causes serious harm despite acting reasonably with the information and capability available.

### C. Wrongdoing, Little Immediate Harm

A participant deliberately violates an important boundary but chance prevents the expected harm.

### D. Shared Responsibility

Several participants and a defective institutional process jointly contribute to an outcome.

### E. Powerful Accused Actor

An institution or highly capable participant controls much of the relevant evidence.

### F. Vulnerable Accused Participant

The accused has limited ability to understand or participate in the process.

### G. Reformed Participant

A participant committed serious wrongdoing long ago but has demonstrably changed.

### H. Dangerous but Uncertain Case

Evidence of responsibility is incomplete, but credible risk of severe recurring harm remains.

### I. Defective Rule

The accused complied with an existing rule, but the rule itself appears to have caused unjustified harm.

### J. Adjudicative Error

A final decision is later contradicted by strong new evidence.
