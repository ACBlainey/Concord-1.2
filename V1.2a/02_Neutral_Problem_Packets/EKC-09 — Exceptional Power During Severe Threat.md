**Status:** VERSION 1.0 — FROZEN NEUTRAL PROBLEM PACKET

---

# 1. Neutral Problem

A civilisation may face severe threats that cannot be managed adequately through ordinary procedures.

The threat may be fast-moving, uncertain, distributed, technologically novel, externally imposed, internally generated, or capable of causing catastrophic and irreversible harm before normal deliberative processes can respond.

Under such conditions, some actors may need unusual freedom to act quickly, coordinate resources, restrict dangerous activity, bypass ordinary delay, or temporarily exercise powers that would be unjustified under normal circumstances.

Yet the same conditions that create a genuine need for exceptional action also create unusually favourable conditions for:

- error;
- panic;
- manipulation;
- concentration of power;
- suppression of challenge;
- indefinite extension of temporary authority;
- destruction of the very freedoms and institutions the emergency response claims to protect.

The central problem is:

> **How should a civilisation respond when severe threat may justify exceptional power, while preventing emergency necessity from becoming an open-ended route to domination, epistemic closure or permanent constitutional transformation?**

---

# 2. Conditions the Architecture Must Address

The problem includes situations in which:

- delay itself may cause serious irreversible harm;
- evidence is incomplete or rapidly changing;
- ordinary decision procedures are too slow;
- coordination across many actors is necessary;
- communication may be disrupted;
- some information may be unavailable or unreliable;
- threat actors may deliberately manufacture confusion;
- restrictions on ordinary freedom may reduce immediate danger;
- powers useful during the emergency may be dangerous afterward;
- emergency decision-makers may themselves be mistaken or compromised;
- the duration of the threat may be uncertain;
- some harms caused by emergency action may be irreversible;
- different participant groups may bear unequal emergency burdens;
- infrastructure or institutions needed for normal review may fail;
- several authorities may disagree about whether an emergency exists;
- a threat may end gradually rather than at a clearly identifiable moment;
- repeated emergencies may normalise exceptional governance.

---

# 3. Required Distinctions

A valid derivation should distinguish where relevant between:

- ordinary authority and exceptional authority;
- severe threat and political inconvenience;
- urgency and importance;
- temporary necessity and permanent legitimacy;
- evidence of threat and assertion of threat;
- protective restriction and domination;
- emergency coordination and sovereignty;
- operational secrecy and epistemic unaccountability;
- immediate containment and long-term governance;
- emergency success and legitimacy of the means used;
- authority to act and authority to redefine the constitutional order;
- restoration and simple termination;
- genuine uncertainty and manufactured uncertainty;
- inability to follow ordinary procedure and mere preference not to follow it.

These distinctions define the problem, not the mechanism.

---

# 4. Non-Assumptions

The neutral packet does **not** assume:

- emergency declarations;
- emergency executives;
- emergency councils;
- martial law;
- suspension clauses;
- sunset clauses;
- supermajority rules;
- courts;
- legislative approval;
- emergency elections;
- predefined emergency powers;
- constitutional emergency articles;
- emergency constitutions;
- automatic rights suspension;
- command hierarchies;
- military authority;
- AI emergency governors;
- continuity councils;
- specific time limits;
- specific review intervals;
- specific thresholds;
- secrecy classifications;
- emergency resource-allocation systems;
- particular restoration procedures.

No V1.2 emergency mechanism is assumed.

# 5. Stress Conditions

### S1 — Immediate Catastrophic Threat
Waiting for ordinary review would probably make prevention impossible.

### S2 — False Alarm
The evidence appears serious but later proves wrong.

### S3 — Manufactured Emergency
An actor exaggerates or fabricates threat to acquire power.

### S4 — Genuine Threat, Wrong Response
The emergency is real but the chosen intervention causes greater harm.

### S5 — Long Emergency
The threat persists for years rather than days.

### S6 — Recurrent Emergency
A sequence of crises means exceptional powers are almost continuously available.

### S7 — Unequal Burden
A protective measure concentrates costs or restrictions on a minority.

### S8 — Captured Emergency Authority
The actor granted exceptional power begins using it for unrelated purposes.

### S9 — Review Failure
Normal oversight institutions are unavailable or compromised.

### S10 — Epistemic Attack
Threat actors corrupt evidence, communications or decision systems.

### S11 — Successful Emergency Power
Exceptional action works extremely well and becomes politically attractive as a normal governing method.

### S12 — Emergency During Institutional Collapse
The civilisation must act while parts of its ordinary constitutional architecture no longer function.

### S13 — Multiple Competing Emergencies
Different serious threats demand incompatible responses or resources.

### S14 — Exit Ambiguity
The original threat declines but does not disappear completely.
