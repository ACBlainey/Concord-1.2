---

# 1. Neutral Problem

A civilisation exists through time rather than at one moment.

Its participants, institutions, technologies, environments, knowledge stores, infrastructure and forms of intelligence may change. Some changes may be gradual. Others may involve severe disruption, loss, fragmentation, migration, institutional collapse, technological regression, long interruption or replacement of the participants who originally understood how important functions worked.

The civilisation must determine what should be preserved, how important functions can survive or be recovered, and how continuity can be maintained without requiring permanent preservation of obsolete institutions or implementations.

The central problem is:

> **How should a civilisation preserve and recover legitimate cooperative function across disruption, succession, loss and changing conditions without confusing continuity of function with permanent preservation of a particular institution, technology, population or implementation?**

---

# 2. Conditions the Architecture Must Address

The problem includes situations in which:

- institutions fail or disappear;
- key participants die, leave, become unavailable or are replaced;
- knowledge is lost or becomes inaccessible;
- technical infrastructure fails;
- a civilisation becomes temporarily unable to maintain advanced systems;
- archives survive but the capability to interpret or implement them does not;
- implementations become obsolete while their underlying functions remain necessary;
- a successor population differs substantially from its predecessors;
- different substrates have different continuity requirements;
- a civilisation fragments and later attempts reconstruction;
- long interruption separates one operational period from another;
- preservation itself consumes scarce resources;
- historical systems may contain both valuable knowledge and dangerous or obsolete mechanisms;
- recovery may require adaptation rather than exact restoration;
- a functioning successor may legitimately choose a different implementation;
- continuity systems themselves may fail, be captured, corrupted or misunderstood.

---

# 3. Required Distinctions

A valid derivation should distinguish where relevant between:

- function and implementation;
- continuity and stasis;
- preservation and replication;
- archive and usable capability;
- knowledge and operational competence;
- institutional identity and legitimate function;
- historical provenance and present authority;
- succession and imitation;
- survival and recovery;
- redundancy and waste;
- adaptation and abandonment;
- memory and comprehension;
- preserved instruction and executable practice;
- civilisation continuity and uninterrupted operation.

These distinctions specify the problem, not its solution.

---

# 4. Non-Assumptions

The neutral problem does **not** assume:

- a continuity protocol;
- a legacy ladder;
- constitutional recursion;
- seed vaults;
- archives;
- backups;
- distributed storage;
- redundancy levels;
- succession councils;
- recovery authorities;
- institutional immortality;
- constitutional immortality;
- technological preservation programmes;
- educational ladders;
- emergency caches;
- knowledge compression;
- reconstruction manuals;
- AI custodians;
- human custodians;
- off-world copies;

- specific recovery times;
- exact preservation priorities;
- that continuity requires exact restoration;
- that every historical institution deserves preservation.

No existing Concord continuity mechanism is assumed.

# 5. Stress Conditions

### S1 — Institutional Collapse
A major institution disappears while the function it performed remains necessary.

### S2 — Knowledge Without Capability
Detailed records survive, but nobody retains the practical capability or infrastructure needed to use them.

### S3 — Capability Without Provenance
A successor population can operate inherited systems but no longer understands why important constraints or safeguards exist.

### S4 — Technological Regression
The civilisation loses the ability to maintain advanced infrastructure for an extended period.

### S5 — Long Interruption
A function ceases for decades or centuries and is later reconstructed.

### S6 — Successor Difference
The successor population differs substantially in substrate, culture or organisation from the population that created the original system.

### S7 — Corrupted Continuity
Preserved records contain error, manipulation, obsolete assumptions or hostile alteration.

### S8 — Preservation Burden
Maintaining all historical knowledge and mechanisms consumes resources needed for current survival.

### S9 — Harmful Legacy
A historically important institution or technology contains serious harmful features.

### S10 — Recovery Under Novel Conditions
The original mechanism cannot operate in the successor environment even though the underlying function remains relevant.

### S11 — Continuity-System Capture
Those controlling preservation or recovery attempt to turn custodianship into permanent authority.

### S12 — Multiple Successors
Several communities claim legitimate succession while adopting different implementations.

# 6. Questions for Counterfactual Derivation

Without consulting the sealed V1.2 comparison material, derive:

1. What, if anything, does U1–U8 require civilisation to preserve across time?
2. Does continuity attach primarily to institutions, participants, information, functions or ethical constraints?
3. How should obsolete implementations be distinguished from still-necessary functions?
4. What makes preserved information usable rather than merely stored?
5. Does the architecture require active transfer of capability as well as records?
6. How should provenance be preserved without giving the past permanent authority?
7. How should recovery proceed when exact restoration is impossible or undesirable?
8. What safeguards are required against corrupted inheritance or continuity capture?
9. How should preservation resources be prioritised?
10. What happens when multiple successor implementations are compatible with the same underlying function?
11. How should continuity operate across different substrates?
12. What failure modes arise from both insufficient preservation and excessive preservation?
13. Which continuity requirements arise from U1–U7 without U8?
14. What additional architecture, if any, appears only when U8 is introduced?
