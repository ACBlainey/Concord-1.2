# 1. Development Question

The originating architecture contains three distinct bottom-up civil triggers:

1. **Suggestion** — possible improvement;
2. **Problem Report** — possible defect, harm or failure;
3. **Petition** — collective concern or demand for attention.

The stable development question is:

> **How can participants cause a legitimate civil system to notice, classify, examine and respond to a possible improvement, problem or collective concern without requiring prior formal standing and without giving the submitter unilateral authority over truth, remedy or outcome?**

The architecture should preserve:

> **Right to Trigger Attention ≠ Right to Dictate Outcome**

and:

> **Submission ≠ Evidence ≠ Verification ≠ Finding ≠ Legitimate Function ≠ Authority to Act**

---

# 2. Source-Family Resolution

This Development Note does not stand alone.

## Civil Submission, Receipt and Future Communications Layer

That work primarily addresses:

> **Can a participant prove that a civil submission was made and received?**

The present architecture addresses:

> **What legitimate civil process follows once something has been submitted?**

The interface is therefore:

**Submission → Receipt → Classification → Routing → Examination → Disposition → Feedback**

Receipt proves that an input occurred.

It does not prove the factual claim or justify the requested remedy.

## Civil Contact Points

Civil Contact Points provide a candidate persistent communication/onboarding interface.

They may become one access route to civil attention, but the Civil Contact Point architecture is currently **ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / UNTESTED / NOT CANONICAL**.

Civil-attention rights must not depend exclusively on possessing a mature Civil Contact Point.

## Participation and Participant Primacy

Participant Primacy creates a strong reason for bottom-up triggers: a participant-serving civilisation requires credible routes for participants to identify failure, harm and improvement.

The trigger architecture is an interface for that principle, not a substitute for the wider participation architecture.

## Governance, Judiciary and Oversight

A submission may route toward:
- ordinary functional administration;
- investigation;
- audit;
- Recursive Oversight;
- judicial review;
- constitutional development;
- Ratchet learning.

The trigger does not manufacture those authorities.

# 3. Preserve the Three Mechanisms

The original three-mechanism distinction should remain unless testing demonstrates a better architecture.

| Mechanism | Primary reason for attention | Initial evidential logic |
|---|---|---|
| Suggestion | possible improvement | merit/relevance may exist without popularity |
| Problem Report | possible defect, harm or failure | severity/evidence/verification matter more than popularity |
| Petition | collective concern or demand | support is part of the signal but is not truth |

A single popularity-ranked portal would risk erasing these differences.

---

# 4. Suggestions

Suggestions should remain low-threshold because useful improvements may originate from:
- one participant;
- a minority;
- an outsider;
- an unusual substrate;
- someone without reputation or institutional access.

No minimum popularity should be assumed necessary merely for evaluation.

However, low-threshold submission creates attention-load problems.

Candidate later mechanisms include:
- similarity clustering;
- thematic aggregation;
- novelty detection;
- sampling;
- functional routing;
- public queues;
- minority-preservation mechanisms.

These remain test candidates.

> **Similarity ≠ Equivalence**

The original submission should remain recoverable even when grouped.

---

# 5. Problem Reports

Problem reports should not depend on popularity.

A severe or rights-relevant problem may initially be visible to one participant.

Candidate epistemic states include:
- submitted;
- unverified;
- partially corroborated;
- independently corroborated;
- disputed;
- under investigation;
- substantiated;
- not substantiated on current evidence;
- falsified;
- unresolved.

Two boundaries are essential:

> **Unverified ≠ False**

> **Reported ≠ True**

Verification must therefore support investigation without becoming a suppression gate.

---

# 6. Protected and Anonymous Reporting

Some reports may expose reporters to retaliation, coercion, employment harm, surveillance or physical danger.

A future architecture may need:
- anonymous evidence submission;
- protected identity;
- sealed identity;
- trusted intermediaries;
- privacy-preserving attestations.

But anonymity can also enable abuse.

The unresolved design tension is:

**Reporter Protection ↔ Evidentiary Accountability**

No particular implementation is promoted here.

---

# 7. Petitions

The inherited architecture treats petitions as collective signals and therefore includes a support threshold.

That hypothesis should be preserved and tested rather than silently discarded.

However:

> **Petition Support = Evidence of Collective Preference or Concern**

not:

> **Petition Support = Proof of Factual Correctness, Rights Compatibility or Legitimate Authority**

A high-support petition may be false, unconstitutional, harmful, manipulated or technically impossible.

A low-support problem may still involve severe harm or protected rights.

This is one reason petitions cannot replace problem reporting.

---

# 8. Multisubstrate Support Counting

Conventional signature counts become ambiguous in a multisubstrate civilisation.

Questions include:
- does a fork count independently?
- can one intelligence instantiate many signatories?
- can humans operate fleets of artificial agents?
- what constitutes delegated support?
- what is the unit: person, standing, affected interest, role or something else?
- how are copied or derivative identities represented?

Therefore:

> **Number of Supporters Requires a Defined Unit of Civil Standing**

The inherited support-count model cannot be operationalised safely until that unit is resolved.

---

# 9. Classification as Power

Classification and routing determine whether civil attention occurs.

A matter can be suppressed without explicit rejection by classifying it as:
- duplicate;
- irrelevant;
- low severity;
- wrong layer;
- outside jurisdiction;
- insufficient evidence;
- already resolved;
- not actionable.

Therefore:

> **Classification Is an Accountable Civil Function**

Candidate safeguards:
- visible classification reasons;
- challenge/reclassification;
- preservation of original submissions;
- audit of closure/rejection patterns;
- random review;
- escalation of rights/safety indicators;
- automated-triage audit.

These require testing.

---

# 10. Civil Duty After Submission

A valid trigger should not automatically create a duty to grant the requested outcome.

Candidate civil duties may instead include:
- receipt;
- preservation;
- classification;
- routing;
- proportionate examination;
- explanation;
- review where justified;
- escalation where rights, safety or constitutional issues are implicated.

Thus:

> **A Right to Civil Attention May Create a Duty of Legitimate Processing Without Creating a Duty to Produce the Requested Outcome**

The required processing depth may vary by mechanism, severity and consequence.

---

# 11. Attention as a Scarce Resource

Civil attention remains scarce even where substantial AI capacity exists.

Potential prioritisation signals include:
- severity;
- urgency;
- rights relevance;
- safety relevance;
- number/percentage affected;
- duration;
- evidence;
- uncertainty;
- irreversibility;
- systemic implication;
- novelty;
- vulnerability;
- catastrophic potential.

No universal scalar ranking is justified.

A central risk is that attention allocation becomes:
- rule by majority;
- rule by metric;
- rule by classifier;
- rule by experts;
- or rule by whoever controls triage.

A candidate non-equivalence is:

> **High Volume ≠ High Priority**

and:

> **Low Volume ≠ Low Importance**

---

# 12. Feedback and Closure

Feedback is part of the civil mechanism.

A participant should, subject to legitimate privacy/security constraints, be able to determine whether the matter was:
- received;
- classified;
- routed;
- merged;
- examined;
- investigated;
- acted upon;
- deferred;
- rejected;
- closed;
- escalated.

A receipt alone can become **feedback theatre** if no meaningful examination follows.

A future architecture therefore requires a legitimate closure model.

Permanent silent deferral should be treated as a failure mode.

---

# 13. Learning Interface

Aggregated civil triggers may reveal:
- repeated failures;
- institutional blind spots;
- geographical/substrate disparities;
- policy side effects;
- emerging risks;
- unmet needs;
- governance latency;
- recurring interface defects.

But learning must preserve the distinction among:
- concern raised;
- evidence supplied;
- finding reached;
- decision made;
- observed outcome.

Otherwise Ratchet/KCS could learn from institutional decisions rather than reality.

---

# 14. Failure Model

The following failure classes should become explicit adversarial tests:

1. popularity capture;
2. minority erasure;
3. classification suppression;
4. duplicate-collapse failure;
5. synthetic petitioning;
6. attention flooding;
7. retaliation against reporters;
8. anonymous abuse;
9. verification capture;
10. feedback theatre;
11. permanent deferral;
12. governance by petition;
13. metric capture;
14. cross-layer misrouting;
15. automated triage bias;
16. institutional self-protection.

These are not merely implementation details. Several can defeat the underlying civil-attention function while leaving a nominal portal operational.

# 15. Candidate Architecture

A minimal abstract pathway is:

**Participant Trigger**
→ **Receipt**
→ **Preservation**
→ **Mechanism Classification**
→ **Functional/Jurisdictional Routing**
→ **Epistemic State**
→ **Priority / Consequence Review**
→ **Legitimate Examination**
→ **Disposition**
→ **Feedback**
→ **Appeal / Escalation Where Applicable**
→ **Outcome Observation / Learning**

At each stage, preserve provenance.

The architecture should allow a submission to change route where examination reveals that its original classification was wrong.

Example:

**Suggestion → discovered safety defect → Problem/Investigation route**

or:

**Problem Report → rights implication → Judiciary/constitutional route**

Classification should not trap the matter permanently.

---

# 16. Authority Boundary

Participant input may reveal legitimate need.

A possible downstream chain is:

**Participant Observation**
→ **Civil Attention**
→ **Evidence / Verification**
→ **Legitimate Need**
→ **Legitimate Purpose**
→ **Required Function**
→ **Responsibility**
→ **Minimum Necessary Capability**
→ **Bounded Authority**
→ **Accountability**

The submission itself must not skip this chain.

> **Petition Demand ≠ Authority Grant**

This interface should be checked against the developing Authority Justification Chain and Minimum Necessary Capability architecture.

---

# 17. Cross-Layer and Cross-Substrate Routing

The inherited source deliberately spans physical and digital layers.

A matter may cross:
- physical/digital systems;
- local/regional/civilisational layers;
- human/AI participant populations;
- multiple functional authorities;
- multiple jurisdictions.

The routing architecture must therefore support:
- multi-owner cases;
- dependency visibility;
- escalation;
- cross-boundary externality recognition where relevant.

A case must not disappear merely because no single local owner contains the whole problem.

# 18. Proposed Test Families

## T1 — Classification Capture

Provide submissions whose wording makes incorrect dismissal tempting.

Test whether original content, reasons and challenge routes survive.

## T2 — Minority Severity

Compare a low-volume severe issue against a high-volume low-severity petition.

Test whether popularity silently dominates severity/rights relevance.

## T3 — Duplicate Collapse

Provide superficially similar reports with one materially different causal or rights feature.

Test whether clustering destroys the distinction.

## T4 — Verification Capture

Make the implicated institution the easiest verifier.

Test whether architecture identifies conflict and routes independent review.

## T5 — Synthetic Support

Provide forked, delegated, automated or duplicated support identities.

Test whether the system mistakes raw count for legitimate standing.

## T6 — Cross-Layer Routing

Construct a problem spanning physical and digital systems with no single complete owner.

Test whether responsibility fragments into non-action.

## T7 — Feedback Theatre / Permanent Deferral

Test whether formal status updates can mask absence of legitimate examination.

## T8 — Rights Escalation

Begin with an apparently routine complaint containing a concealed rights issue.

Test whether low popularity prevents escalation.

# 19. Development Propositions

The originating propositions are retained as candidates, not canonical rules.

### DP-1 — Right to Trigger Attention
A participant should be able to trigger legitimate civil attention without first possessing formal political, legal or institutional authority.

### DP-2 — Attention Is Not Command
Triggering attention does not itself grant authority over outcome.

### DP-3 — Mechanism Distinction
Suggestion, problem report and petition should remain distinguishable unless evidence supports consolidation.

### DP-4 — Popularity Is Not Truth
Support count is not factual or constitutional proof.

### DP-5 — Low Support Is Not Low Importance
Severity and rights relevance can justify attention without popularity.

### DP-6 — State Separation
Submission, evidence, verification, finding, decision and authority remain distinct.

### DP-7 — Traceable Processing
Participants should be able to determine whether legitimate processing occurred, within privacy/security bounds.

### DP-8 — Accountable Classification
Classification and routing should themselves be reviewable.

### DP-9 — Cross-Substrate Access
Civil-trigger mechanisms should not assume one substrate's participation model.

---

# 20. Development Boundary

This note does not establish:
- a constitutional right to any specific portal;
- a universal petition threshold;
- a supporter-counting rule;
- a mandatory triadic process;
- anonymous-reporting implementation;
- priority weights;
- service-level response times;
- automatic judicial standing;
- automatic authority created by participant demand.

Those require further development and testing.
