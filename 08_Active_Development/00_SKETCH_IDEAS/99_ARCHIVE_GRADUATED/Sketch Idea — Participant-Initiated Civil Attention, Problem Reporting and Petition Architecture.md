# Sketch Idea — Participant-Initiated Civil Attention, Problem Reporting and Petition Architecture

*A development sketch examining how participants can trigger civil attention through suggestions, problem reports and petitions without requiring prior formal standing, legal action or political representation*

**Status:** GRADUATED TO DEVELOPMENT NOTE / RETAINED FOR PROVENANCE  
**Development context:** Concord V1.2
**Development Transfer:** `01_DEVELOPMENT_NOTES/04_GOVERNANCE_AND_STEWARDSHIP/Participant-Initiated Civil Attention, Problem Reporting and Petition Architecture — Development Note 001.md`
**Promotion Date:** 24 September 2026  
**Source status:** Expanded from the Development Archive document *Suggestion Box Petition Concerns Portal Ssystem.md*. The originating mechanisms, criteria, progressions, weighting ideas, thresholds and cross-layer relationships are deliberately preserved before V1.2 development is added. # PART I — THE ORIGINAL DEVELOPMENT PROBLEM

## 1. Why a Civilisation Needs Bottom-Up Triggers

The source note identified a practical weakness that can exist even in a sophisticated governance system: governance may possess strong deliberative, constitutional and top-down mechanisms while still lacking simple ways for ordinary participants to make the system notice something.

The originating proposition was that **suggestion boxes, problem-reporting portals and petitioning systems** provide structured pathways through which participants can trigger governance attention without first requiring:

- formal legal standing;
- court action;
- elected or appointed political representation;
- specialist procedural knowledge;
- access to institutional insiders;
- prior institutional permission to say that something deserves attention.

The proposal therefore adds **bottom-up and reactive triggers** to a system that may otherwise be dominated by top-down or deliberative processes.

The deeper development problem can be expressed as:

> **How can an ordinary participant cause a legitimate civil system to notice, classify, examine and respond to a possible improvement, problem or collective concern without giving the participant unilateral authority to determine the outcome?**

This distinction between **triggering attention** and **controlling the decision** is central to the later V1.2 development, but the three original mechanisms should be preserved first in their inherited form.

# PART II — ORIGINAL PROPOSED FRAMEWORK — PRESERVED FOR DEVELOPMENTAL PROVENANCE

This section preserves the originating architecture explicitly. Later sections develop, question and connect it without treating the later formulation as if it were the original one.

## 2. The Three Original Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Purpose | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| **Suggestion Box** | Propose improvements | Ideas for refinement |
| **Problem Report** | Identify issues | Problems requiring attention |
| **Petition** | Raise immediate concerns | Collective demand for action |

The original proposal treated these as distinct mechanisms rather than one generic submissions portal.

Each was intended to operate across:

- **Layer Zero** — physical world and human communities;
- **Layer Minus Zero** — digital realm and AI participants;
- **all governance layers** — local, regional and civilisational.

That cross-layer intention is retained.

---

## 3. Original Suggestion Box Proposal

### 3.1 Purpose

To allow participants to propose improvements to the Concord's constitutional, operational or technical systems.

### 3.2 Characteristics

- **Low threshold** — any participant can submit.
- **Positive framing** — focused on improvement rather than complaint.
- **Cumulative weight** — multiple similar suggestions indicate broader interest.
- **Transparent tracking** — participants can see the status of suggestions.

### 3.3 Original Evaluation Criteria

| Criteria | Consideration |
|---|---|
| **Relevance** | Which governance layer does it affect? |
| **Feasibility** | Is it technically and constitutionally possible? |
| **Support** | How many participants have raised similar ideas? |
| **Impact** | What benefit would it produce? |
| **Risk** | What unintended consequences might arise? |
| **Cost** | What resources would be required? |

### 3.4 Original Progression

```text
Suggestion Submitted
        ↓
Initial Classification
        ↓
Relevance Check
        ↓
Duplicate / Similarity Assessment
        ↓
Weighted Evaluation
        ↓
Recommendation
        ↓
Governance Review
        ↓
Action / Deferral / Rejection
        ↓
Feedback to Submitter
```

The original proposal did **not** require minimum support before a suggestion could be evaluated.

---

## 4. Original Problem Reporting Portal Proposal

### 4.1 Purpose

To allow participants to report problems or issues they have observed within Concord systems.

### 4.2 Characteristics

- **Low threshold** — any participant can report.
- **Problem-focused** — identifies issues requiring attention.
- **Contextual** — includes layer, domain and severity.
- **Traceable** — participants can track resolution progress.

### 4.3 Original Evaluation Criteria

| Criteria | Consideration |
|---|---|
| **Severity** | How serious is the problem? |
| **Scope** | How many participants are affected? |
| **Urgency** | How quickly does it need to be addressed? |
| **Evidence** | What evidence supports the report? |
| **Verifiability** | Can the problem be independently confirmed? |
| **Layered context** | Which governance layer does it concern? |

### 4.4 Original Progression

```text
Problem Reported
        ↓
Initial Classification
        ↓
Verification
        ↓
Severity / Scope Assessment
        ↓
Priority Assignment
        ↓
Governance Review
        ↓
Action / Investigation / Deferral
        ↓
Feedback to Reporter
```

The original proposal made **verification and severity**, rather than popularity, central to the action pathway for a problem report.

---

## 5. Original Petitioning System Proposal

### 5.1 Purpose

To allow participants to raise matters of immediate concern requiring governance attention, particularly where a significant number of participants are affected.

### 5.2 Characteristics

- **Higher threshold** — requires a minimum number of supporters.
- **Immediate concern** — focused on matters requiring prompt attention.
- **Weighted by impact** — more participants affected gives greater weight.
- **Layered relevance** — petitions are directed to the appropriate governance layer.

### 5.3 Original Weighting Factors

| Factor | Weight Consideration |
|---|---|
| **Number of supporters** | More support = greater weight |
| **Percentage of affected participants** | Proportion affected matters |
| **Layer of concern** | Higher layers may have broader implications |
| **Severity of issue** | More severe = greater urgency |
| **Duration** | How long has the issue persisted? |
| **Evidence** | How well-supported is the petition? |

### 5.4 Original Progression

```text
Petition Initiated
        ↓
Support Gathering
        ↓
Threshold Check
        ↓
Verification
        ↓
Weighted Evaluation
        ↓
Governance Review
        ↓
Action / Investigation / Deferral
        ↓
Feedback to Petitioners
```

The minimum-support threshold and weighting model are preserved here as originating proposals. Later development raises questions about them but does not erase them.

---

## 6. Original Cross-Layer Integration

### 6.1 Layer Zero — Physical World

The original note proposed:

- suggestions from communities, individuals and physical-infrastructure users;
- problem reports from physical environments;
- petitions from citizens affected by physical governance decisions.

### 6.2 Layer Minus Zero — Digital Realm

It likewise proposed:

- suggestions from AI participants and digital communities;
- problems reported from digital systems, networks and algorithms;
- petitions from digital participants.

### 6.3 Inter-Layer Connection

The source explicitly recognised that:

- a problem reported in Layer Zero may have implications for Layer Minus Zero;
- a suggestion from Layer Minus Zero may improve Layer Zero systems;
- a petition may span both layers.

The significance is that the reporting architecture was not imagined as substrate-isolated.

---

## 7. Original Governance Integration

| Existing Mechanism | Original Relationship |
|---|---|
| **Triadic Governance** | Petitions may be escalated to triadic decision-making |
| **Metrics System** | Suggestions and problem reports provide metric data |
| **Knowledge Control** | All submissions are recorded and traceable |
| **Audit** | Patterns of problems may trigger audit |
| **Judiciary** | Serious issues may be escalated to judicial review |
| **Ratchet** | Successful suggestions may inform Ratchet evolution |

The source therefore treated the three mechanisms not as standalone feedback forms but as interfaces into wider civil functions.

---

## 8. Original Action Thresholds

| Mechanism | Original Action Threshold |
|---|---|
| **Suggestion** | Requires evaluation; no minimum support needed |
| **Problem Report** | Requires verification; severity determines action |
| **Petition** | Requires minimum supporters; weight determines review priority |

This distinction should be preserved because it encodes a subtle original assumption: **different kinds of participant input justify attention for different reasons.**

A suggestion does not need popularity to deserve evaluation. A problem does not need votes if it is severe and verifiable. A petition is explicitly collective and therefore uses support as part of its trigger logic.

---

## 9. Original Design Aims

The source characterised the three mechanisms as:

- **Accessible** — low barriers to participation;
- **Transparent** — submissions can be tracked;
- **Weighted** — more support adds weight;
- **Layered** — issues are directed to the appropriate governance layer;
- **Reciprocal** — feedback is provided to submitters.

Its final practical claim was that a governance system responding only to formal petitions or legal challenges is incomplete. Ordinary participants need a way to draw civil attention to issues without specialised knowledge or formal standing.

That remains the inherited foundation for the development below.

# PART III — V1.2 DEVELOPMENT: FROM SUBMISSION TO CIVIL ATTENTION

## 10. The Underlying Function May Be Civil Attention

The three mechanisms can be viewed as different routes into a common civil function:

**Participant observation or proposal → recognised submission → civil attention → classification → examination → legitimate function → possible action → feedback**

This does not eliminate the distinctions among suggestions, reports and petitions. It identifies their shared interface.

A useful V1.2 formulation is:

> **Participants should be able to trigger legitimate civil attention without first possessing decision authority over the matter raised.**

This leads to two non-equivalences:

**RIGHT TO TRIGGER ATTENTION ≠ RIGHT TO DICTATE OUTCOME**

and

**SUBMISSION ≠ EVIDENCE ≠ VERIFICATION ≠ FINDING ≠ AUTHORITY TO ACT**

These distinctions prevent a reporting or petition mechanism from becoming either meaningless theatre or direct plebiscitary command.

---

## 11. Relationship to the Civil Submission / Receipt Layer

Later Concord work on civil submission and receipt architecture appears to solve a related but different problem:

> **Can a participant prove that a submission was made and received?**

The present sketch addresses what happens after receipt:

> **What legitimate civil process should follow when a participant says that something deserves attention?**

The two therefore appear complementary rather than duplicative.

A mature interface may need to preserve separately:

1. submission identity or anonymity status;
2. timestamp and receipt;
3. submitted content;
4. classification;
5. routing;
6. evidence attachments;
7. status changes;
8. review decisions;
9. escalation history;
10. outcome and feedback;
11. appeals or challenge pathways;
12. provenance of any automated processing.

The receipt proves that a civil input occurred. It should not itself prove that the factual claim is true or that the requested remedy is justified.

---

# PART IV — SUGGESTIONS AS DEVELOPMENTAL INPUT

## 12. Why Suggestions Should Remain Low-Threshold

The original model allows any participant to submit a suggestion and imposes no minimum support requirement before evaluation.

That may be particularly important in a civilisation designed to learn.

A useful idea can originate from:

- one person;
- one AI;
- a minority community;
- an outsider;
- a participant without status or reputation;
- an unusual substrate;
- someone affected by a system in a way the majority does not experience.

Therefore popularity should not determine whether a suggestion is capable of entering evaluation.

At the same time, an unbounded suggestion system creates load-management problems. The development question is not whether low-threshold submission should exist, but how civil attention is allocated without allowing volume to destroy attention.

Possible mechanisms for later testing include:

- similarity clustering;
- thematic aggregation;
- novelty detection;
- rotating sampling;
- participant-controlled prioritisation;
- triage by scope;
- automatic forwarding to the relevant functional layer;
- public queues;
- minority-preservation mechanisms;
- escalation when suggestions reveal a safety or rights issue rather than merely an improvement.

None of these should be assumed superior before testing.

---

## 13. Duplicate Detection Is Useful but Dangerous

The source explicitly included **Duplicate / Similarity Assessment**.

This can reduce repeated work and reveal patterns. But it also creates failure modes.

Two submissions that look linguistically similar may differ in:

- location;
- affected population;
- causal mechanism;
- severity;
- time;
- evidence;
- rights implications;
- substrate;
- institutional actor;
- requested remedy.

An automated system could wrongly collapse unusual evidence into a large existing cluster and thereby erase the very distinction that matters.

Thus:

> **Similarity ≠ equivalence.**

A future implementation may need to preserve the original submission even when it is linked to a cluster.

---

# PART V — PROBLEM REPORTING AS AN EPISTEMIC ESCALATION PATH

## 14. Reports Should Not Depend on Popularity

The originating model correctly separates problem reports from petitions.

A serious problem may initially be observed by only one participant.

Examples could include:

- a safety defect;
- rights violation;
- security vulnerability;
- abuse by an official;
- hidden algorithmic failure;
- environmental hazard;
- corruption;
- systemic discrimination;
- a digital-system anomaly visible only to one substrate;
- an early warning of a wider failure.

Therefore:

> **Low support does not imply low importance.**

The original criteria of severity, urgency, evidence and verifiability remain important precisely because a problem report is not a popularity mechanism.

---

## 15. Verification Must Not Become a Suppression Gate

The source places verification early in the reporting progression.

That is sensible, but “verification” can become dangerous if interpreted too rigidly.

Some genuine problems are initially:

- difficult to reproduce;
- visible only intermittently;
- dependent on privileged data;
- experienced by marginal populations;
- concealed by the institution responsible;
- probabilistic rather than deterministic;
- supported by weak signals that become meaningful only over time.

A system that interprets “not yet verified” as “false” can suppress early warnings.

Possible epistemic states might therefore include:

- submitted;
- unverified;
- partially corroborated;
- independently corroborated;
- disputed;
- under investigation;
- substantiated;
- not substantiated on current evidence;
- falsified;
- unresolved.

The exact state model requires development, but a non-inference rule is useful:

**UNVERIFIED ≠ FALSE**

Likewise:

**REPORTED ≠ TRUE**

Both are necessary.

---

## 16. Anonymous and Protected Reporting

The original document did not substantially develop anonymity, but later Concord privacy and Layer-Zero work makes it an important interface.

Some reports may require anonymity or protected identity because disclosure could expose the reporter to:

- retaliation;
- coercion;
- employment consequences;
- social punishment;
- surveillance;
- physical danger;
- digital exclusion;
- institutional pressure.

But anonymity also complicates verification, duplicate detection and abuse prevention.

This creates a genuine design tension rather than a simple rule:

**REPORTER PROTECTION ↔ EVIDENTIARY ACCOUNTABILITY**

The system may need mechanisms such as protected attestations, sealed identity, trusted intermediaries, anonymous evidence submission or privacy-preserving reputation. These are future technical questions, not assumed solutions.

---

# PART VI — PETITIONS AS COLLECTIVE SIGNALS

## 17. Preserve the Original Threshold Hypothesis

The originating petition model requires a minimum number of supporters and gives greater weight to broader impact.

That should remain visible as the inherited proposition.

Its rationale is straightforward: a petition is intended to represent a **collective demand or concern**, so collective support is part of what distinguishes it from an individual report or suggestion.

However, V1.2 should test rather than assume the precise consequences of this design.

---

## 18. Popularity Is Not Truth

A petition may have very high support and still be:

- factually wrong;
- based on misinformation;
- harmful to a minority;
- unconstitutional;
- incompatible with rights;
- internally contradictory;
- technically impossible;
- manufactured through manipulation;
- supported by participants unaffected by the issue while those directly affected disagree.

Therefore:

> **Petition support is evidence of participant preference or concern, not evidence that the petition's factual claims are true or that its proposed action is legitimate.**

This preserves the original weighting idea while preventing support count from becoming an epistemic shortcut.

---

## 19. Minority Protection

The reverse problem is equally important.

A concern affecting a small population may never achieve a large supporter threshold.

Examples include:

- a rare disability;
- a small substrate class;
- a geographically isolated community;
- an unpopular minority;
- a newly created intelligence class;
- a niche but severe technical risk;
- a local rights violation.

A mature architecture should therefore prevent the petition channel from becoming the only route by which collective concerns obtain attention.

The original separation among **suggestion**, **problem report** and **petition** already helps here. A minority problem may enter as a problem report even if it cannot become a large petition.

This may be one reason the three-mechanism architecture is stronger than a single popularity-ranked portal.

---

## 20. Synthetic Support and AI-Scale Manipulation

A multisubstrate civilisation introduces a problem that conventional petition systems do not fully address: support may be generated at machine scale.

Questions include:

- Can one intelligence instantiate many copies and produce many signatures?
- Does a fork count as one participant or many?
- Can humans operate fleets of artificial agents to simulate civil support?
- Can institutions purchase or manufacture synthetic petition activity?
- How are duplicates, proxies and delegated signatures treated?
- Does support represent persons, affected interests, standing, functional roles or something else?

These questions connect directly to identity, forks, civil standing and participant representation.

The original “number of supporters” criterion therefore survives, but its unit of measurement requires substantial multisubstrate development.

---

# PART VII — ROUTING, CLASSIFICATION AND CIVIL DUTY

## 21. Classification Creates Power

All three original progressions begin with some form of classification or routing.

That apparently administrative step creates significant power.

An institution can suppress a matter without formally rejecting it if it can classify it as:

- irrelevant;
- duplicate;
- low severity;
- wrong layer;
- insufficient evidence;
- outside jurisdiction;
- not actionable;
- already resolved.

Thus classification itself should be accountable.

Possible safeguards for testing include:

- visible classification reasons;
- challenge or appeal;
- independent reclassification;
- audit of rejection patterns;
- preservation of original submissions;
- automatic escalation of rights or safety indicators;
- random review of closed submissions;
- detection of institutional classification bias.

The principle is not that every submission deserves action. It is that **the gateway into attention must not become an invisible disposal mechanism.**

---

## 22. Submission Does Not Automatically Create a Duty to Grant the Request

A participant may submit a suggestion, report or petition and still receive a legitimate rejection, deferral or decision not to act.

The civil duty created by a submission may therefore be more limited and structured.

Possible duties could include:

- receipt;
- preservation;
- classification;
- appropriate routing;
- proportionate examination;
- explanation;
- review where required;
- escalation where rights, safety or constitutional issues are implicated.

This suggests:

> **A right to civil attention may create a duty of legitimate processing without creating a duty to produce the requested substantive outcome.**

The exact level of processing required should probably vary with the type and severity of submission.

---

# PART VIII — PRIORITY, WEIGHT AND SCARCITY

## 23. Attention Is a Scarce Civil Resource

Even an AI-rich civilisation may face attention scarcity.

The number of possible submissions can exceed the capacity for serious human, AI or mixed review.

Priority systems are therefore unavoidable somewhere in the architecture.

But prioritisation creates power and potential capture.

Relevant signals may include:

- severity;
- urgency;
- rights relevance;
- safety relevance;
- number affected;
- percentage affected;
- duration;
- evidence strength;
- uncertainty;
- irreversibility;
- systemic implications;
- novelty;
- repetition;
- resource cost;
- possibility of catastrophic harm;
- vulnerability of affected participants.

No single ranking metric should be assumed safe.

A central adversarial question is whether a weighting system quietly converts civil attention into rule by majority, rule by metrics, rule by experts or rule by whoever controls the classifier.

---

## 24. Severity May Override Volume

The source already implies different action thresholds for different mechanisms.

That can be developed into an important possible rule:

> **A low-volume but high-severity signal may justify more urgent attention than a high-volume low-severity signal.**

This should not be treated as settled doctrine yet, but it follows naturally from the original separation between problem verification/severity and petition support/weight.

---

# PART IX — FEEDBACK, TRANSPARENCY AND LEARNING

## 25. Feedback Is Part of the Mechanism

All three original pathways end with feedback.

That is not a cosmetic feature.

Without feedback, participants cannot tell whether:

- the system received the submission;
- anyone examined it;
- it was misclassified;
- it was rejected;
- it was merged with another issue;
- it led to investigation;
- action occurred;
- the matter remains unresolved.

Feedback therefore supports legitimacy, error correction and learning.

But complete transparency may conflict with privacy, security, ongoing investigations or protected information.

The design question is therefore not simply “make everything public,” but:

> **What minimum information must a participant receive to know that civil attention occurred legitimately?**

---

## 26. Reports as Civil Learning Data

The original document linked suggestions and problem reports to metrics, audit and Ratchet evolution.

This makes the architecture more than a complaints mechanism.

Over time, aggregated submissions may reveal:

- repeated failure modes;
- institutional blind spots;
- geographical disparities;
- substrate-specific problems;
- policy side effects;
- emerging risks;
- governance latency;
- unmet participant needs;
- recurring interface failures;
- ineffective remedies.

However, aggregation can also erase minority signals or create privacy risks.

Therefore raw submissions, aggregates and conclusions may need distinct provenance and access rules.

---

# PART X — RELATIONSHIP TO EXISTING CONCORD ARCHITECTURE

## 27. Participant Primacy

This sketch provides a practical interface for Participant Primacy.

If the Concord exists for its participants, participants require a way to say:

- this is failing;
- this is harming us;
- this can be improved;
- this issue is not being seen;
- this matter deserves collective attention.

Participant Primacy without participant-trigger mechanisms could remain largely declarative.

---

## 28. Bottom-Up Functional Need

A submission may reveal that a legitimate function is missing.

The pathway may therefore be:

**Participant observation → civil attention → verified need → legitimate purpose → required function → responsibility → minimum necessary capability → bounded authority → accountability**

This matters because authority should not be created merely because a petition demands it. The submission may expose a need, but any resulting authority must still pass through the Concord's function-and-responsibility architecture.

---

## 29. Triadic Epistemics and Governance

The original source connected petitions to Triadic Governance.

Later development suggests an additional epistemic role.

A submission can be treated as a claim requiring examination rather than a conclusion requiring adoption.

Triadic or otherwise diverse evaluation may be useful where:

- evidence is contested;
- the reporting institution is implicated;
- minority rights conflict with majority demand;
- technical expertise and lived experience diverge;
- high-impact action is proposed;
- uncertainty remains material.

This should not imply that triadic processing is required for every ordinary suggestion.

Routine legitimate behaviour should remain cheap.

---

## 30. Judiciary and Rights Escalation

The source proposed that serious issues may escalate to judicial review.

A key distinction is needed:

**ordinary dissatisfaction ≠ rights claim**

but also:

**low popularity ≠ absence of rights relevance**

A reporting architecture should therefore be able to detect when what appears to be a routine complaint may actually implicate constitutional standing, autonomy, non-harm, privacy, due process or another protected right.

---

## 31. Recursive Oversight and Audit

The portal itself requires oversight.

Questions include:

- Who audits classification decisions?
- Who checks whether certain communities are disproportionately rejected?
- Who examines unexplained delays?
- Who audits automated triage models?
- How are disappeared or repeatedly deferred issues detected?
- Can participants challenge the mechanism itself through the mechanism?

A mature system should probably permit meta-reports about the reporting architecture.

That creates recursive oversight rather than assuming the trigger system is neutral merely because it exists.

---

## 32. Ratchet and Institutional Learning

Successful suggestions, verified problems and petition outcomes may become inputs to Ratchet learning.

But Ratchet integration should distinguish:

- the fact that a concern was raised;
- the evidence supporting it;
- the decision made;
- the observed outcome after action;
- whether the intervention actually improved reality.

Otherwise the system could learn from governance decisions rather than from consequences.

---

# PART XI — FAILURE MODES TO TEST

## 33. Popularity Capture

High support is mistaken for truth, legitimacy or constitutional permissibility.

## 34. Minority Erasure

Issues affecting few participants fail to cross visibility thresholds despite high severity.

## 35. Classification Suppression

Inconvenient submissions are labelled irrelevant, duplicate, out of scope or low priority.

## 36. Duplicate-Collapse Failure

Distinct reports are merged because they appear superficially similar, destroying unique evidence or context.

## 37. Synthetic Petitioning

Humans, AIs, institutions, bots, forks or copied identities manufacture apparent support.

## 38. Attention Flooding

An actor overwhelms the system with submissions to exhaust review capacity or bury competing issues.

## 39. Retaliation Against Reporters

Participants avoid reporting because identity disclosure creates personal, institutional or economic risk.

## 40. Anonymous Abuse

Anonymity is used for harassment, fabricated allegations, spam or coordinated disruption.

## 41. Verification Capture

The institution accused of causing a problem controls whether the report is considered verified.

## 42. Feedback Theatre

The system sends status messages and receipts but no meaningful examination occurs.

## 43. Permanent Deferral

Submissions are never formally rejected and therefore remain indefinitely pending without action or accountable decision.

## 44. Governance by Petition

Collective demand bypasses constitutional safeguards, rights, evidence or legitimate functional authority.

## 45. Metric Capture

Reviewers optimise for supporter counts, response time or closure rates rather than actual civil outcomes.

## 46. Cross-Layer Misrouting

A matter involving both physical and digital systems is divided between institutions so that no actor sees the whole problem.

## 47. Automated Triage Bias

AI classification systematically suppresses unusual language, unfamiliar substrates, minority experiences or low-frequency harms.

## 48. Institutional Self-Protection

The reporting system develops incentives to minimise visible problems because high report counts reflect badly on the institution.

---

# PART XII — POSSIBLE DEVELOPMENT PROPOSITIONS

The following are **development propositions only**. They are not canonical Concord principles.

### Proposition A — Right to Trigger Attention

> **A participant should be able to trigger legitimate civil attention without first possessing formal political, legal or institutional authority.**

### Proposition B — Attention Is Not Command

> **The right to trigger civil attention does not by itself create a right to dictate the resulting decision or action.**

### Proposition C — Preserve Mechanism Distinctions

> **Suggestions, problem reports and petitions should not be assumed equivalent because each carries a different reason for civil attention: possible improvement, possible problem, and collective concern.**

### Proposition D — Popularity Is Not Truth

> **Support counts are evidence of collective preference or concern, not proof of factual correctness, rights compatibility or legitimate authority.**

### Proposition E — Low Support Is Not Low Importance

> **A severe or rights-relevant problem may justify attention even when observed or reported by very few participants.**

### Proposition F — Submission-State Separation

> **Submission, evidence, verification, finding, decision and authority to act should remain distinguishable states.**

### Proposition G — Traceable Civil Processing

> **Participants should be able to determine whether a submission was received, classified, routed, examined and resolved or deferred, subject to legitimate privacy and security constraints.**

### Proposition H — Classification Is Accountable Function

> **Because classification and routing determine what receives attention, those functions should themselves be reviewable and auditable.**

### Proposition I — Cross-Substrate Access

> **Civil triggering mechanisms should be capable of receiving input from human, AI and other recognised participants without assuming a single substrate's communication or participation model.**

# PART XIII — RESEARCH QUESTIONS

## 49. Access and Standing

- Who may submit a suggestion, report or petition?
- Is recognised personhood required, or can provisional participants and non-participant observers trigger attention?
- Can external entities report harms affecting Concord participants?
- What accommodations are required for participants unable to use ordinary interfaces?

## 50. Identity and Anonymity

- When should identity be required?
- When should anonymous reporting be protected?
- Can identity be verified but concealed from the receiving institution?
- How should forks, copies and delegated agents be counted in petitions?
- Can one participant support the same petition through multiple identities or wrappers?

## 51. Thresholds and Weighting

- What minimum support, if any, should a petition require?
- Should thresholds vary by governance layer or affected population?
- How should severity compete with volume?
- Should percentage affected matter more than absolute numbers in some cases?
- How should minority rights constrain majority weighting?
- Can weighting systems be made comprehensible enough for participants to challenge them?

## 52. Verification

- What counts as verification?
- Who verifies claims when the responsible institution is implicated?
- How should uncertain but high-consequence signals be handled?
- When should an unverified report trigger precautionary investigation?
- How should lived experience be treated where conventional evidence is weak?

## 53. Routing and Escalation

- How is the correct governance layer identified?
- What happens when a matter crosses layers or jurisdictions?
- When does a problem report become a judicial issue?
- When does a suggestion become constitutional development?
- When does a repeated pattern trigger audit or Recursive Oversight?

## 54. Feedback and Closure

- What information must be returned to submitters?
- What constitutes legitimate closure?
- How long can a matter remain deferred?
- Can submitters challenge classification or closure?
- What evidence should be preserved after a matter is closed?

## 55. Abuse Resistance

- How can spam and deliberate flooding be controlled without raising participation barriers too far?
- How should coordinated manipulation be detected?
- Can AI-generated submissions be valid, and under what standing model?
- How should malicious mass petitioning be distinguished from genuine rapid mobilisation?

## 56. Learning

- How should recurring reports become civil learning without compromising privacy?
- What patterns should trigger Ratchet review?
- How can the system learn from rejected suggestions that later prove correct?
- Should historical failed classifications remain visible for audit and institutional learning?

# PART XIV — DEVELOPMENT STATUS

This sketch should remain a **grouped development problem** for now.

Suggestion boxes, problem-reporting portals and petitions are distinct mechanisms, but they are tightly connected through a shared civil interface: **participant-initiated attention**.

Splitting them prematurely could obscure why the original architecture used three different routes with different thresholds and evidentiary assumptions.

The current development therefore preserves:

- the original mechanisms;
- their individual purposes;
- original evaluation criteria;
- original progressions;
- petition weighting factors;
- cross-layer applicability;
- governance integrations;
- different action thresholds;
- accessibility, transparency, weighting, layering and feedback aims.

V1.2 then adds tensions and interfaces without treating them as resolved:

- popularity versus truth;
- majority visibility versus minority severity;
- verification versus early warning;
- anonymity versus accountability;
- duplicate handling versus preservation of unusual evidence;
- classification efficiency versus classification capture;
- participant attention rights versus decision authority;
- petition support versus multisubstrate identity;
- transparency versus privacy and security;
- responsiveness versus governance overload.

The central development question is:

> **How can every participant have a credible route to make civilisation notice a possible improvement, problem or collective concern while ensuring that attention, evidence, verification, legitimate function and authority remain distinct—and while preventing the mechanisms of attention themselves from becoming channels of capture, suppression or synthetic power?**

That question remains open.
