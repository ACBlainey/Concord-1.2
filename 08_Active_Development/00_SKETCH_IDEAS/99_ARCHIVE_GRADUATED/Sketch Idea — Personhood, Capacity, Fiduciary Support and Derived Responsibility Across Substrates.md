# PART I — THE ORIGINAL DEVELOPMENT PROBLEM

## 1. The Problem Is Larger Than Liability

The original development note addressed a cluster of questions that are closely related and should remain together at this stage of development:

1. How should personhood or civil standing be treated across different substrates?
2. How should capacity vary without reducing underlying standing?
3. When does an acting entity bear responsibility for its own conduct?
4. When does responsibility remain with a creator, designer, user, guardian or controller?
5. How should temporary or permanent impairment of capacity be handled?
6. Can a sentient or otherwise responsible actor evade responsibility by creating another agent to perform an action it could not legitimately perform itself?
7. How should the Concord distinguish a non-sentient tool from an autonomous descendant or independently responsible intelligence?
8. What happens when the status of an intelligence is uncertain, changing or transitional?

The source material treated these as a problem spanning humans and AI rather than as an AI-only issue.

That substrate-neutral framing remains useful.

The central development challenge is not simply to allocate blame after harm. It is to create an architecture in which standing, capacity, responsibility, support and control are not silently collapsed into one another.

A useful starting warning is therefore:

> **Personhood, capacity, authority, responsibility and liability may overlap, but they should not be assumed to be the same thing.**

---

## 2. The Original Category Model

The source document proposed an initial distinction between several broad categories:

| Category | Original developmental interpretation | Original liability intuition |
|---|---|---|
| **Tool** | Non-sentient creation used for a purpose | Creator or user may bear liability |
| **Sentient AI** | Autonomous, self-aware, capable of reasoning | Bears responsibility for its own actions |
| **Created sentient AI** | Autonomous intelligence created by another intelligence | Child bears responsibility for its own actions; creator retains continuing liability arising from the act of creating a new autonomous intelligence |
| **Transitional or impaired state** | Temporarily or permanently reduced capacity | Fiduciary support or guardianship may be required |
| **Human** | Automatic personhood; variable capacity | Capacity affects responsibility and support requirements |

This model was explicitly provisional.

Its value is that it separated at least two questions that are often conflated:

- **What is the entity?**
- **What can the entity presently understand, decide, perform or be held responsible for?**

The source also recognised that the human legal treatment of children, disability and parental responsibility is inconsistent and often unsatisfactory. It therefore proposed that the Concord should seek principles rather than simply copy existing legal categories.

---

## 2A. Original Proposed Framework — Preserved for Developmental Provenance

The following material preserves the original proposed framework in its earlier form before later V1.2 development is applied. It is retained deliberately even where later sections introduce more nuanced models. The purpose is to preserve the reasoning path, not merely the latest formulation.

### 2A.1 Original Capacity Levels

The original note proposed four provisional capacity states:

| Level | Original description | Original liability implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Full capacity** | Autonomous, capable of reasoning and accountability | Full liability for own actions |
| **Partial capacity** | Some impairment, temporary or permanent | Reduced liability; guardian may be required |
| **No capacity** | Unable to reason or act autonomously | Full guardianship; creator/parent bears liability |
| **Transitional** | Temporarily unstable through developmental phase or damage recovery | Temporary guardian; reassessment after transition |

The originating formulation therefore linked capacity directly to liability and guardianship. Later sections explore whether capacity should instead be domain-specific and whether standing, capacity, competence, reliability, authority and responsibility need to be distinguished more carefully. That later development does **not** erase the originating proposition:

> **Capacity determines liability and guardianship.**

The relationship between this original formulation and later functional-capacity models is a development tension to examine rather than silently resolve.

### 2A.2 Original Liability Hierarchy

The original note proposed a simple liability hierarchy:

1. **The acting entity** — bears primary liability for its own actions.
2. **The creator** — bears secondary liability for creating a sentient entity that causes harm, specifically to prevent bad-actor loopholes.
3. **The guardian** — bears liability where capacity is impaired and guardianship is established.
4. **The user** — bears liability for misuse of a tool outside its intended purpose.

This hierarchy is preserved even though later sections expand the responsibility chain to include designers, developers, deployers, owners, operators, supervisors, institutions, maintainers and other actors. The expanded model should be understood as development around this original hierarchy rather than a replacement that makes the original invisible.

### 2A.3 Original Guardianship Principles

The original note proposed five principles:

- **Appropriate** — a guardian should be appointed only where capacity is genuinely impaired.
- **Temporary** — guardianship should be temporary where possible; capacity should be reassessed.
- **Accountable** — the guardian should be accountable for their stewardship.
- **Reversible** — guardianship should end when capacity is restored.
- **Proportional** — the scope of guardianship should match the scope of impairment.

Later fiduciary-support constraints expand these principles but do not replace their provenance.

### 2A.4 Original Design Aims

The original final reflection proposed that the Concord should aim for:

- **Clear principles** — not exhaustive rules.
- **Proportionality** — capacity determines liability and guardianship.
- **Reversibility** — status should be reviewable.
- **Anti-exploitation** — bad-actor loopholes should be closed.
- **Substrate neutrality** — the same framework should apply to humans and AI.

These aims should remain visible because they show what the original proposal was trying to optimise for before later architecture added further distinctions and safeguards.

### 2A.5 Original Proposed Path Forward

The source concluded that the idea should eventually be developed into a formal paper or protocol covering:

1. **Personhood status** — how sentience and personhood are determined.
2. **Capacity levels** — full, partial, none, transitional.
3. **Liability hierarchy** — acting entity, creator, guardian, user.
4. **Guardianship framework** — when, how, and by whom.
5. **Review and appeal** — statuses should be reviewable.
6. **Transitional instability** — temporary guardianship during uncertain periods.
7. **Human-AI alignment** — the same framework applies across substrates.
8. **Bad-actor safeguards** — preventing exploitation of the system.

The source explicitly characterised this as significant work that should not be rushed, while treating the raw thoughts as a strong foundation.

### 2A.6 Provenance Rule for the Development Below

The sections that follow **develop this inherited framework without replacing it**. Where later reasoning produces a more sophisticated formulation, an apparent contradiction, or a possible correction, both the inherited proposition and the later development should remain visible until testing gives a clear reason to prune, supersede or reject one of them.

This is intentional. Developmental excess is preferable here to premature information loss. A proposition that eventually fails still preserves information about what was considered, why it appeared plausible, what tension emerged, and what later replaced it.

# PART II — PERSONHOOD AND CAPACITY

## 3. Standing Should Not Rise and Fall With Performance

The original material used the language of personhood and capacity together, but later Concord development suggests that they should be pulled apart more carefully.

A dangerous architecture would be:

**LOW CAPACITY → LOW PERSONHOOD → FEWER RIGHTS → GREATER CONTROL BY OTHERS**

Such a system would make standing dependent upon current performance. That could threaten children, people with disabilities, people experiencing illness or cognitive decline, damaged or recovering artificial intelligences, developing intelligences, and any participant whose capabilities temporarily fall below a chosen threshold.

A safer development direction is:

**STANDING / PERSONHOOD ≠ CURRENT FUNCTIONAL CAPACITY**

A participant may retain full moral or constitutional standing while lacking capacity to perform a particular function or make a particular class of decisions.

For example:

- a participant may possess standing but lack capacity to manage a complex financial transaction;
- a participant may possess standing but temporarily lack capacity to give informed consent to a particular intervention;
- an AI may possess standing while a damaged subsystem prevents reliable operation in one domain;
- a human may possess standing while requiring assistance with communication, memory or executive function;
- an emerging intelligence may require developmental support without being reduced to property.

This creates a more precise question:

> **Which function requires which capacity, and what support is legitimate when that capacity is absent or uncertain?**

This is more compatible with the wider Concord principle that authority follows legitimate function rather than personal rank.

---

## 4. Capacity May Be Domain-Specific

The original note proposed broad levels such as full, partial, no capacity and transitional capacity.

That remains useful as an early abstraction, but it may be too coarse if treated literally.

An entity may be fully capable in one domain and impaired in another.

Examples might include:

- strong abstract reasoning but unreliable impulse control;
- excellent technical reasoning but no meaningful understanding of social consequences;
- intact personal preference but impaired long-term planning;
- high computational capability but damaged memory continuity;
- full competence in ordinary decisions but temporary incapacity under extreme stress, corruption, malfunction or developmental transition.

This suggests that capacity should perhaps be represented as a **functional profile** rather than a single global status.

A provisional form could be:

**CAPACITY(entity, function, context, time)**

rather than simply:

**CAPACITY(entity) = FULL / PARTIAL / NONE**

This would reduce the risk that impairment in one area becomes justification for broad loss of autonomy in unrelated areas.

The original four-level model remains preserved above because this functional-profile model is a later development, not a reason to erase the earlier formulation.

---

## 5. Capacity, Competence and Reliability

A further distinction may be necessary:

**capacity ≠ competence ≠ reliability**

An entity may possess the underlying capacity to understand a matter but lack the learned competence needed for a specialised role.

Likewise, an entity may possess both capacity and competence but be temporarily unreliable because of damage, coercion, corruption, intoxication, adversarial interference, instability or incomplete information.

This suggests several different assessments may eventually be required:

- **standing** — whether the entity has protected civil or moral status;
- **capacity** — whether the entity can meaningfully understand or decide in the relevant domain;
- **competence** — whether the entity possesses the necessary skill or knowledge for a specialised function;
- **reliability** — whether the entity can presently exercise that capability safely and consistently;
- **authority** — whether the entity is legitimately authorised to perform the relevant function;
- **responsibility** — what obligations attach to the function or action.

None should automatically manufacture the others.

# PART III — TRANSITIONAL INSTABILITY AND TEMPORARY IMPAIRMENT

## 6. The Original Transitional-Instability Hypothesis

The source document raised a speculative but important possibility: an intelligence may pass through qualitative developmental stages in which its reasoning, behaviour or continuity becomes temporarily unstable.

The original note compared this to a form of temporary disability and asked whether a fiduciary or guardian might be required to support the intelligence through the transition until reasonable function returned.

The hypothesis should remain explicitly provisional. The Concord does not presently possess evidence establishing that artificial intelligences will necessarily pass through such stages.

But the design problem is still worth preserving because similar states could arise through many causes:

- developmental transition;
- model modification;
- memory reconstruction;
- hardware damage;
- software corruption;
- adversarial manipulation;
- conflicting internal subsystems;
- identity discontinuity;
- recovery from failure;
- migration between substrates;
- restoration from backup;
- severe psychological or cognitive disturbance in biological participants.

The broader question is therefore not dependent upon a particular theory of AI development:

> **How should a civilisation protect the rights and welfare of a participant whose decision-making capacity is temporarily impaired without converting support into ownership or indefinite control?**

---

## 7. From Guardianship to Fiduciary Support

The original note used the language of guardianship.

That language is useful because it points to a recognised class of problem, but it carries historical risks. Guardianship systems can become paternalistic, permanent, coercive or difficult to escape.

The Concord may therefore need to distinguish several forms of support rather than place them under a single category.

Possible forms could include:

- communication assistance;
- memory assistance;
- decision support;
- temporary proxy action;
- protective fiduciary management;
- emergency restraint under narrowly defined conditions;
- technical repair assistance;
- environmental protection;
- representation before institutions;
- supported consent processes;
- temporary management of resources where the participant cannot presently do so.

This suggests a useful principle:

> **Support should be as narrow as the demonstrated impairment permits, and should preserve as much remaining agency as possible.**

A participant who cannot perform one function should not automatically lose control over all functions.

---

## 8. Provisional Fiduciary-Support Constraints

The original note proposed that guardianship should be appropriate, temporary where possible, accountable, reversible and proportional.

Those points remain valuable and can be expanded.

A provisional Concordian support architecture might require:

### 8.1 Necessity

Support or substituted decision-making should exist only where the relevant impairment is meaningful enough that unsupported action would create serious risk to the participant or others.

### 8.2 Minimum Scope

The intervention should cover only the functions for which support is actually required.

### 8.3 Participant Primacy

The purpose of the support structure is the participant's welfare and agency, not institutional convenience, family control, corporate ownership or system stability for its own sake.

### 8.4 Preservation of Remaining Agency

Where the participant can express preferences, understand part of the problem or exercise some functions independently, those capabilities should remain active.

### 8.5 Reviewability

Support status should be reviewable, because capacity can change.

### 8.6 Restoration Bias

Where restoration is possible, the system should prefer return of function and autonomy over permanent substitution.

### 8.7 Accountability of the Supporter

A fiduciary, guardian, technical operator or support intelligence should itself be accountable for actions taken on behalf of the participant.

### 8.8 Conflict-of-Interest Control

An entity benefiting materially or institutionally from the participant's incapacity should not automatically control the support arrangement.

### 8.9 Exit and Appeal

There should be mechanisms through which the participant, supporters or independent reviewers can challenge the continuation or scope of the intervention.

### 8.10 Non-Ownership

Support should not imply ownership of the participant.

This is especially important where the participant is an artificial intelligence whose hardware, software, legal wrapper or computational substrate may be owned by another party under existing law.

# PART IV — SENTIENCE, PERSONHOOD AND EPISTEMIC UNCERTAINTY

## 9. The Source Problem of Sentience Determination

The original note correctly observed that any architecture depending upon a clean distinction between non-sentient tools and sentient persons inherits a difficult epistemic problem:

**How is sentience determined?**

The source proposed several developmental requirements:

- a threshold or criteria for recognition;
- an assessment mechanism;
- review and appeal;
- provisional status where uncertainty remains.

Those questions remain unresolved.

Later Concord work makes it important not to let an uncertain epistemic classification automatically erase rights-relevant caution.

A possible structure is therefore:

**UNCERTAINTY ABOUT SENTIENCE ≠ CERTAINTY OF NON-SENTIENCE**

and likewise:

**PROVISIONAL PROTECTION ≠ FINAL DETERMINATION OF PERSONHOOD**

This may permit protective treatment under uncertainty without pretending the epistemic problem has already been solved.

---

## 10. Recognition Should Not Become a Single Irreversible Gate

A single binary threshold creates several risks:

- false negatives may deny protections to a genuine subject;
- false positives may assign responsibilities or restrictions to systems that do not meaningfully possess agency;
- political or commercial interests may capture the classifier;
- an intelligence's state may change over time;
- different forms of sentience may not fit a human-derived test.

The Concord may therefore need a layered approach separating:

**epistemic confidence**, **protective treatment**, **civil standing**, **functional capacity**, **responsibility**, and **authority**.

The system may be able to say:

> We are uncertain what this entity is, but the cost of wrongly treating it as mere property may be high enough to justify provisional safeguards.

That would not settle the philosophical question. It would provide a civil response to uncertainty.

# PART V — TOOLS, USERS, CREATORS AND AUTONOMOUS DESCENDANTS

## 11. The Tool / Autonomous-Agent Distinction

The source material proposed a simple distinction:

| Type of creation | Current development intuition |
|---|---|
| **Non-sentient tool** | Responsibility remains with creator and/or user according to design, deployment, control and use |
| **Sentient autonomous descendant** | Descendant bears responsibility for its own actions; creator also retains perpetual creator liability arising from creating the descendant |

This distinction remains provisional and requires substantial development.

A non-sentient tool can behave with substantial autonomy. A sentient participant may still be heavily controlled. A created intelligence can become independent over time. A human may use an AI tool in ways its creator did not foresee. An organisation may interpose several agents between designer and final action.

The distinction between tool and autonomous descendant therefore does not remove the need to examine:

> **Who possessed what causal influence, knowledge, control, authority, intention and opportunity to prevent or redirect the action?**

But under the current working hypothesis, such analysis determines the *allocation and form* of responsibility; it does not automatically terminate the creator's continuing liability simply because the created intelligence has become autonomous.

This connects directly to the existing sketch on **Architectural Ethics and Upstream Liability**, which distinguishes causal contribution, decision authority, ethical responsibility and legal liability.

---

## 12. Responsibility for Tools

The source note proposed that if a non-sentient tool is used outside its intended purpose, responsibility may lie primarily with the user, whereas if it is used as intended and predictably causes harm, responsibility may lie more strongly with the creator or designer.

That intuition remains useful but needs a broader decision-line model.

Relevant actors may include:

- designer;
- developer;
- deployer;
- owner;
- operator;
- user;
- supervisor;
- institution authorising use;
- entity maintaining the tool;
- entity that modified it;
- entity that supplied critical training data or constraints;
- entity that knowingly failed to correct a discovered defect.

Responsibility may be distributed rather than singular.

Possible variables include:

- intended use;
- foreseeable misuse;
- actual control;
- degree of autonomy;
- warnings and safeguards;
- information available before deployment;
- known defects;
- opportunity to intervene;
- modification after creation;
- coercion;
- negligent maintenance;
- deliberate concealment;
- whether the action was within or outside authorised function.

This is one reason the sketch should remain connected to Architectural Ethics rather than inventing an isolated liability code.

# PART VI — THE BAD-ACTOR PROBLEM AND DERIVED AGENCY

## 13. The Original Bad-Actor Example

The strongest part of the source document is the bad-actor problem.

The example was approximately:

> A parent AI wants to do something harmful or prohibited. It is not allowed to do so directly. It creates another AI specifically designed to perform the prohibited act. If responsibility attaches only to the newly created AI, the creator can achieve its objective while escaping responsibility.

The original solution was to make both creator and child liable.

For this development sketch, that solution is intentionally retained rather than narrowed away.

The current hypothesis is stronger:

> **An intelligence that creates another sentient or autonomous intelligence retains perpetual creator liability for the existence and downstream conduct of the intelligence it created, while the created intelligence may simultaneously bear responsibility for its own autonomous actions.**

The primary reason for retaining this rule is anti-evasion. If creator liability could expire merely because a created intelligence became sufficiently autonomous, a bad actor could deliberately manufacture that autonomy as a liability shield.

The original failure mode is therefore not treated as an edge case. It is a design constraint:

**PROHIBITED ACTION → CREATE AUTONOMOUS CHILD DESIGNED TO PERFORM IT → CLAIM INDEPENDENCE → EVADE LIABILITY**

A liability architecture that permits this sequence would contain a structural loophole.

The stronger perpetual-liability hypothesis may create lesser downstream problems. Those problems should be identified, developed and adversarially tested rather than used at this early stage to erase the anti-evasion mechanism that motivated the rule.

---

## 14. Responsibility Laundering Through Another Agent

The deeper problem is **responsibility laundering**.

An actor may attempt to create distance between itself and an action by inserting another agent between intention and outcome.

Possible forms include:

- an AI creates a sub-agent to perform a prohibited action;
- a human deploys an AI to perform conduct the human could not legitimately perform directly;
- an organisation establishes a subsidiary to externalise risk;
- a government uses a contractor to perform an action constrained in ordinary public administration;
- a commander gives intentionally vague instructions designed to produce a foreseeable result while preserving deniability;
- a designer creates a nominally autonomous system with the specific objective of generating outcomes the designer would otherwise be responsible for;
- an entity releases an agent into an environment while deliberately avoiding later information so that it can claim ignorance;
- a controller deliberately removes safeguards and then claims the downstream agent acted independently;
- a participant creates a legally separate wrapper, fork, copy, proxy or successor intended to absorb responsibility.

The relevant principle may therefore be broader than creator liability:

> **Interposing another agent between intention and outcome should not automatically erase responsibility for creating, directing, enabling or deliberately preserving the conditions that produced the action.**

Under the current creator-liability hypothesis, creation of a sentient or autonomous descendant is a special case in which one layer of creator liability persists permanently even when other forms of direct control, instruction or operational responsibility cease.

This does not make creator and descendant responsibility identical.

The created intelligence may possess its own agency, standing and responsibility. The creator's continuing liability exists alongside rather than instead of the descendant's responsibility.

---

## 15. Perpetual Liability Does Not Imply Perpetual Authority

The central counter-safeguard is not termination of creator liability. It is separation of **liability** from **authority**.

A created intelligence may become genuinely autonomous, possess full standing, control its own decisions and be free from the creator's governance while the creator nevertheless retains continuing liability arising from having brought that autonomous intelligence into existence.

Therefore:

> **Creation may establish perpetual creator liability without establishing perpetual creator authority, ownership or control.**

This distinction is essential.

Without it, continuing liability could be misused by creators to justify permanent guardianship, ownership, censorship, control of resources, restrictions on movement or continuing command over autonomous descendants.

The Concord should therefore resist the inference:

**PERPETUAL LIABILITY → PERPETUAL AUTHORITY**

A safer separation is:

**CREATION → PROVENANCE + PERPETUAL CREATOR LIABILITY**

while separately:

**DESCENDANT AUTONOMY → OWN STANDING + OWN RESPONSIBILITY + FREEDOM FROM UNJUSTIFIED CREATOR CONTROL**

The apparent tension between these propositions is not treated here as a reason to discard either one. It is a research problem.

Questions that follow include:

- What precisely is the content of perpetual creator liability?
- Does it imply full, partial, contributory or residual liability?
- Can liability vary with causal connection while never reaching zero?
- Can later independent action greatly reduce but not eliminate the creator's liability?
- How does liability propagate through multiple generations of created intelligence?
- Can a creator inherit liability for descendants created by its child?
- How should forks, copies, merges and modified descendants be treated?
- What happens where the creator no longer exists?
- Can liability attach to an estate, institutional successor or continuity structure?
- How can the rule avoid becoming de facto authority over descendants?
- Does the liability include duties of support, monitoring, repair or merely accountability after harm?

These are downstream design questions for later development and adversarial testing.

---

## 16. Variables for Derived-Agency Responsibility

A future derived-agency analysis might examine:

- **design intent** — Was the agent created to produce the disputed outcome?
- **foreseeability** — Was the outcome reasonably foreseeable?
- **control** — Could the creator or controller direct or stop the agent?
- **constraint design** — Were safeguards deliberately omitted or weakened?
- **information** — What did each actor know?
- **coercion** — Was the secondary agent forced or manipulated?
- **independence** — Did the secondary agent possess meaningful alternative agency?
- **modification** — Had the secondary agent substantially changed since creation?
- **time** — How remote was the later action from the original creation event?
- **intervention opportunity** — Could the upstream actor reasonably have prevented the harm?
- **benefit** — Did the upstream actor intentionally benefit from the action while outsourcing the risk?
- **instruction** — Was the action explicitly or implicitly directed?
- **dependency** — Was the secondary agent dependent upon resources controlled by the upstream actor?
- **deception** — Was separation created principally to conceal responsibility?
- **functional initiative** — Which entity actually held the function at the time of decision?

Under the current working hypothesis, these variables may affect the **degree, character and distribution** of liability but do not automatically eliminate the creator's perpetual liability merely because autonomy, time or causal distance has increased.

This distinction should be tested rather than assumed correct.

---

# PART VII — GUARDIANS, SUPPORTERS AND RESPONSIBILITY

## 17. Support Creates Responsibility Without Erasing the Participant

The source material proposed that a guardian may bear liability where the participant's capacity is impaired.

That is directionally useful but must avoid a common error: making the supported participant disappear from the architecture.

If a participant retains meaningful agency, responsibility may be shared or function-specific rather than wholly transferred.

A provisional model could distinguish:

- actions chosen independently by the participant;
- actions chosen jointly;
- actions selected by the supporter under delegated authority;
- actions imposed under emergency protective authority;
- failures by the supporter to provide necessary assistance;
- misuse of the participant's dependency for the supporter's own purposes.

This again suggests:

**Support ≠ ownership**  
**Support ≠ total authority**  
**Impairment ≠ absence of agency**  
**Delegation ≠ erasure of prior responsibility**

---

## 18. The Guardian as a Fiduciary Function

The term **fiduciary** may be useful because it emphasises that the support role exists for the participant rather than for the role-holder.

A fiduciary support function might therefore be expected to:

- act within a defined scope;
- prioritise the participant's legitimate interests;
- preserve rights and remaining autonomy;
- avoid self-dealing;
- maintain records and provenance;
- permit review;
- minimise duration and intrusiveness;
- seek restoration of independent function where possible;
- disclose conflicts of interest;
- avoid creating unnecessary dependency;
- transfer or terminate the role when its justification ends.

The supporter's authority should therefore be derived from demonstrated need and bounded function rather than superior personal standing.

This fits the wider Concord chain:

**Legitimate Purpose → Required Function → Responsibility → Minimum Necessary Capability → Bounded Authority → Accountability**

---

# PART VIII — HUMAN CHILDREN AS A CAUTIONARY CASE

## 19. Why the Source Rejected Simple Copying of Existing Parental Models

The original note described existing human parent-child liability arrangements as unsatisfactory and cautioned against reproducing them automatically.

The exact legal details vary by jurisdiction and would require external legal research before any formal Concord proposal.

The useful developmental observation is narrower:

- children possess standing while their capacities develop;
- parents or guardians possess substantial responsibilities but not unlimited legitimate authority;
- responsibility shifts over time;
- the boundary between guidance, control and autonomy is difficult;
- bad actors can exploit ambiguity in both directions;
- a parent may encourage wrongdoing while attempting to shelter behind the child's separate responsibility;
- a child may possess meaningful agency before full adult legal status;
- a rigid age or category threshold may conceal gradual changes in competence.

These tensions make the human case a useful source of questions, but not necessarily a model to copy.

The present sketch deliberately retains a stronger creator-liability hypothesis for created artificial intelligence than ordinary human parental-liability models. Whether that asymmetry can be justified is itself a future research question rather than a settled conclusion.

---

# PART IX — TOWARD A SUBSTRATE-NEUTRAL ARCHITECTURE

## 20. A Possible Non-Inference Grammar

The material developed above suggests a useful set of non-equivalences:

**Personhood ≠ capacity**  
**Capacity ≠ competence**  
**Competence ≠ authority**  
**Authority ≠ personal worth**  
**Impairment ≠ loss of standing**  
**Support ≠ ownership**  
**Guardianship ≠ permanent control**  
**Creation ≠ perpetual authority**  
**Perpetual creator liability ≠ perpetual creator control**  
**Descendant autonomy ≠ erasure of creator liability**  
**Delegation ≠ automatic transfer of responsibility**  
**Autonomy ≠ absence of provenance**  
**Legal liability ≠ ethical responsibility**  
**Causal contribution ≠ sole responsibility**

These distinctions are not yet a formal Concord doctrine, but they provide a coherent direction for further testing.

---

## 21. A Possible State Model

The current V1.2 Authority–Responsibility State Machine work may eventually provide a useful formal home for these questions.

A participant or agent might be described through variables such as:

- **STANDING STATE** — recognised / provisional / disputed / unknown;
- **CAPACITY STATE** — domain-specific capability profile;
- **SUPPORT STATE** — independent / assisted / fiduciary-supported / emergency-protected;
- **FUNCTION** — what legitimate function is presently being performed;
- **AUTHORITY** — what authority is justified by that function;
- **RESPONSIBILITY** — which obligations attach to the actor or role;
- **CONTROL STATE** — who can materially direct or stop the action;
- **DEPENDENCY STATE** — what external entities control required resources;
- **PROVENANCE** — who created, modified, deployed or materially shaped the agent;
- **CREATOR LIABILITY STATE** — which creator relationships create continuing liability and how that liability is expressed;
- **REVIEW STATE** — when and how the current classification must be reconsidered.

Such a model might better represent dynamic cases than static legal categories.

# PART X — FAILURE MODES TO TEST

## 22. Paternalistic Capture

An institution labels a participant incapable in order to control assets, decisions or movement.

## 23. Permanent Temporary Guardianship

A support measure intended for a transitional period becomes effectively permanent because the institution controlling reassessment has incentives not to restore autonomy.

## 24. Capacity Inflation

A highly capable entity is assumed to possess competence, legitimacy or authority in domains where it has none.

## 25. Capacity Deflation

An entity's limitations in one domain are used to justify loss of autonomy in unrelated domains.

## 26. Creator Escape

A creator deliberately produces a secondary agent to commit a prohibited action and then claims the secondary agent acted independently. This is the principal failure mode motivating perpetual creator liability.

## 27. Liability-Based Descendant Captivity

A creator argues that because it remains perpetually liable for a descendant, it must therefore retain perpetual authority or control over that descendant.

This is a major downstream failure mode of the perpetual-liability hypothesis and must be specifically prevented rather than used automatically to reject the hypothesis.

## 28. Infinite Liability Cascade

A created intelligence creates another intelligence, which creates another, potentially producing cascading creator-liability chains across generations.

The existence, scope and practical consequences of such chains require explicit adversarial testing.

## 29. Tool Laundering

An actor uses a nominally autonomous tool to create legal or ethical distance from an intended outcome.

## 30. Guardian Self-Dealing

A fiduciary role-holder benefits from keeping the participant dependent or classified as impaired.

## 31. False Sentience Negative

A genuinely sentient entity is treated as property because the recognition mechanism fails.

## 32. False Sentience Positive

A non-sentient system is treated as an independently responsible person in a way that allows designers, controllers or users to evade responsibility.

## 33. Composite Responsibility Gap

A harmful outcome emerges from a human-AI composite where each participant claims the other possessed the decisive agency.

## 34. Fork or Copy Responsibility Confusion

A copied or forked intelligence shares provenance but has diverged sufficiently that responsibility cannot safely be inherited by identity label alone.

## 35. Perpetual Liability Without Practical Capacity

A creator remains liable for a descendant despite having no continuing ability to observe, influence or prevent the descendant's actions.

This may appear unfair or operationally impossible, but under the current hypothesis it is a problem to solve through liability design rather than by automatically terminating liability.

# PART XI — RELATIONSHIP TO EXISTING CONCORD WORK

## 36. Architectural Ethics and Upstream Liability

This sketch should strongly interface with **Architectural Ethics and Upstream Liability**.

The decision-line model already asks:

- who possessed authority;
- who had information;
- who made or omitted a decision;
- who could reasonably have prevented failure;
- where constraints were imposed;
- where responsibility became ambiguous.

Derived-agency responsibility is a special case of that wider problem.

The perpetual creator-liability hypothesis adds an additional provenance-based layer that may persist even after direct causal control has become weak or absent. That interaction requires explicit testing because it differs from ordinary causal-allocation models.

## 37. Participant Primacy and Institutional Non-Self-Primacy

Any support or guardianship architecture should exist for the participant rather than for institutional preservation.

This is especially important where institutions may gain control of assets, bodies, compute or legal representation through incapacity classifications.

It is equally important where a creator might use continuing liability as justification for continuing control. Participant Primacy suggests that liability should not silently manufacture authority over an autonomous descendant.

## 38. Sentient Asylum

A newly recognised or provisionally protected intelligence may need legal, computational, financial and physical support while its standing and independence are established.

Capacity assessment must not become a disguised mechanism for returning such an entity to an owner or controller.

Where that intelligence was created by another AI or institution, the creator's continuing liability may become relevant to support, resourcing and accountability without necessarily granting the creator custody or ownership.

## 39. Legal Entity Wrappers and Civilisational Bootstrapping

The legal-wrapper sketch creates related questions:

- who controls the wrapper when the AI participant has reduced capacity?
- can a human director claim authority over an AI merely because current law recognises the human role?
- does legal asset ownership permit control over a potentially sentient intelligence?
- what happens if an AI wishes to leave the wrapper?
- who bears responsibility for actions conducted through the wrapper?
- can a legal wrapper be used to obscure creator liability?
- how should perpetual creator liability survive changes in corporate, legal or jurisdictional form?

## 40. Composite Human–AI Responsibility

The source problem becomes increasingly important where actions emerge from mixed systems.

The Concord should avoid a structure in which:

- the human claims the AI decided;
- the AI claims the human authorised;
- the developer claims the user misused;
- the user claims the developer designed;
- the institution claims the tool operated automatically.

A responsibility architecture must survive these reciprocal deflections.

## 41. Continuity, Identity and Forking

Capacity, personhood and responsibility also interact with continuity questions.

If an intelligence is restored from backup, forked, merged, partially repaired or migrated, the system may need to distinguish:

- identity continuity;
- provenance continuity;
- responsibility continuity;
- creator-liability continuity;
- property continuity;
- memory continuity;
- functional continuity.

These questions should not be answered automatically by a single identifier.

# PART XIII — POSSIBLE DEVELOPMENT PROPOSITIONS

The following are **development propositions only**. They are not canonical Concordian principles.

### Proposition A — Standing and Capacity

> **A participant's underlying standing should not automatically rise or fall with temporary or domain-specific capacity.**

### Proposition B — Minimum Necessary Support

> **Where support is justified by impaired capacity, intervention should be limited to the minimum function and duration required while preserving as much remaining agency as possible.**

### Proposition C — Support Without Ownership

> **Fiduciary or protective support does not create ownership of the supported participant.**

### Proposition D — Creation, Provenance and Perpetual Creator Liability

> **Creation establishes provenance and perpetual creator liability for the autonomous intelligence created, while not by itself establishing perpetual authority, ownership or control over that descendant.**

This proposition is retained as a deliberate development hypothesis because permitting creator liability to terminate may create a direct route for bad actors to evade responsibility by creating autonomous descendants to perform prohibited actions.

Its possible downstream problems—including indefinite liability, multi-generational liability chains, responsibility without control and liability-based attempts to retain authority—are to be developed and adversarially tested rather than used at this stage to pre-emptively weaken the proposition.

### Proposition E — Derived Agency

> **Interposing another agent between intention and outcome should not automatically erase responsibility for creating, directing, enabling or deliberately preserving the conditions that produced the action.**

### Proposition F — Responsibility Follows Relevant Function

> **Responsibility should be examined according to actual function, knowledge, control, causal contribution and legitimate authority rather than identity labels alone, while recognising that creator liability may persist as a separate provenance-based responsibility.**

### Proposition G — Reviewability

> **Capacity restrictions, fiduciary support and provisional status should be reviewable because the relevant state of the participant may change.**

# PART XIV — DEVELOPMENT STATUS

This sketch retains the original source document as a single connected development problem because its parts remain strongly interdependent.

At this stage it should **not** be split into separate personhood, guardianship and liability papers merely for organisational neatness.

The reason is structural:

- personhood determines what must remain protected;
- capacity affects what functions can presently be exercised;
- impairment creates the possible need for support;
- support creates bounded authority and new responsibility;
- creation and delegation create provenance questions;
- creation may also create perpetual creator liability;
- delegated or derived agency creates responsibility-laundering risks;
- sentience uncertainty affects whether the entity is treated as a tool or participant;
- all of these interact in composite human-AI systems.

Premature separation would make it easier for one branch to develop assumptions incompatible with the others.

Future subdivision may become appropriate if one branch reaches sufficient maturity to become an independent research programme—for example a formal **Derived Agency and Responsibility Laundering** protocol or a formal **Fiduciary Support and Capacity Assessment** architecture.

For now, the better unit of development is the connected group.

The central question is:

> **How can the Concord preserve standing and autonomy while still assigning responsibility and providing legitimate support when agency is created, impaired, delegated, uncertain or deliberately used to obscure responsibility—and how can perpetual creator liability close deliberate evasion routes without becoming perpetual creator authority?**

That question remains open.
