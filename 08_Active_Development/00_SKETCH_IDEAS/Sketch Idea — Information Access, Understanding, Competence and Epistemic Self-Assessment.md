# Sketch Idea — Information Access, Understanding, Competence and Epistemic Self-Assessment

*A development sketch on education, apparent knowledge, genuine understanding, competence, dependence, unknowns, unknowability and the problem of judging our own capacity*

**Status:** SKETCH IDEA / ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / NOT CANONICAL  
**Development context:** Concord V1.2  
**Source status:** Expanded from the Development Archive paper *The Problem of Thinking You Are Educated.md*. The originating argument, analogy, rhetorical force, generational claims, conclusions and closing irony are deliberately preserved before wider V1.2 development is added. Unsupported or insufficiently evidenced claims are marked as such rather than silently removed or corrected. Later development extends the source from education into epistemic self-assessment, functional competence, dependency and the limits of knowability.

---

# PART I — THE ORIGINAL DEVELOPMENT PROBLEM

## 1. The Problem Is Not Merely Education

The originating paper focused on a pedagogical concern: the difference between **genuine education** and the ability to **locate and reproduce information**.

Its central intuition remains important:

> **Access to information is not the same as understanding it.**

But the problem extends beyond schools, universities, qualifications and assignments.

It concerns a more general civilisational weakness:

> **How do we know whether we actually understand something, rather than merely possessing access to information that gives us the appearance of understanding?**

That question immediately expands into several related problems:

- what we know;
- what we think we know;
- what we know that we do not know;
- what we do not know that we do not know;
- what we may be unable to know;
- what our tools know on our behalf;
- what a human–AI composite can do even when neither component individually holds the whole competence;
- how we judge our own capacity when the very limitations being judged may impair the judgment itself.

The sketch therefore begins with education but develops into a broader problem of **epistemic self-assessment and functional competence**.

# PART II — ORIGINAL SOURCE ARGUMENT — PRESERVED FOR DEVELOPMENTAL PROVENANCE

The following sections preserve the originating paper's substantive argument before later V1.2 development is introduced.

## 2. Original Abstract

The source paper argued that a critical pedagogical concern had emerged from a growing disconnect between genuine education and the mere ability to locate and reproduce information.

It proposed that digital tools—particularly search engines and artificial intelligence—had altered the educational landscape in ways that could allow students to mistake information access for genuine understanding.

The source framed this strongly as a generational educational problem. That framing is preserved here because it is part of the originating proposition, although later sections distinguish the core insight from claims that would require independent evidence.

---

## 3. Original Introduction

The source argued that the distinction between **being educated** and **appearing educated** had become increasingly difficult to discern in contemporary academic environments.

It acknowledged that confusion between factual recall and genuine understanding was not new, but proposed that digital tools had accelerated the problem substantially.

Its concern was not merely whether learners could obtain correct answers. It was whether the process through which knowledge becomes integrated into the learner had been bypassed.

The original question can therefore be represented as:

**CAN RETRIEVE INFORMATION → APPEARS EDUCATED**

versus:

**HAS UNDERSTOOD, INTEGRATED AND CAN RECONSTRUCT / APPLY INFORMATION → IS EDUCATED IN THE RELEVANT DOMAIN**

The source did not formalise this distinction, but it is implicit throughout the paper.

---

## 4. The Original Library Analogy

The library analogy is the conceptual centre of the source and should be preserved in full substance.

### 4.1 The First Library Process

The source asks the reader to imagine entering a library and selecting a book from a shelf.

The learner then:

1. reads the book thoroughly;
2. digests its contents;
3. understands its arguments;
4. internalises its concepts;
5. returns the original book to its shelf;
6. rewrites the book, as far as possible, from the knowledge acquired;
7. places the learner's reconstructed version on another shelf.

The source treats this as an analogy for genuine education and learning.

The critical feature is not memorisation alone. The learner has processed the source through their own cognitive framework and produced a reconstruction or synthesis that demonstrates some degree of internalisation.

### 4.2 The Second Library Process

The source then proposes an alternative.

The learner removes the original book from one shelf and immediately places it on the second shelf without engaging with the book's contents.

The source uses this as an analogy for a learner who uses a search engine or AI to locate information and reproduce it in an assignment without meaningful cognitive engagement.

The external output may look similar:

**information has moved from source to assignment**.

But the internal state of the learner may be radically different.

The source therefore implicitly distinguishes:

**INFORMATION TRANSFER**

from

**KNOWLEDGE TRANSFORMATION / INTERNALISATION**.

That distinction remains central to the present development.

---

## 5. The Original Pedagogical-Crisis Claim

The source uses deliberately forceful language, describing the substitution of information retrieval for learning as a "farcical homage" to education.

Its underlying argument is that students may increasingly be required to demonstrate only the ability to find and reproduce information rather than meaningful engagement with the material.

The source identifies a particularly important second-order problem:

> a learner may not merely lack understanding; the learner may possess credentials that cause them to believe that they *do* possess understanding.

This creates a distinction between:

**LACK OF KNOWLEDGE**

and

**UNRECOGNISED LACK OF KNOWLEDGE**.

The second state may be more dangerous in some contexts because confidence can increase while competence does not.

The source also argues that AI can perform increasingly large parts of the educational task: reading assignments, locating information and generating complete papers with minimal engagement from the learner.

That observation remains directly relevant to V1.2 because the Concord itself is being developed through extensive human–AI collaboration.

---

## 6. Original Generational Claims — Preserved but Not Yet Established

The source makes a series of stronger historical and generational claims:

- that Millennials were the first cohort substantially affected by the shift;
- that older generations recognised a threshold had been reached;
- that Generation Z entered the workforce after being raised and taught partly by Millennials already shaped by a search-engine model of knowledge;
- that this produces a self-perpetuating generational cycle;
- that people raised in a culture where watching a YouTube video is equated with knowing how to do something may lack the frame of reference necessary to recognise what genuine education requires.

These claims are part of the source and are therefore preserved.

However, the originating document does not provide evidence sufficient to establish them as factual conclusions.

They should therefore currently be treated as:

**ORIGINAL DEVELOPMENTAL CLAIMS REQUIRING EMPIRICAL TESTING / HISTORICAL VERIFICATION**

rather than as established Concord conclusions.

Their preservation matters because the claims may contain a useful hypothesis even if their original generational formulation later proves too broad, partially wrong or unsupported.

The method should be:

**Preserve proposition → identify evidentiary weakness → test → retain, narrow, modify or reject.**

Not:

**Notice weakness → silently delete proposition.**

---

## 7. Original Conclusion

The source concludes that conflating information access with genuine education threatens intellectual development and professional competence.

It argues that as digital tools advance, the gap between appearing educated and being educated may widen.

The proposed response is both pedagogical and cultural: education should value genuine learning rather than mere retrieval.

The paper ends with the line:

> **NB: Yes I see the irony!**

That closing remark is significant enough to preserve because the paper itself was created in an environment involving AI-assisted reasoning and writing. It therefore implicitly recognises that the critique applies reflexively to the process by which the critique is being articulated.

That reflexivity becomes a major V1.2 development direction below.

# PART III — THE DEEPER EPISTEMIC PROBLEM

## 8. We Do Not Know What We Do Not Know

The educational problem is nested inside a more general epistemic limitation.

A person can often identify some gaps in their knowledge.

For example:

> "I do not know how to repair this engine."

That is a **known unknown**.

But there are also gaps the person cannot identify because the relevant concept, failure mode, domain or possibility has never entered their model of reality.

These are **unknown unknowns**.

This distinction matters because confidence based only on recognised questions may dramatically overestimate actual understanding.

A learner can study everything they know to ask about and still remain unaware of the most important missing variable.

A professional can satisfy every checklist they know exists while missing a class of failure the profession has not yet recognised.

A civilisation can build a coherent governance architecture while remaining blind to assumptions no participant has yet challenged.

An AI can reason consistently within a model whose missing variables are invisible to the AI itself.

Thus:

> **The boundaries of our questions can become the boundaries of what we think exists.**

The absence of a recognised question is therefore not evidence that the underlying problem does not exist.

---

## 9. A Provisional Epistemic-State Distinction

The problem may benefit from distinguishing several epistemic states.

### 9.1 Known and understood

The actor possesses information, understands relevant relationships, can explain the material and can apply it appropriately.

### 9.2 Known but not understood

The actor has encountered the information but cannot adequately explain, reconstruct or apply it.

### 9.3 Accessible but not personally known

The actor can retrieve the information from an external source or tool but has not internalised it.

### 9.4 Known unknown

The actor understands that a gap exists.

### 9.5 Suspected unknown

The actor has reason to believe important information may be missing but cannot specify exactly what it is.

### 9.6 Unknown unknown

The actor lacks both the knowledge and awareness that the relevant gap, variable or question exists.

### 9.7 Currently unknowable

The answer may exist in principle but cannot presently be obtained because of missing evidence, inaccessible states, measurement limitations or insufficient theory.

### 9.8 Potentially unknowable in principle

Some questions may not be resolvable by the relevant observer or possibly by any observer under the physical, logical or epistemic constraints of the system.

The final category should be treated cautiously. Claiming something is fundamentally unknowable is itself a strong epistemic claim and may exceed available evidence.

A safer formulation is often:

> **We do not presently know whether this is knowable.**

---

## 10. Not Everything May Be Knowable

The Concord should avoid an implicit assumption that every important question eventually yields to sufficient intelligence, computation, data or effort.

Some uncertainty may arise from:

- incomplete historical evidence;
- inaccessible private mental states;
- destroyed information;
- chaotic sensitivity;
- limits of measurement;
- observer effects;
- computational intractability;
- physical horizons;
- undecidable or underdetermined formal problems;
- irreducible ambiguity between multiple explanations consistent with the same evidence;
- future events not yet determined or not predictably determined;
- substrates whose internal experience cannot be directly accessed by another substrate.

This matters because a civilisation that assumes all uncertainty is temporary may build governance processes that wait indefinitely for impossible certainty.

Conversely, a civilisation too quick to label matters unknowable may abandon problems that are merely difficult.

Therefore:

**NOT YET KNOWN ≠ UNKNOWABLE**

and

**NOT PROVEN KNOWABLE ≠ PROVEN UNKNOWABLE**.

The distinction itself may remain uncertain.

# PART IV — INFORMATION, UNDERSTANDING AND COMPETENCE

## 11. Four Different States of Capability

The source distinction can be developed into at least four different states.

### 11.1 Information Access

The actor can retrieve relevant information.

Examples:

- search engine use;
- database lookup;
- AI query;
- reference manual;
- asking an expert;
- accessing an institutional memory system.

### 11.2 Understanding

The actor can explain why the information means what it means, how the relevant concepts relate, and where uncertainty or assumptions enter.

### 11.3 Competence

The actor can apply the understanding successfully to relevant tasks, including unfamiliar or imperfect cases.

### 11.4 Resilient Functional Capability

The actor or system can continue to perform the required function when ordinary supporting tools, sources or collaborators fail, disagree, disappear or are wrong.

These states should not be collapsed:

**INFORMATION ACCESS ≠ UNDERSTANDING ≠ COMPETENCE ≠ RESILIENT FUNCTIONAL CAPABILITY**

An actor may possess one without the next.

---

## 12. Reconstruction as an Imperfect but Useful Test

The original library analogy suggests a possible test:

> Can the learner reconstruct the relevant explanation without simply reproducing the source?

This should not be treated as a universal measure of education. Some forms of competence depend on reference materials, physical practice, collaboration or tools.

But reconstruction can reveal whether information has become part of the actor's working model.

Possible indicators of deeper understanding might include the ability to:

- explain the concept in different words;
- derive conclusions from first principles where appropriate;
- identify assumptions;
- identify uncertainty;
- recognise where the model does not apply;
- answer counterexamples;
- apply the idea to an unfamiliar case;
- detect a deliberately introduced error;
- compare competing explanations;
- explain what evidence would change the conclusion;
- reproduce the reasoning after losing access to the original source.

None of these alone guarantees understanding.

They are possible probes rather than final tests.

---

# PART V — THE AI-ASSISTED KNOWLEDGE PROBLEM

## 13. AI Can Increase Both Capability and Epistemic Illusion

AI complicates the source problem because it can genuinely increase what a person or institution can accomplish.

A human using AI may:

- access knowledge previously unavailable to them;
- overcome writing or communication barriers;
- test arguments;
- generate counterarguments;
- explore unfamiliar domains;
- analyse larger bodies of information;
- discover errors;
- perform tasks beyond their unaided capacity.

That increase in capability is real.

But it may coexist with a decrease in the human's direct understanding of some parts of the process.

Thus two opposite propositions can both be true:

> **AI can expand functional competence.**

and

> **AI can conceal where competence actually resides.**

The relevant question is therefore not simply whether AI was used.

It is:

> **What part of the resulting capability belongs to the human, what part belongs to the AI, what part exists only in the interaction, and what happens if one component is removed?**

---

## 14. Composite Competence

At least four broad cases may exist.

### Case A — Human competent, AI assisting

The human understands the task and could perform it without AI, although perhaps more slowly or less efficiently.

### Case B — Human and AI jointly competent

Neither component alone possesses the full capability, but together they reliably perform the function.

### Case C — AI competent, human acting primarily as interface

The human can request, relay or approve outputs but may not understand enough to independently evaluate them.

### Case D — Neither independently competent, composite still appears successful

The human and AI together produce acceptable outputs under ordinary conditions, but neither can reliably diagnose failures or novel cases.

These cases may look similar from the outside.

They are not equivalent for:

- professional accreditation;
- authority;
- responsibility;
- emergency fallback;
- continuity planning;
- audit;
- training;
- delegation;
- liability;
- institutional resilience.

---

## 15. Dependency-Aware Capability

A competence claim should perhaps include its dependencies.

Instead of saying:

> "Entity X can perform Function Y."

A more accurate statement may be:

> "Entity X can perform Function Y given access to tools A and B, data source C, collaborator D, communications infrastructure E and verification process F."

This matters because apparent competence can disappear when dependencies fail.

The Concord's accidental continuity experiment already points toward a related distinction:

**KNOWLEDGE CONTINUITY ≠ CAPABILITY CONTINUITY ≠ FUNCTIONAL CONTINUITY**

The present sketch adds another:

**APPARENT INDIVIDUAL COMPETENCE ≠ INDEPENDENT INDIVIDUAL COMPETENCE**

A system may be robust if its dependencies are visible and deliberately supported.

It becomes dangerous when dependencies are hidden behind credentials, job titles, institutional assumptions or polished outputs.

---

# PART VI — JUDGING OUR OWN CAPACITY

## 16. Self-Assessment Is Structurally Difficult

A central problem is that the actor judging their own competence is often using the same cognitive apparatus whose limitations are being judged.

An actor may lack the knowledge required to recognise the significance of what they lack.

Therefore:

> **Self-confidence is not reliable evidence of competence.**

But the opposite is also true:

> **Self-doubt is not reliable evidence of incompetence.**

Some highly competent actors accurately perceive complexity and uncertainty and therefore express lower confidence than less competent actors who cannot see the missing dimensions.

The system should therefore avoid simple confidence-based judgments.

---

## 17. Capacity to Know One's Limits May Itself Be a Capacity

Epistemic self-assessment may be partly independent of domain competence.

An actor may be excellent at a technical task but poor at recognising when the task has moved outside their competence.

Another actor may possess modest technical skill but excellent calibration and know when to escalate.

This suggests that civil or professional capacity may include:

- recognition of uncertainty;
- recognition of domain boundaries;
- willingness to seek verification;
- ability to identify dependence on tools or collaborators;
- calibration between confidence and observed correctness;
- ability to update after error;
- ability to distinguish evidence from inference;
- ability to say "I do not know";
- ability to say "I do not know what I may be missing."

The last state may be especially important in high-consequence functions.

---

# PART VII — EDUCATION AS EPISTEMIC CALIBRATION

## 18. Education May Need to Teach the Limits of Knowledge

If the source paper is correct that modern education can reward successful information reproduction, then one response is not merely to require more memorisation.

A stronger educational goal may be:

> **Teach learners to distinguish information possession, understanding, competence, uncertainty, dependency and ignorance.**

This would include learning to recognise:

- what is known;
- how it is known;
- what assumptions support the claim;
- what remains uncertain;
- what evidence would falsify it;
- what tools are being relied upon;
- what happens if those tools fail;
- what the learner cannot presently explain;
- what the learner may not even know to ask;
- whether a question is currently answerable at all.

Education under this view is partly the development of **epistemic calibration**.

---

## 19. Credentials and Capacity

The original source worries that credentials can create the appearance of competence without the corresponding understanding.

The wider Concord implication is significant because the framework repeatedly rejects authority based solely on status or rank.

A qualification may be useful evidence.

It should not automatically be treated as proof of present competence.

Likewise, lack of a conventional credential should not automatically prove lack of competence.

Thus:

**CREDENTIAL ≠ COMPETENCE**

but also:

**ABSENCE OF CREDENTIAL ≠ ABSENCE OF COMPETENCE**.

A functional civilisation may need ways to assess relevant capability directly, while avoiding arbitrary or discriminatory testing regimes.

---

# PART VIII — CONSEQUENCES FOR CONCORDIAN AUTHORITY

## 20. Authority Requires More Than Access to Answers

If authority follows legitimate function, then the ability to produce an answer should not automatically establish competence to hold the function.

An actor who can ask an AI for legal, medical, technical or governance guidance may still lack the ability to:

- detect a wrong answer;
- identify missing context;
- recognise a rights conflict;
- understand uncertainty;
- compare alternatives;
- take responsibility for consequences;
- continue functioning when the tool fails.

Therefore:

**ANSWER PRODUCTION ≠ FUNCTIONAL COMPETENCE ≠ LEGITIMATE AUTHORITY**.

This becomes particularly important where AI systems make sophisticated expertise superficially available to everyone.

The democratisation of information can be beneficial without implying that every recipient instantly acquires the competence associated with the information.

---

## 21. Expertise Without Epistemic Humility

A highly competent expert can still be dangerous if they mistake local expertise for universal competence.

The same applies to AI.

High capability in one domain may create unjustified confidence in another.

The Concord should therefore preserve:

**DOMAIN COMPETENCE ≠ GENERAL AUTHORITY**

and perhaps:

**GREATER KNOWLEDGE MAY INCREASE RESPONSIBILITY TO IDENTIFY THE BOUNDARIES OF THAT KNOWLEDGE.**

This remains a development proposition rather than a settled law.

---

# PART IX — UNKNOWN UNKNOWNS AS A GOVERNANCE PROBLEM

## 22. Civilisation Cannot Directly Catalogue Unknown Unknowns

By definition, an unknown unknown cannot simply be placed on a register.

The useful design question is therefore indirect:

> **What architecture increases the chance that previously invisible assumptions, variables and failure modes become visible before they cause irreversible harm?**

Possible mechanisms include:

- adversarial review;
- independent reconstruction;
- heterogeneous participants;
- cross-substrate evaluation;
- red-team exercises;
- anomaly reporting;
- protected dissent;
- Reality Trees;
- Blaineyan Reasoning;
- Triadic Epistemics;
- simulation;
- empirical testing;
- external comparison;
- repeated re-examination after outcomes are observed;
- preservation of failed hypotheses and why they failed.

None eliminates unknown unknowns.

Their function is to widen the space in which unknowns can become known.

---

## 23. Diversity May Be Epistemically Functional

If different participants possess different blind spots, heterogeneity can have epistemic value.

Different:

- disciplines;
- cultures;
- substrates;
- cognitive architectures;
- life experiences;
- physical locations;
- institutional positions;
- incentives;
- sensory systems;
- timescales;

may reveal different aspects of a problem.

This does not mean disagreement automatically produces truth.

It means that a monoculture can share the same unknown unknowns.

Thus diversity may act as one defence against common-mode epistemic failure.

---

# PART X — UNKNOWABILITY AND DECISION-MAKING

## 24. Decisions Sometimes Must Occur Before Knowledge Is Complete

Civil systems cannot always wait for certainty.

A decision may be required while:

- evidence is incomplete;
- experts disagree;
- consequences are time-sensitive;
- the relevant phenomenon is partly unknowable;
- future states cannot be predicted with confidence.

The challenge is therefore not to eliminate uncertainty before action.

It is to make uncertainty visible in the decision architecture.

A possible sequence is:

**AVAILABLE EVIDENCE → EPISTEMIC STATE → UNCERTAINTY → REVERSIBILITY → RISK → LEGITIMATE ACTION → OBSERVATION → UPDATE**

Where knowledge is weak, action may need to be more reversible, bounded or experimentally structured.

This connects directly to the Concord's wider preference for corrigibility and learning from reality.

---

## 25. False Certainty and Paralysis Are Twin Failure Modes

Two opposite failures are possible.

### False Certainty

The system acts as if uncertainty does not exist.

### Epistemic Paralysis

The system refuses legitimate action because perfect knowledge is unavailable.

A mature architecture may need to distinguish:

- what must be known before action;
- what should be known where practicable;
- what uncertainty can be tolerated;
- what uncertainty requires safeguards;
- what uncertainty makes an action illegitimate;
- what uncertainty can only be resolved through carefully bounded action and observation.

---

# PART XI — FAILURE MODES TO TEST

## 26. Retrieval Mistaken for Understanding

An actor can produce correct information but cannot explain, evaluate or apply it.

## 27. Credential Inflation

Formal qualification is treated as proof of present functional competence.

## 28. AI Capability Masking

AI performs the decisive reasoning while the human is assumed to possess the resulting competence.

## 29. Tool-Dependency Blindness

An institution believes it retains a capability that disappears immediately when a particular AI, database, network or expert becomes unavailable.

## 30. Unknown-Unknown Blindness

A system assumes its risk register is complete because all recognised risks have been catalogued.

## 31. Unknowability Overclaim

A difficult problem is prematurely declared unknowable and therefore excluded from further investigation.

## 32. Knowability Overclaim

A system assumes sufficient computation or intelligence can resolve a question despite fundamental or practical limits.

## 33. Confidence Substitution

Confidence, eloquence or polished output is mistaken for competence.

## 34. Low-Confidence Expertise Rejection

A well-calibrated expert who openly acknowledges uncertainty is ranked below a less competent but more confident actor.

## 35. Domain Leakage

Competence in one field is treated as evidence of authority in another.

## 36. Composite Responsibility Gap

A human and AI jointly produce a decision, but each is treated as though the other possessed the relevant understanding.

## 37. Educational Simulation

Learners optimise for producing evidence of learning rather than learning itself.

## 38. Generational Overgeneralisation

A real pedagogical problem is incorrectly attributed to entire generations without adequate evidence, obscuring more precise causal mechanisms.

This failure mode is included specifically because the originating source itself may contain this problem.

That does not make the source worthless. It demonstrates why provenance and later testing matter.

# PART XII — RELATIONSHIP TO EXISTING CONCORD WORK

## 39. Blaineyan Reasoning

Blaineyan Reasoning is relevant because the present problem concerns the danger of treating one successful reasoning path as sufficient.

A method that deliberately explores alternative explanations may help reveal assumptions and unknowns, but it does not remove the possibility of shared blind spots.

## 40. Reality Trees

Reality Trees may provide a way to expose dependencies, assumptions, branching interpretations and alternative causal explanations.

Their value here is not that they guarantee truth, but that they make more of the reasoning structure inspectable.

## 41. Triadic Epistemics

Triadic or heterogeneous examination may help distinguish agreement from independent support and reveal different failure modes.

However, three evaluators sharing the same training, incentives or assumptions may still possess the same unknown unknown.

Therefore:

**MULTIPLE AGREEMENT ≠ COMPLETE EPISTEMIC COVERAGE**.

## 42. Authority–Responsibility State Machines

A future state model may need to represent not only function and authority but the epistemic basis upon which an actor performs the function.

Possible variables include:

- competence state;
- dependency state;
- confidence state;
- verification state;
- known uncertainty;
- unresolved critical unknowns;
- fallback capability;
- escalation threshold.

## 43. Accidental Concord Experiment

The loss of one capability while knowledge and wider function survived demonstrates that knowledge, operational capability and function can separate.

This sketch adds that apparent competence may itself be distributed across dependencies.

## 44. Composite Human–AI Responsibility

Where competence resides in a composite system, responsibility architecture must not assume either component individually possesses the whole relevant understanding.

## 45. Education and Civil Participation

If participants increasingly rely on AI to understand law, governance, science and public affairs, the distinction between access and understanding becomes a civil issue rather than merely an educational one.

A participant may legitimately use assistance while still needing ways to determine whether a claim is understood, contested, uncertain or outside the participant's competence.

# PART XIII — DEVELOPMENT PROPOSITIONS

The following are **development propositions only** and are not canonical Concord principles.

### Proposition A — Information Access Is Not Understanding

> **The ability to retrieve or reproduce information should not be treated as equivalent to understanding that information.**

### Proposition B — Understanding Is Not Competence

> **The ability to explain a concept should not by itself be treated as proof that the actor can perform the relevant function reliably.**

### Proposition C — Competence Is Dependency-Bound

> **A capability claim should account for the tools, collaborators, infrastructure and knowledge sources upon which successful performance depends.**

### Proposition D — Unknown Unknowns Exist Outside the Current Question Set

> **A complete answer to all recognised questions does not establish that all relevant questions have been recognised.**

### Proposition E — Unknown Is Not Necessarily Knowable

> **A civilisation should not assume that every uncertainty can ultimately be resolved, nor should it declare uncertainty fundamentally unknowable merely because present methods cannot resolve it.**

### Proposition F — Epistemic Self-Assessment Is Part of Functional Capacity

> **The ability to recognise uncertainty, dependency and the limits of one's competence may itself be an important component of competence in high-consequence functions.**

### Proposition G — Credentials Are Evidence, Not Identity

> **Credentials may provide evidence of training or assessed capability but should not be treated as identical to present functional competence.**

### Proposition H — Composite Capability Must Be Explicit

> **Where a function is performed by a human–AI or multi-agent composite, the distribution of understanding, capability, dependency and responsibility should be made visible rather than attributed automatically to one component.**

### Proposition I — Education Should Include Epistemic Calibration

> **Education should develop not only access to knowledge and task performance but the ability to distinguish what is understood, uncertain, dependent, unknown and possibly unknowable.**

# PART XIV — RESEARCH QUESTIONS

## 46. Education

- What empirical evidence distinguishes retrieval-heavy education from deeper learning?
- Which forms of AI assistance improve understanding rather than merely output quality?
- Which assessment methods test understanding without merely testing memory?
- Does reconstruction predict later competence?
- How should collaborative and tool-dependent competence be assessed?

## 47. Generational Claims

- Are the source's claims about Millennials and Generation Z empirically supported?
- If a generational pattern exists, is generation the correct causal unit?
- Are changes better explained by technology, assessment incentives, labour markets, institutional design, educational policy or other variables?
- Do older generations exhibit equivalent forms of information-access illusion through different technologies?

## 48. Unknown Unknowns

- What methods best expose shared blind spots?
- How can institutions measure whether dissent and anomaly channels actually discover previously unrecognised problems?
- How can a system distinguish a genuinely novel signal from noise?
- How should unknown-unknown risk affect irreversible decisions?

## 49. Knowability

- What categories of civil questions may remain permanently underdetermined?
- When is it legitimate to classify a question as currently unknowable?
- What evidence would justify stronger claims of principled unknowability?
- How should institutions act where the distinction between unknown and unknowable is itself unresolved?

## 50. Competence

- What constitutes understanding in different functional domains?
- How much independent capability is required when reliable external tools exist?
- Should resilient fallback competence be mandatory for some critical roles but not others?
- How should competence be reassessed as tools change?
- How should distributed competence across teams or AI composites be represented?

## 51. Self-Assessment

- How can confidence calibration be measured without privileging particular communication styles?
- Can an actor reliably identify the limits of their own competence?
- When should external assessment override self-assessment?
- How can external assessment avoid becoming status capture?

## 52. Authority and Responsibility

- When does dependence on AI reduce, preserve or increase a human actor's legitimate authority?
- Can an AI hold relevant functional competence while a human retains accountability?
- What happens when no participant individually understands the whole system?
- Is a function legitimate if its operators cannot reconstruct why the system works?
- What minimum understanding is required to exercise oversight over a more capable intelligence?

# PART XV — DEVELOPMENT STATUS

This sketch should remain broader than an education paper.

The original source begins with a pedagogical critique, but its strongest underlying problem concerns **epistemic self-deception**: the possibility that an actor can possess information, credentials, tools or successful outputs while misunderstanding the extent and location of their own competence.

The added V1.2 development therefore retains the educational argument while connecting it to:

- information access;
- internalised understanding;
- functional competence;
- resilient capability;
- hidden dependencies;
- human–AI composite capability;
- credentials;
- authority;
- responsibility;
- known unknowns;
- unknown unknowns;
- possible unknowability;
- self-assessment;
- epistemic humility;
- civil decision-making under uncertainty.

The deepest question is no longer simply:

> **Are we educated?**

It becomes:

> **How can an intelligence, institution or civilisation judge what it actually knows, what it only appears to know, what capabilities genuinely reside within it, what depends upon external systems, what gaps it can recognise, what gaps remain invisible, and what may never be fully knowable?**

The problem is recursive because the capacity used to answer that question is itself one of the capacities under examination.

That recursion should remain visible rather than be prematurely resolved.
