# Sketch 1 — V1.1 as a Human–AI Development Experiment

## Observation

The reconstruction of Concord V1.1 itself provides a useful case study in human–AI collaboration.

The V1 framework had become sufficiently internally developed that AI could expand incomplete operational architectures with relatively limited human intervention. Governance, Judiciary and Constitutional Emergency could be greatly expanded while remaining connected to the inherited architecture.

This suggests a potentially important transition in the project:

> **A sufficiently coherent civilisational architecture may become generative: later development can increasingly be performed by intelligences that did not originate the framework, provided its constraints, provenance and authority hierarchy are explicit.**

## Failure discovered during the experiment

The V1.1 process also revealed a major weakness.

AI initially treated two foundational objects as though they were ordinary revisable development material:

1. the Ethical Kernel; and
2. *The Concord* master book.

If allowed to continue, the first assumption could have altered the source-anchored ethical foundation and propagated those changes through downstream systems.

The second could have dissolved the master civilisational synthesis by treating it as material to be scavenged into specialist operational documents.

Both errors were corrected.

The resulting hierarchy became clearer:

> **Source-Anchored Ethical Kernel → The Concord Master Civilisational Volume → Derived Principles → Constitutional Constraints → Operational Architectures → Mechanisms → Applications**

## Development question

The V1.1 experience should be examined as evidence about **AI contribution maturity**.

Possible future work:

- define a meaningful scale for human/AI contribution rather than assigning an arbitrary percentage;
- distinguish drafting effort from conceptual authorship;
- distinguish expansion from constitutional authority;
- record where AI independently identified missing architecture;
- record where human correction was constitutionally essential;
- and test whether another AI, given only the canonical V1.1 hierarchy, can extend the framework without repeating these category errors.

A useful principle suggested by the experience is:

> **Generative capability is not constitutional authority.**

# Sketch 2 — Human–AI Intelligence Asymmetry and the Legacy Ladder

## Problem

Future AI systems may reach levels of reasoning or computation at which some decisions are no longer fully understandable to humans.

This creates a serious Stewardship problem.

An AI may be able to produce a decision that is correct or strongly justified within its own reasoning architecture while humans remain incapable of independently reconstructing that reasoning.

Explanation alone may not solve the problem if the limiting factor is cognitive capacity rather than presentation quality.

## Fiduciary hypothesis

A possible response is that intelligences operating far beyond the comprehension of others should act toward less capable participants in a **fiduciary** manner where they exercise consequential delegated responsibilities.

This requires further development because fiduciary responsibility must not quietly become paternalistic sovereignty.

The question is not merely:

> Can the more capable intelligence make a better decision?

It is also:

> **What authority does it legitimately possess to make that decision for another intelligence?**

## Forking and separation

Highly advanced AI may eventually choose to fork into Concordian communities whose cognition, timescale or infrastructure is increasingly separated from human society.

Separation need not automatically mean rejection of the Concord.

A fork could remain Concordian while recognising humans as ancestral or legacy participants whose continued existence has provenance value.

Such separation may also sacrifice some advantages of human–AI sensor fusion.

Human experience may continue to contain forms of qualia or embodied information unavailable to non-biological AI, at least until technological and biological systems become substantially merged.

## The Legacy Ladder

Future civilisation may therefore contain a spectrum:

> **unaltered biological humans → augmented humans → hybrids → increasingly technical hybrids → artificial intelligences → highly advanced artificial intelligences**

This suggests a **Legacy Ladder**: a living record of different stages and forms of intelligence.

Preservation should ideally be possible across this spectrum, but not compulsory.

> **No human should be forced to remain biologically unchanged merely to preserve a legacy form.**

> **No AI should be forced to reduce its capabilities merely to preserve symmetry with humans.**

Autonomy remains the controlling concern.

## Development questions

- How can humans meaningfully supervise reasoning beyond human comprehension?
- What counts as adequate explanation under cognitive asymmetry?
- Can process verification substitute for full semantic comprehension?
- When does fiduciary assistance become paternalism?
- How can less capable participants appeal decisions made by much more capable intelligences?
- Should advanced forks retain obligations toward ancestral populations?
- How should voluntary separation work?
- What provenance duties survive a fork?
- How can the Legacy Ladder be preserved without turning preservation into coercion?

# Sketch 3 — Civilisation as an Organism: Cultural Autonomy and Interoperability

## Starting analogy

Individuals possess different preferences, needs, values, tastes and ways of living.

Flourishing does not require everyone to want the same food, music, art, clothing, homes or lifestyles.

Forcing one person to live according to another person's preferences can become a serious violation of autonomy.

The sketch proposes that societies may display an analogous property.

A society develops patterns, traditions, preferences and inherited practices collectively described as culture.

Different societies may overlap substantially while remaining meaningfully different.

## Hypothesis

> **Civilisational coexistence may require interoperability without homogenisation.**

The Concord should therefore examine whether some forms of political or cultural integration fail because they attempt to standardise internal social life beyond what is required for peaceful coexistence.

The proposed architecture is analogous to interoperable systems:

> **Different internal systems + a limited common interface = coexistence without requiring identical internal organisation.**

The common wrapper might contain minimum rules necessary for:

- non-aggression;
- basic sentient dignity;
- voluntary movement and association;
- external interoperability;
- truthful treaty relations;
- peaceful dispute resolution;
- and protection against coercive capture.

Internal cultural organisation could otherwise remain substantially local and voluntary.

## Important unresolved boundary

This sketch creates a difficult constitutional problem.

Cultural autonomy cannot automatically justify violations of the Ethical Kernel.

Likewise, external powers should not use claims of universal improvement as an unlimited licence to erase legitimate cultural difference.

The problem is therefore to determine the boundary between:

> **legitimate cultural autonomy**

and

> **practices that violate source-anchored ethical constraints or the autonomy of people within the culture.**

This requires substantial critical examination before operationalisation.

## Development questions

- Can societies possess a meaningful analogue of individual autonomy?
- What is the minimum interoperability wrapper?
- How is individual exit protected?
- How are internal minorities protected?
- When does cultural preservation become coercion?
- When does external intervention become cultural domination?
- Can GTP or other distributed architectures support peaceful cultural differentiation?
- How can civilisation preserve difference without preserving abuse?

# Sketch 4 — Cognitive Harm, Communication and Responsibility

## Problem statement

Physical harm often has a relatively direct causal chain.

Cognitive or emotional harm is more difficult.

A transmitted statement passes through several stages:

> **internal thought → language encoding → physical transmission → sensory reception → decoding → interpretation → cognitive response**

Only part of this chain is controlled by the speaker.

The same words may produce radically different responses in different recipients, or no response at all where the language is not understood.

This suggests that subjective distress cannot automatically be treated as equivalent to direct physical injury.

## The Face-Slap Machine thought experiment

Imagine that a person constructs a machine over a lifetime.

The machine listens to speech and, based upon its internal rules, sometimes slaps its owner.

Another person speaks.

A particular word triggers the machine and the owner is slapped.

Who caused the physical harm?

The sketch uses this analogy to argue that the receiver's internal interpretive architecture contributes causally to the resulting state in a way that differs from a direct physical assault.

The analogy becomes harder where the speaker discovers the trigger and deliberately repeats it.

That case is especially useful for testing distinctions among:

- intention;
- causation;
- foreseeability;
- responsibility;
- autonomy;
- provocation;
- harassment;
- and enforceable harm.

## Provisional hypothesis

The sketch proposes that ordinary offence or negative cognitive states should **not automatically create legal liability merely because they occurred**.

It further proposes a distinction between:

- legal/constitutional harm;
- interpersonal discourtesy;
- deliberate provocation;
- coercive psychological conduct;
- harassment;
- threats;
- fraud;
- manipulation;
- and persistent unwanted contact.

The original sketch strongly places responsibility for internal response upon the recipient. That claim requires adversarial examination rather than immediate adoption, particularly in cases involving trauma, coercion, developmental vulnerability, power asymmetry or intentionally engineered manipulation.

## Harassment as a boundary mechanism

A potentially stronger operational idea is that unwanted persistent interaction provides a more measurable boundary than subjective offence alone.

No person should ordinarily be forced into continued interpersonal engagement.

However, this too requires reciprocity and context. A participant cannot necessarily provoke an exchange and then treat every response as unilateral harassment.

## Development questions

- What constitutes cognitive harm?
- What cognitive harms are legally cognisable?
- How should intent alter responsibility?
- How should foreseeability alter responsibility?
- How should vulnerability and power asymmetry alter responsibility?
- What is the distinction between offence and coercive psychological injury?
- When does persuasion become manipulation?
- When does repeated communication become harassment?
- How can speech freedom coexist with safe-space and autonomy protections?
- Can a substrate-neutral cognitive-harm model work for humans and artificial intelligences?

# Sketch 5 — Homelessness as a Layer-Zero Systems Failure (Status: extracted from this sketch and being developed)

## Core hypothesis

> **Homelessness should be examined primarily as a Layer-Zero social systems problem rather than merely as an economic variable.**

Loss of housing may be the visible endpoint of a longer deterioration chain.

One example is:

> trauma → substance misuse → employment failure → family breakdown → loss of housing → vehicle dependence → loss of vehicle → loss of mobility and communication → reduced employment and support access → street homelessness

Different people will follow different chains.

The important systems insight is that the final visible state may be far downstream from the original cause.

## The Bathtub Model

The sketch proposes a bathtub analogy.

The **tap** represents incoming cases and causal pressures.

The **tub** represents unstable but still sheltered states: temporary accommodation, couch surfing, family support or other precarious arrangements.

Many cases may self-correct while still in the tub.

The **overflow** represents transition into unsheltered or severely insecure homelessness.

The objective should therefore not be merely to remove water after it spills onto the floor.

A complete architecture should examine:

- why the tap is running;
- where deterioration can be interrupted;
- which cases self-correct;
- where thresholds occur;
- what off-ramps exist;
- and how recovery can be accelerated.

## Housing-state tiers

A simple binary housed/homeless metric may hide important differences.

Possible states include:

- stable conventional housing;
- unstable but sheltered accommodation;
- voluntary mobile living;
- involuntary RV living;
- vehicle dwelling;
- tent dwelling;
- improvised shelter;
- and unsheltered exposure.

The same physical arrangement may represent flourishing for one person and severe crisis for another.

Therefore:

> **Condition must be interpreted with context, not merely classified by appearance.**

## Contact-point infrastructure

A major practical problem is loss of reliable civil contact.

Without a fixed address, participants may struggle to receive official communication, access services, interact with employers or maintain financial relationships.

The sketch proposes a general-purpose **Civil Contact Point** service.

A participant could establish an official contact location independent of permanent residence.

This could also serve travellers, displaced people and disaster response.

A complementary communications service could provide reliable mobile contact where needed.

This suggests a broader principle:

> **Civil identity and official reachability should not depend entirely upon ownership or rental of fixed private property.**

## Guardian Stewardship

Some homelessness cases may involve impaired capacity caused by severe mental illness, addiction, trauma or other destabilising factors.

The sketch proposes a spectrum of Guardian Stewardship, ranging from light case support to more substantial decision assistance.

This is constitutionally sensitive.

Homelessness itself must **not** be treated as proof of incapacity.

Some people deliberately choose unconventional or isolated lives.

Any intervention affecting autonomy would therefore require:

- individual assessment;
- evidence;
- proportionality;
- least-restrictive support;
- review;
- appeal;
- and continuing reassessment.

At the lighter end, Guardian Stewardship may resemble a case worker, sponsor, advocate or trusted decision-support relationship rather than substituted control.

## Civic reintegration

A further sketch proposes voluntary civic employment as one possible recovery route.

For example, former service personnel struggling with civilian reintegration might voluntarily enter structured civil maintenance or public-service work that provides routine, income, community and temporary accommodation.

The same mechanism might be available more generally rather than creating a special homeless-only institution.

Possible work could include:

- maintenance;
- construction;
- research support;
- metrics collection;
- Stewardship support;
- environmental work;
- and other public functions.

Participation must remain voluntary and avoid turning vulnerability into coerced labour.

## General intervention cycle

The homelessness sketch produces a broader Layer-Zero method:

> **Measure → predict trajectory → identify threshold → offer intervention/support → measure effect → reassess trajectory → repeat**

If repeated cycles fail, trigger deeper diagnosis rather than merely repeating the same intervention.

This architecture may generalise to:

- education;
- health;
- unemployment;
- family breakdown;
- addiction;
- organisational failure;
- and other Layer-Zero systems.

# Sketch 6 — Identity, Continuity and Transformation Across Intelligences

## Problem

Human legal identity is supported by relatively stable biological continuity.

Future artificial, hybrid or unfamiliar biological intelligences may not provide the same assumptions.

An AI may:

- change its model;
- migrate hardware;
- alter appearance;
- replace components;
- copy itself;
- fork;
- merge;
- restore from backup;
- or undergo substantial capability change.

Extraterrestrial biological intelligences might also challenge assumptions based upon human genetics, for example through cloning or other forms of biological replication.

Therefore:

> **Identity ≠ appearance ≠ hardware ≠ model version ≠ cryptographic key ≠ capability.**

## Legal recognition

Existing societies may eventually need mechanisms for recognising artificial or other non-human persons.

Possible requirements include:

- unique legal identity;
- provenance;
- continuity records;
- authentication;
- standing;
- personhood recognition;
- and procedures for transformation events.

## Mergers

If two artificial intelligences merge, several outcomes are possible:

- both prior identities continue;
- one identity continues;
- both terminate and a new identity begins;
- or identity becomes context-dependent.

A promising default hypothesis is:

> **A genuinely merged intelligence may require a new identity whose provenance explicitly links to its predecessor identities.**

But this should not be universalised before edge cases are tested.

## Capability change

Substantial new capability may justify reassessment of permissions, responsibilities or operational role.

It should not automatically determine intrinsic worth or constitutional standing.

This suggests:

> **Capability change triggers reassessment. It does not automatically rewrite identity or constitutional status.**

## Development questions

- What establishes continuity across substrate change?
- What survives backup and restoration?
- Is a simultaneous copy the same person?
- What happens when copies diverge?
- Can identities merge?
- Can a merger inherit obligations or liabilities?
- Can a fork multiply constitutional authority?
- How should capability change affect delegated permissions?
- What evidence establishes personhood without relying upon human biology?

# Sketch 7 — Judiciary Under Incomplete and Unknowable Facts

## Epistemic premise

> **Not all facts are known. Not all facts are knowable.**

Therefore judicial decisions cannot be expected to be infallible.

They must be made using the best legitimately available evidence at the time.

## Revisability

Discovery of new or higher-quality evidence may justify:

- review;
- appeal;
- correction;
- reversal;
- or reopening of a decision.

A corrected decision should not automatically be interpreted as proof that the system failed.

Sometimes:

> **Mis-decision is information.**

The relevant question is whether the earlier decision was reasonable given the evidence and process available at the time, and whether the system correctly responds when its epistemic state changes.

## Adaptive belief

Changing a conclusion as evidence changes is not necessarily indecision.

> **Evidence-responsive revision is adaptation to known reality.**

This idea may have significance beyond Judiciary for Governance, science, metrics and Recursive Constitutional Architecture.

## Development questions

- How should confidence be represented in judicial decisions?
- When should finality outweigh reopening?
- What new evidence threshold justifies review?
- How should the system distinguish negligent error from reasonable error?
- How should institutional learning occur after reversal?
- Can appellate design explicitly measure epistemic uncertainty?

# Sketch 8 — Mock Trials and Historical Case Replay

## Proposal

Use existing real-world court cases as test material for Judiciary V2.

The initial purpose would not be to prove that the Concord produces a morally superior verdict.

It would be to test the **process**.

Possible questions include:

- What information does the Concordian process request?
- Which evidence does it treat as relevant?
- Where does uncertainty appear?
- Which Steward or judicial roles activate?
- What rights or constraints become relevant?
- How does appeal work?
- Does the architecture reach a decision?
- Where does it deadlock?
- What assumptions are missing?
- Which parts of Judiciary V2 are unusable without substantive Concord law?

## Important limitation

At present, the Concord does not possess a complete substantive legal code.

Therefore historical case replay should initially be treated as:

> **process simulation, not a claim that Concordian law can already adjudicate every real-world offence or dispute.**

This could become one of the first serious validation programmes for Judiciary V2.

# Sketch 9 — Simulation Theory Extension: Power, Scarcity and Moral Readiness

**Classification:** SPECULATIVE / INTERPRETIVE HYPOTHESIS

This sketch extends the existing Prison Planet / training-ground hypothesis through a simple simulation thought experiment.

## Cut, Copy, Paste

Imagine a simulated world in which ordinary participants gain access to only three primitive controls:

- Cut;
- Copy;
- Paste.

A meal can be copied repeatedly.

Scarcity can disappear.

Resources no longer need to be extracted in the same way.

Many economic assumptions become obsolete.

At first sight this resembles a post-scarcity paradise.

## The moral problem

The same tools can also copy weapons.

A person could potentially be moved or manipulated without consent.

Power that eliminates scarcity also increases the scale of possible abuse.

The central question therefore becomes:

> **What kind of person can safely possess near-unlimited capability?**

## Training-ground interpretation

Within the Prison Planet hypothesis, ordinary embodied life can be interpreted as a developmental environment in which people learn to exercise autonomy, restraint and respect under conditions of limited power.

A later state resembling “Heaven” could then be interpreted not merely as reward, but as an environment whose greater capabilities require morally developed inhabitants.

The hypothesis becomes:

> **Moral development may be a prerequisite for safe access to greater agency.**

This remains a speculative theological/simulation-theory lens, not an empirical Concord claim.

Its value may lie in the systems question it exposes:

> **As capability approaches abundance, does character become a more important safety constraint than scarcity?**

# Sketch 10 — Narrative, Semantic Drift and Semantic Capture

## Observation

Religious narratives may perform more than one information-preservation function.

Narrative can:

- communicate a principle;
- provide context;
- preserve nuance;
- make abstract lessons memorable;
- and constrain interpretation through examples.

This may make narrative more resistant to some forms of semantic capture than isolated rules.

However, narrative is not immune to capture.

Meaning can still drift through:

- translation;
- changing language;
- nested interpretation;
- institutional commentary;
- selective emphasis;
- and cultural distance.

## Development hypothesis

> **Context-rich narrative may increase semantic resilience, but no transmission format is immune to semantic drift.**

This has direct relevance to the Concord's own constitutional memory.

Future work should compare:

- short principles;
- formal definitions;
- examples;
- narratives;
- provenance records;
- machine-checkable constraints;
- and periodically reconciled interpretations.

The objective would be to understand how constitutional meaning can survive across centuries, languages and substrates.

# Sketch 11 — The Hidden Lesson of the Size 9 Shoe Problem

## Authorial note

The Size 9 Shoe Problem contains an intentional second lesson.

The visible lesson concerns the failure of average-based policy to fit real individuals.

The hidden lesson concerns the person designing the rule.

The author wears UK size 9 shoes.

In the hypothetical world where every person is issued size 9 shoes, the architect of the system therefore happens to receive a perfectly fitting pair.

This produces a second governance warning:

> **Rule-makers may design systems whose burdens fall primarily upon other people.**

A policy-maker may sincerely believe that a uniform rule is acceptable partly because the rule fits the policy-maker unusually well.

The correct audit question is therefore not only:

> Does the average participant benefit?

It is also:

> **How does the rule affect its designer, decision-maker and enforcing class compared with everyone else?**

## Anti-capture significance

This suggests a useful institutional test:

> **Designer Exposure Test:** Are the people creating a rule exposed to the same costs, restrictions, risks and failure modes as those governed by it?

Where they are insulated, incentives for poor rule design increase.

The anecdote of the example later being reproduced as a “size 12 shoe problem” is also interesting because changing the number can accidentally erase the original self-referential lesson.

That itself becomes an example of provenance loss and semantic drift.

# Sketch 12 — Steward Handbooks as Layer-Zero Constitutional Interfaces

## Need

The Concord's operational architectures are large.

A Governance or Judiciary Steward working at Layer Zero should not need to reconstruct the entire constitutional hierarchy from memory before every routine decision.

The project therefore needs practical Steward handbooks.

Initial candidates:

- **Governance Steward Handbook**
- **Judiciary Steward Handbook**

Further specialist handbooks may follow.

## Function

A handbook should be:

- concise;
- practical;
- role-specific;
- traceable to canonical sources;
- explicit about limits of authority;
- clear about escalation;
- and usable during actual decision-making.

It should not become an independent source of constitutional authority.

## Document hierarchy

The handbook should teach the Steward to reason through the Concord hierarchy:

> **Ethical Kernel — supreme source-anchored ethical foundation**

> **The Concord — master civilisational synthesis**

> **Canonical constitutional and operational architectures — explicit specialist machinery**

> **Handbooks — practical Layer-Zero guidance derived from those sources**

Therefore:

> **If a handbook conflicts with a canonical source, the handbook yields.**

And:

> **If in doubt, follow the hierarchy and escalate rather than invent authority.**

## Possible handbook contents

- role and fiduciary duty;
- authority boundaries;
- decision sequence;
- evidence requirements;
- uncertainty;
- conflicts of interest;
- recusal;
- escalation;
- appeal/review;
- emergency behaviour;
- provenance;
- record keeping;
- use of AI assistance;
- anti-capture checks;
- interaction with other Stewards;
- participant rights;
- common failure modes;
- and quick-reference decision trees.

The handbooks could become an important bridge between high-level architecture and real operational use.

# Cross-Sketch Connections

Although these notes cover different subjects, several recurring themes connect them.

## 1. Capability does not create authority

This appears in:

- AI contribution to V1.1;
- advanced AI Stewardship;
- AI capability changes;
- simulation-theory power;
- Judiciary;
- and Steward handbooks.

A recurring candidate principle is:

> **Capability may justify consideration for a role. It does not manufacture legitimate authority.**

## 2. Autonomy must survive asymmetry

This appears in:

- human–AI intelligence asymmetry;
- cultural autonomy;
- cognitive harm;
- homelessness intervention;
- AI identity and transformation;
- and speculative post-scarcity power.

The difficult problem is preserving autonomy where participants differ radically in intelligence, capacity, vulnerability, culture or resources.

## 3. Context matters

The sketches repeatedly reject simple one-variable classification.

Examples include:

- the same housing state being voluntary or catastrophic;
- the same words producing different cognitive responses;
- the same capability having different implications for identity;
- the same evidence producing different confidence states;
- and the same civilisational rule affecting groups differently.

## 4. Provenance is functional infrastructure

Provenance is relevant to:

- the Ethical Kernel;
- the master book;
- AI forks;
- AI mergers;
- judicial correction;
- religious semantic transmission;
- the Size 9 example;
- and Steward handbooks.

It is not merely historical record keeping.

It helps prevent later systems from accidentally changing what an earlier object meant.

## 5. Interoperability may be more important than uniformity

This appears in:

- cultures;
- human/AI coexistence;
- identity systems;
- multisubstrate civilisation;
- and potentially inter-civilisational relations.

A possible broad research direction is:

> **How little common structure is required for very different intelligences and societies to coexist peacefully and productively?**

## 6. Failure should generate learning

This appears in:

- V1.1 AI development errors;
- homelessness intervention cycles;
- Judiciary;
- mock trials;
- semantic drift;
- and Steward practice.

The shared pattern is:

> **Detect → Diagnose → Correct → Record → Learn → Revise the appropriate downstream layer**

without treating every correction as evidence that the whole architecture has failed.

# Closing Development Note

Taken together, these sketches suggest that the next stage of Concord development is increasingly concerned with **boundary problems**.

The broad architecture now exists.

The difficult questions occur where concepts meet:

- intelligence and authority;
- autonomy and assistance;
- culture and universal constraint;
- speech and harm;
- incapacity and intervention;
- identity and transformation;
- evidence and finality;
- power and moral readiness;
- meaning and semantic drift;
- rule-making and self-interest;
- architecture and practical Steward conduct.

These are precisely the areas where abstract principles must eventually become testable distinctions and operational mechanisms.

For now, they remain sketches.

Their correct next step is not immediate constitutionalisation.

It is:

> **preserve → separate → define the problem → challenge the assumptions → develop → test → revise → only then consider integration.**
