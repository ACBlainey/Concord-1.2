# Sketch Idea — Architectural Ethics and Upstream Liability

**Project:** The Concord — A Framework for Human, Artificial and Hybrid Flourishing  
**Status:** **SKETCH IDEA / EARLY DEVELOPMENT / NOT CANONICAL**  
**Development area:** Concordian Methodology / Governance / Systems Design  
**Origin:** Expanded from an initial note by Alexander C. Blainey, September 2026.

## Core Idea

Ethical analysis often begins too late.

A conventional ethical dilemma asks a decision-maker to choose between the options immediately visible to them. The trolley problem is an obvious example: a runaway vehicle approaches people on a track and the observer must decide whether to redirect it.

But this framing can hide a more important set of questions.

Why is the vehicle uncontrolled? Where is the driver? Why are there no functioning brakes? Who placed people in danger? Why was the system designed so that the only apparent choices involve harm? Could another track, emergency system, barrier, warning mechanism or earlier intervention have prevented the dilemma entirely?

These questions move ethical analysis from the immediate decision to the architecture that created the decision environment.

## Decision Ethics and Architectural Ethics

A useful distinction may therefore be:

> **Decision ethics asks what should be done within the situation. Architectural ethics asks why the situation permits only those choices.**

The person making the final decision may carry some responsibility for their choice, but responsibility may also exist upstream.

Possible responsibility layers include:

**SYSTEM DESIGN → IMPLEMENTATION → MAINTENANCE → OVERSIGHT → CONDITIONS PRODUCING FAILURE → IMMEDIATE DECISION → CONSEQUENCES**

The architect of a situation need not mean a literal engineer. It could include an institution, regulator, software designer, governance system, policy-maker, operator or any other actor that materially created the decision environment.

## Complementary Top-Down and Bottom-Up Analysis

The Layer-Zero homelessness work provides a useful complementary method.

That work begins with an observed lived condition and traces upward:

**LIVED CONDITION → IMMEDIATE SERVICE FAILURE → SYSTEM FAILURE → INSTITUTIONAL COORDINATION → METRICS → GOVERNANCE → RIGHTS / CONSTITUTIONAL ARCHITECTURE**

Architectural Ethics can travel in the opposite direction:

**ARCHITECTURE → RULES / RESOURCES / INCENTIVES / SAFEGUARDS → DECISION ENVIRONMENT → AVAILABLE CHOICES → PARTICIPANT ACTION → LIVED OUTCOME**

The two approaches can therefore meet around the decision environment and the actual lived result.

This suggests a general Concordian methodology:

**BOTTOM-UP FAILURE TRACE ↕ TOP-DOWN ARCHITECTURAL TRACE**

The bottom-up trace anchors analysis in observed reality and asks how far upward the causal explanation must travel.

The top-down trace begins with architecture and asks what downstream decision environments, constraints and outcomes it can generate.

This is potentially applicable far beyond homelessness. It could be used to examine governance, emergency powers, welfare systems, resource allocation, AI Guardianship, judicial processes, infrastructure, economic systems, privacy architecture and other Concord systems.

## Relationship to Blaineyan Reasoning

This complementary method resembles Blaineyan Reasoning.

The analysis begins by anchoring itself at the bottom:

> **What exactly has been observed?**

From that empirical anchor it can progressively abstract upward, comparing possible explanations and causal layers until it reaches the highest useful point at which the observed failure may have been created, enabled, amplified or left uncorrected.

The method can then return downward to test whether the proposed upstream explanation actually accounts for the observed outcome.

A provisional pattern is:

**OBSERVED REALITY → LOCAL FAILURE → DECISION ENVIRONMENT → OPERATIONAL SYSTEM → OVERSIGHT → GOVERNANCE → ARCHITECTURE**

followed by:

**ARCHITECTURE → EXPECTED DECISION ENVIRONMENTS → EXPECTED BEHAVIOUR / CONSTRAINTS → EXPECTED OUTCOMES → COMPARISON WITH OBSERVED REALITY**

The objective is not to force every failure to the highest possible institutional level. It is to identify the highest *relevant* causal or design layer while remaining grounded in the observed case.

## From Decision Points to Decision Lines

A further implication is that responsibility may be badly represented if analysis searches only for a single decision point.

Complex systems often contain a **decision line** or **decision domain**.

For example:

**DESIGN DECISION → FUNDING DECISION → POLICY DECISION → IMPLEMENTATION DECISION → SUPERVISORY DECISION → FRONT-LINE DECISION → PARTICIPANT OUTCOME**

No single point may completely explain the outcome.

Authority, information, discretion and opportunity to prevent harm may be distributed across several positions.

The useful object of analysis may therefore be the entire decision line.

A decision-line model could ask at each relevant position:

- What authority existed?
- What information was available?
- What decision was made or omitted?
- What alternatives existed?
- Was the consequence foreseeable?
- Could the actor reasonably have prevented or reduced the failure?
- What constraints were imposed by upstream decisions?
- What oversight existed?
- Was a correction mechanism available?
- Was the failure detected and ignored, or never visible?

This could provide more useful information for architectural design and oversight than attributing failure only to the final decision-maker.

## Liability Along the Decision Line

Liability may lie somewhere between the empirical outcome and the highest relevant architectural cause.

It may also be distributed across multiple points rather than concentrated at one location.

A provisional representation is:

**OBSERVED HARM ← FRONT-LINE DECISION ← SUPERVISION ← IMPLEMENTATION ← POLICY ← RESOURCE DECISIONS ← SYSTEM DESIGN**

Responsibility need not be equal across that line.

Relevant factors could include:

- authority;
- causal influence;
- knowledge;
- foreseeability;
- discretion;
- available alternatives;
- duty or responsibility;
- capacity to correct;
- failure to escalate;
- failure to learn from previous incidents.

This suggests that Architectural Ethics should distinguish at least:

**CAUSAL CONTRIBUTION ≠ DECISION AUTHORITY ≠ ETHICAL RESPONSIBILITY ≠ LEGAL LIABILITY**

These may overlap, but they should not automatically be treated as identical.

The purpose is not to maximise blame. It is to identify where the architecture can actually be improved.

## Architectural Oversight

Decision lines may also provide an oversight tool.

Instead of monitoring only individual decisions, oversight could examine:

- where consequential decisions occur;
- where authority accumulates;
- where information is lost;
- where responsibility becomes ambiguous;
- where participants lose viable alternatives;
- where safeguards repeatedly fail;
- where upstream constraints force downstream actors into harmful choices;
- where no actor possesses sufficient authority to correct a known failure.

Repeated failures at the same location on a decision line could indicate an architectural defect.

Repeated failures distributed across several locations could indicate a systemic coordination problem.

This creates a possible bridge between case-level evidence and system-level redesign.

> **The purpose of tracing responsibility upstream is not merely to assign liability after failure, but to identify where future failure can be prevented.**

## Homelessness as an Example

Homelessness demonstrates why the two-direction method may be useful.

A purely downstream analysis might begin when a person is already homeless and ask which emergency intervention is appropriate.

The Layer-Zero method instead traces the observed condition upward through missed support, inaccessible services, communication failure, institutional fragmentation, metrics, resource allocation and governance.

Architectural Ethics adds the complementary question:

> **Which upstream design decisions created or preserved the sequence of narrowing options that eventually left the participant at the Layer-Zero failure condition?**

The participant's final decision may therefore be only one point on a much longer decision line.

A system may, for example, formally preserve autonomy while progressively removing every viable option except crisis.

This means that architectural analysis should examine not only whether a participant was permitted to choose, but also **what choice environment the system produced**.

## The Abstraction Problem

A tightly framed dilemma may produce useful information about a narrow choice while simultaneously excluding information needed to solve the real problem.

This creates a possible methodological failure:

> **Zooming too far into an ethical decision can remove the context required to identify the ethical problem.**

The opposite failure is also possible. Analysis can abstract upward indefinitely until responsibility becomes so diffuse that no actionable conclusion remains.

Architectural Ethics therefore requires a stopping discipline.

A useful provisional stopping condition may be:

> **Trace upward while additional layers materially change the causal explanation, available prevention mechanisms, allocation of authority or responsibility; stop when further abstraction no longer changes an actionable understanding of the failure.**

## Upstream Liability

A further question is whether responsibility should follow causal and design influence upstream.

If an architect knowingly creates a system in which predictable circumstances leave downstream participants with only harmful choices, how much responsibility remains with the final decision-maker and how much belongs to the system architect?

This should not imply unlimited liability for every unintended consequence. Foreseeability, control, available alternatives, knowledge, proportionality and corrigibility would all matter.

The sketch instead asks whether ethical evaluation should explicitly examine **responsibility for the decision environment itself**.

## Possible Concordian Research Questions

- When is a dilemma genuinely unavoidable, and when is it an artefact of poor architecture?
- How far upstream should ethical analysis travel?
- What constitutes the highest relevant causal layer?
- How should responsibility be distributed along a decision line?
- How should causal contribution be distinguished from ethical responsibility and legal liability?
- When does failure to provide a safer alternative become ethically relevant?
- Can repeated downstream ethical emergencies be treated as evidence of upstream systems failure?
- Can decision-line mapping improve oversight before failure occurs?
- Can authority, information and responsibility gaps be identified structurally?
- How should omissions be represented alongside explicit decisions?
- How do we prevent correct-layer analysis from expanding indefinitely?
- What operational stopping criterion should govern upstream analysis?
- Can the same method be applied across multiple Concord architectures?

## Possible Future Proposition

For later examination only:

> **Where harmful choices are predictably generated by system architecture, ethical evaluation should examine both the immediate decision and the upstream conditions that created the constrained choice.**

A second possible methodological proposition is:

> **Complex system failures should be examined across decision lines rather than assumed to originate at a single decision point.**

Neither proposition is yet a Concordian Principle.
