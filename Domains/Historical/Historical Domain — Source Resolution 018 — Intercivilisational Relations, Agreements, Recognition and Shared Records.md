**Project:** The Concord

**Status:** ACTIVE DOMAIN RESOLUTION / INTERCIVILISATIONAL PASS

## 1. Purpose

This pass examines Historical requirements created when Concord participants, institutions or civilisation-level systems interact across civilisational boundaries.

Primary V1.2 sources include:

- `11_Intercivilisational_Architecture/README.md`;
- `Concord Civilisational Wrappers — Foundational Boundary, Mobility and Responsibility Architecture.md`;
- `Concord Wrapper Procedural Architecture — Context, Information, Consent and Autonomy Across Civilisational Boundaries.md`;
- `Functionally Bounded Summits — Failure-Contained Negotiation for Hostile and Low-Trust Interoperability.md`;
- `Cross-Boundary Externality Recognition and Responsibility Mechanism.md`;
- `Emergent Polities, Sentient Secession and Recognition.md`;
- related wrapper, recognition, peaceful-distance, jurisdiction and responsibility architecture.

The core Historical problem is unusual:

> **A shared event can have more than one legitimate civilisational record.**

Historical must preserve Concord's own record without silently claiming ownership of another civilisation's history.

---

## 2. Intercivilisational Historical Custody Does Not Create Supracivilisational Authority

The Intercivilisational Architecture README states:

> **The Concord provides the tools; adoption of those tools does not transfer authority to the Concord over their users.**

The same boundary applies to Historical.

If another civilisation uses a Concord wrapper, protocol or record schema, Historical does not thereby acquire authority over:

- that civilisation's archives;
- its interpretation of events;
- its internal records;
- its historical narrative;
- its retention policy.

Candidate principle:

> **Intercivilisational Historical Interoperability ≠ Supracivilisational Historical Authority.**

---

## 3. A Shared Event May Produce Multiple Records

An interaction between Civilisation A and Civilisation B can generate:

- A's internal record;
- B's internal record;
- a jointly agreed record;
- technical telemetry;
- third-party observations;
- later interpretations.

These records may disagree.

Historical should not force them into one false canonical narrative.

Candidate principle:

> **Shared Event ≠ Single Historical Perspective.**

---

## 4. Agreement on a Record Is Different From Agreement on Interpretation

Two civilisations may agree that:

- a meeting occurred;
- a message was transmitted;
- a border crossing occurred;
- an agreement was signed;
- a resource transfer occurred.

They may still disagree about:

- motive;
- legitimacy;
- causation;
- responsibility;
- legal meaning;
- political significance.

Therefore:

> **Shared Factual Record ≠ Shared Historical Interpretation.**

This is a direct intercivilisational extension of Historical 017.

---

## 5. Communication Before Recognition Creates Historical State

Emergent Polities states:

> **Diplomatic Communication ≠ Sovereign Recognition**

and:

> **Communication Interface ≠ Treaty Recognition.**

Historical must preserve this distinction.

A communication with an unrecognised polity is evidence that communication occurred.

It is not evidence that sovereignty was recognised.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Record of Communication ≠ Historical Record of Recognition.**

---

## 6. Recognition Has Resolution

The recognition architecture distinguishes:

- sentient community;
- political representative;
- autonomous polity;
- sovereign state.

Historical must preserve the level of recognition in force at the time.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Recognition Must Preserve Its Resolution.**

A later recognition decision must not be projected backward.

---

## 7. Recognition Is Corrigible

The source architecture explicitly requires recognition to remain reviewable as evidence and conditions change.

Historical should preserve:

`Recognition State A → Evidence/Conditions → Review → Recognition State B`.

Candidate principle:

> **Later Recognition ≠ Earlier Recognition.**

Likewise:

> **Withdrawal or Revision of Recognition ≠ Historical Erasure of Prior Recognition.**

Candidate principle:

> **Shared-System History Must Preserve Responsibility Allocation, Not Merely System Ownership.**

## 40. Historical Access Rules May Conflict Across Civilisations

Civilisation A may require long retention.

Civilisation B may require deletion.

A shared record may involve both.

This exposes a genuine unresolved problem:

- whose retention rule applies?
- can shared facts be deleted unilaterally?
- can one party preserve its own copy?
- what if participant privacy rights differ?
- what if one civilisation later withdraws consent?
- what if the record is accountability-critical?

No complete answer was source-resolved.

Candidate principle:

> **Shared Historical Record ≠ Automatically Shared Retention Rule.**

This requires future intercivilisational protocol work.

## 41. Unilateral Erasure Is a Cross-Boundary Anti-Capture Risk

Where a record proves:

- agreement;
- responsibility;
- harm;
- transfer;
- warning;
- commitment;

allowing the implicated party to erase all surviving evidence would defeat accountability.

But preventing erasure can conflict with autonomy, sovereignty and privacy.

This is therefore not reducible to simple permanent retention.

Candidate requirement:

> **Accountability-Critical Shared Records Need a Retention Architecture That Does Not Depend Solely on the Continuing Consent of the Party Whose Conduct They Evidence.**

Exact implementation remains unresolved.

## 52. Current Finding

Intercivilisational history cannot safely be modelled as one civilisation's ordinary Historical system made larger.

The boundary changes the architecture.

Historical must support:

- multiple legitimate record holders;
- asymmetric access;
- disputed interpretation;
- translation;
- changing recognition;
- shared but bounded agreements;
- distributed custody;
- conflicting retention rules;
- non-participation;
- peaceful distance;
- domain-specific relationships.

The deepest finding is that **shared history does not require a shared historical authority**.

Two civilisations can preserve a verifiable common record of an event while retaining different internal archives, different interpretations and different political identities.

The strongest formulation from this pass is:

> **Civilisations may share a history without surrendering ownership of their histories to one another.**
