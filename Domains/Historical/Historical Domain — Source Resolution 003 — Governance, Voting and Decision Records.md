## 1. Purpose

This pass examines Governance, voting and decision records as the next test of the emerging Historical Domain boundary.

It follows:

- `Historical Domain — Source Resolution 001.md`, which identified KCS, protected access, permission and Continuity as major existing components of the Historical function; and
- `Historical Domain — Source Resolution 002 — Judiciary and Evidence.md`, which produced the candidate rule:

> **Preserve the Record; Preserve Its Provenance; Preserve Its Historical State; Do Not Inherit the Authority of the System That Produced It.**

The purpose of this pass is to determine whether Governance independently requires the same custody-without-authority architecture.

## 3. Governance Explicitly Requires Durable Decision Records

Governance V2 states that every significant governance decision should produce a decision record.

The record may contain:

- proposal;
- authority;
- participants;
- votes;
- abstentions;
- conflicts;
- recusals;
- evidence relied upon;
- principal arguments;
- decision;
- implementation instructions;
- metrics;
- review date;
- expected outcome;
- alternatives and risk analysis for higher-consequence decisions;
- dependencies;
- second-order effects;
- dissenting reasoning;
- and material AI contribution where applicable.

Its purpose is explicitly reconstructive: future reviewers should be able to determine not merely what happened, but why it happened under the information and authority available at the time.

This is a direct Historical requirement.

However:

> **Historical Custody of a Governance Decision ≠ Governance Authority**

Historical can preserve the decision object and its context without becoming entitled to make, reverse, reinterpret or implement the decision.

A provisional lifecycle is:

**Proposal / Deliberation → Governance Decision → Operational Implementation → Review / Revision → Historical Custody of Decision State and Provenance**

The exact archival threshold may vary by decision class.

## 4. A Vote Is an Exercise of Authority Where It Changes State

Governance V2 explicitly treats a vote as an action where it changes system state.

This is important for Historical because a voting record is therefore not merely a statistical artefact. It may be part of the provenance of an exercise of delegated constitutional authority.

Governance also distinguishes:

- For;
- Against;
- Abstain;
- absence;
- conflict;
- recusal;
- and insufficient decision threshold.

These states must not be flattened into a generic result.

Where practicable, the reason for abstention may itself be recorded because repeated abstention can become diagnostic information.

Historical preservation should therefore be capable of retaining the decision-relevant distinctions that existed at the time.

> **Historical Preservation of a Vote ≠ Historical Authority to Recast the Vote**

The archive must not silently convert abstention into consent, absence into abstention, minority position into disappearance, or a failed threshold into an authorised state change.

## 5. Minority Reasoning Is Explicitly Historical Information

Governance V2 states that a minority vote should not disappear merely because it loses.

The reason is epistemic rather than political endorsement. Minority reasoning may contain:

- a real risk;
- an alternative interpretation;
- a hidden dependency;
- a future failure mode;
- or evidence later shown to matter.

Governance explicitly warns against allowing governance history to become a record written only by the winning position.

This strongly converges with the Judiciary result.

In both systems:

> **Preservation Is Not Endorsement**

and:

> **Unresolved Disagreement Is Information**

This suggests that preservation of dissent/minority reasoning is not merely a Judiciary-specific archival preference. It is a cross-domain Historical requirement wherever later learning may depend on reconstructing what alternatives were visible at the time.

## 6. Decision, Implementation and Outcome Must Remain Distinguishable

Governance V2 separates the authorised decision from implementation.

Implementation records may need to preserve:

- originating decision;
- responsible actor/system;
- resources authorised;
- execution time;
- resulting state;
- deviations;
- errors;
- corrective actions;
- metrics;
- and review conditions.

This creates an important Historical chain:

**Proposal → Decision → Authorisation → Implementation → Result → Measurement → Review → Revision / Continuation / Reversal**

These objects should not collapse into one another.

A failed outcome does not prove the original reasoning was irrational.

A failed implementation does not prove the authorised policy was followed.

A later revision does not prove the earlier decision never existed.

Historical must therefore preserve enough typed relationship and temporal context to reconstruct these distinctions.

## 7. Audit Requires Independent, Recoverable Records

Governance decisions are explicitly expected to be independently auditable.

Audit may examine:

- whether authority existed;
- whether procedure was followed;
- whether evidence was recorded;
- whether conflicts were disclosed;
- whether votes were accurately recorded;
- whether implementation matched the decision;
- whether resources matched authorisation;
- whether review occurred;
- and whether jurisdiction was respected.

This makes durable records a dependency of meaningful audit.

If Governance alone can rewrite or selectively remove the evidence by which Governance is audited, audit becomes structurally weaker.

Governance V2 later states this more directly: an institution controlling its own history may selectively preserve successes and approved narratives while suppressing failure, dissent, criticism and discarded alternatives.

It concludes that independent preservation contributes to resistance against institutional capture.

This is strong support for Historical as an independent custody function.

> **The System Being Audited Should Not Possess Unilateral Control Over the Survival of the Record Required to Audit It.**

This is a candidate Historical anti-capture principle.

## 8. Decision Versioning Is Already Required

Governance V2 requires preservation of materially relevant versions.

A version record may identify:

- previous version;
- new version;
- change;
- rationale;
- effective date;
- affected systems;
- migration requirements;
- review conditions;
- author/authority;
- evidence;
- and superseding version.

Governance explicitly states that a current canonical version may replace an older version operationally without erasing it historically.

This is almost a direct statement of the Historical-domain boundary:

> **Operational Supersession ≠ Historical Erasure**

Historical therefore needs to preserve both:

- which version is/was operationally authoritative at a given time; and
- the historical lineage linking prior and subsequent states.

Historical custody must not create operational ambiguity. The existence of an archived earlier rule does not make it currently operative.

## 9. Governance Already Distinguishes Provenance From Authority and Truth

Governance V2 contains a developed provenance architecture.

Decision provenance links, where appropriate:

- originating proposal;
- evidence;
- amendments;
- deliberation;
- votes;
- minority reasoning;
- implementation;
- metrics;
- review;
- and later versions.

Evidence provenance may preserve:

- source;
- collection method;
- modification;
- analysis;
- chain of custody;
- and distinctions among original source, copied material, transformed data, summary, model output, inference, prediction and official interpretation.

AI provenance may additionally preserve system, role, output, review, modifications and responsibility.

Governance also explicitly states:

> **Provenance tells us where something came from. It does not tell us automatically whether it is correct.**

This aligns directly with the Historical-domain separation already emerging from KCS and Judiciary.

Historical preserves provenance.

Historical does not convert provenance into truth.

Historical preserves Governance decisions.

Historical does not convert those decisions into scientific truth, constitutional truth or permanent precedent.

## 10. Governance Requires Memory but Does Not Need to Own the Memory Substrate

Governance V2 explicitly states that Governance requires memory.

Without reliable institutional memory, civilisation risks:

- recreating old failures;
- losing successful solutions;
- misremembering decisions;
- misattributing ideas;
- and becoming dependent upon oral institutional culture.

It also states that knowledge preservation systems should distinguish:

- active knowledge;
- historical knowledge;
- superseded material;
- disputed material;
- experimental material;
- speculative material;
- and rejected material.

This supports the same composition found in Source Resolution 001:

**Governance produces and uses governance records → KCS/Historical preserves durable states and provenance → Governance retrieves relevant history when making or reviewing decisions.**

Governance does not need to become the civilisation-wide archive merely because it depends upon historical memory.

## 11. Governance History Is Explicitly Evidence

Governance V2 states:

> **Governance history is therefore not merely archival. It is evidence.**

Historical records may reveal:

- repeated policy failure;
- recurring institutional assumptions;
- successful safeguards;
- changes in public conditions;
- long-term unintended effects;
- and repeated failure to respond to evidence.

This creates a critical two-way interface.

**Governance → Historical:** completed decisions, provenance, dissent, versions, implementation and outcomes become durable records.

**Historical → Governance:** authorised historical retrieval supplies evidence for comparison, learning, audit and correction.

Historical therefore is not a dead repository. It is a protected memory substrate capable of returning bounded historical evidence without becoming the decision-maker.

## 12. Failed, Rejected and Superseded Governance Material Has Historical Value

Governance V2 explicitly rejects deletion as the default response to supersession.

Failed ideas should preserve why they failed.

Superseded policies may remain useful evidence of:

- developmental history;
- earlier assumptions;
- old failure modes;
- constitutional reasoning;
- and alternative mechanisms.

The operational system should clearly identify the current state, but Historical should preserve the path by which the current state arose.

This strengthens a candidate Historical object-state distinction:

**Current / Active ≠ Historically Important**

and:

**Superseded / Rejected ≠ Valueless**

This is consistent with KCS.

## 13. Independent Preservation Is an Anti-Capture Mechanism

Governance V2 explicitly identifies selective institutional memory as a capture risk.

If an institution controls its own historical record, it may preserve:

- successes;
- favourable interpretations;
- official narratives;
- approved precedents;

while suppressing:

- failure;
- dissent;
- criticism;
- discarded alternatives.

Independent preservation therefore has constitutional significance beyond archival convenience.

Historical can provide a structural separation between:

**authority to act**

and:

**unilateral authority to rewrite the surviving evidence of how that authority was exercised.**

This is one of the strongest findings of this pass.

## 14. Secrecy Does Not Cancel Historical Custody

Governance V2 distinguishes public visibility from protected information and later states explicitly that some classified material may eventually become historical evidence.

It states that records should not be destroyed merely because they remain secret.

Declassification may later support:

- accountability;
- scholarship;
- institutional learning;
- and public trust.

This supports the Historical model developed in Source Resolution 001:

**Historical Custody ≠ Public Accessibility**

A governance record may be:

- historically preserved;
- access-restricted;
- classified;
- sealed;
- redacted for a particular retrieval;
- later declassified;
- or subject to another legitimate retention/access state.

Historical therefore needs independent preservation and access-state dimensions.

## 15. Governance–Historical Boundary

### Historical candidate responsibilities

- preserve completed/significant governance decision records;
- preserve decision provenance;
- preserve vote-state distinctions where legitimate;
- preserve abstention/conflict/recusal information subject to privacy and access rules;
- preserve minority/dissenting reasoning;
- preserve evidence provenance associated with governance records;
- preserve implementation and outcome relationships;
- preserve audit-relevant records;
- preserve decision/policy versions and effective-time context;
- preserve failed, rejected and superseded governance material with appropriate state labels;
- preserve protected/classified governance records without equating custody with publication;
- preserve consequential AI-contribution provenance where supplied;
- return authorised, purpose-bounded historical records and derivatives;
- preserve enough record integrity that later Governance, Audit or Judiciary can reconstruct consequential decisions.

### Governance responsibilities retained

- propose policy;
- deliberate;
- exercise delegated governance authority;
- vote;
- determine governance decisions within jurisdiction;
- set implementation instructions;
- determine operational policy;
- review outcomes;
- reconsider/revise decisions;
- define legitimate governance confidentiality/classification within constitutional limits;
- determine which governance records are operationally active;
- initiate constitutionally legitimate declassification/reclassification processes where Governance has that authority;
- act upon historical evidence.

Historical therefore supports Governance without absorbing it.

## 16. Voting and Ballot Custody: Partially Resolved, Not Fully Resolved

This pass strongly resolves **governance voting records** as part of decision provenance.

It does **not** yet establish a complete architecture for **ballot custody** in the broader electoral sense.

The source examined clearly supports preservation of votes cast by authorised governance participants where those votes form part of a governance decision record.

It does not, from this pass alone, establish:

- custody of citizen secret ballots;
- identity separation for anonymous/secret voting;
- election recount architecture;
- ballot destruction/retention thresholds;
- cryptographic election records;
- public-verifiability rules;
- or the boundary between electoral administration and Historical.

Therefore:

> **Governance Vote Record ≠ Automatically Resolved Electoral Ballot Archive**

Electoral/secret-ballot custody remains a separate source-resolution target if such architecture exists elsewhere in the Concord.

This distinction prevents the Historical-domain exercise from silently inventing an election architecture not established by the source.

## 17. New Candidate Historical Principles

This pass strengthens the Source Resolution 002 principle:

> **Preserve the Record; Preserve Its Provenance; Preserve Its Historical State; Do Not Inherit the Authority of the System That Produced It.**

It also suggests three additional candidate principles for testing against further domains:

> **Operational Supersession Must Not Require Historical Erasure.**

> **The System Being Audited Should Not Possess Unilateral Control Over the Survival of the Record Required to Audit It.**

> **Restriction From Public View Must Not Become Disappearance From Civilisational Memory or Accountability.**

These should remain candidates until tested against privacy, deletion, safety, self-stewardship and other relevant systems.

## 18. ESCP Consequence

The Governance source itself reinforces the need for ESCP-aware domain resolution.

A current decision record preserves what was known, considered and represented at the time.

Later evidence may expose a dimension absent from that original evaluation space.

Historical preservation allows civilisation to compare:

**What was represented then**

with:

**What became visible later.**

Erasing superseded reasoning, minority concerns, failed predictions or old evidence would damage the ability to perform that comparison.

Historical therefore may become one of the practical civilisational safeguards against recursive evaluation-space closure.

This does not make Historical an epistemic authority.

It preserves evidence from which later evaluators may discover that an earlier evaluation space was incomplete.

## 20. Current Finding

Governance strongly confirms the Historical Domain hypothesis.

The Governance source independently requires:

- durable decision records;
- vote-state preservation;
- minority reasoning;
- decision and evidence provenance;
- implementation traceability;
- auditability;
- version history;
- preservation of failed/rejected/superseded material;
- independent institutional memory;
- historical evidence;
- protected/secret records that do not disappear;
- and separation between provenance, truth and authority.

Most importantly, Governance itself identifies **independent preservation** as protection against institutional capture.

The custody-without-authority rule therefore survives its second major cross-domain test:

**Judiciary creates and uses judicial records → Historical preserves them → Judiciary retains judicial authority.**

**Governance creates and uses governance records → Historical preserves them → Governance retains governance authority.**

This materially strengthens Historical as a genuine cross-domain civil function rather than a repository category.
