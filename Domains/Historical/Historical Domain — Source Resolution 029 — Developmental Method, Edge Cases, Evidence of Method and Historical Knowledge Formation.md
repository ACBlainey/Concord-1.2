The central question is:

> **If a civilisation preserves a conclusion, what must also survive for the future to understand how that conclusion emerged, what it was evidence of, and what it was never evidence of?**

---

## 2. Development History Is Evidence

The Empirical Evidence README explicitly treats the development of the Concord itself as an object of study.

It distinguishes:

- evidence that a methodology was used;
- evidence that a system works;
- evidence that a principle is correct.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Evidence of Method Use ≠ Historical Evidence of Method Effectiveness ≠ Historical Evidence of Conclusion Truth.**

This distinction should survive indefinitely where such records matter.

---

## 3. Process Evidence Is Its Own Evidential Class

A development history can demonstrate that:

- a question was asked;
- a method was applied;
- an edge case exposed a failure;
- architecture was revised.

It does not necessarily demonstrate that the resulting system works in reality.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Process Evidence ≠ Historical Outcome Evidence.**

---

## 4. Methodological Generativity Is Not Operational Validation

The homelessness case demonstrates that Concord methods generated new questions, safeguards and cross-system principles.

The source explicitly says this is evidence of methodological generativity, not operational efficacy.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Generativity of a Method ≠ Historical Validation of Its Products.**

This is a useful safeguard against retrospective inflation of successful-looking development history.

---

## 5. Development Can Produce Knowledge Through Failure

The case begins with a practical problem, applies existing methods, discovers a failure mode and revises the architecture.

Historical chain:

`Problem → Observation → Method → Interpretation → Edge Case → Failure → Revision → Candidate Principle → Retest`.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Development Knowledge Can Reside in the Failure-and-Revision Path, Not Merely the Final Architecture.**

This reinforces Source Resolutions 009, 010, 020 and 027.

---

## 6. Edge Cases Are Historical Probes

The source states that normal cases test whether assumptions hold, while edge cases reveal the assumptions themselves.

Historical implication:

> **Historical Outlier ≠ Merely Low-Frequency Record.**

An outlier may expose the hidden model under which the majority of cases were interpreted.

---

## 7. Rare Cases Can Have High Architectural Information Value

A single unusual case may change an architecture more than thousands of normal cases.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Frequency ≠ Historical Architectural Information Value.**

This connects directly to the Humanity Machine and relational-value work.

---

## 8. The Historical Importance of an Event Can Be Its Effect on the Model

Some events matter historically because they caused civilisation to revise how it understood something.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Significance Can Reside in Model Change Caused by an Event, Not Merely in the Event's Direct Scale.**

---

## 9. The Edge Case Can Reveal an Invisible Assumption

In the homelessness example, declining income, housing size and consumption could be interpreted as deterioration, but retirement provides an alternative explanation.

The metrics may be correct while the interpretation is wrong.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Metric Accuracy ≠ Historical Interpretive Accuracy.**

Already supported elsewhere, but the development case shows how a concrete anomaly exposes the assumption.

---

## 10. Historical Should Preserve the Assumption That Failed

If only the corrected model survives, the future may know that architecture changed but not what error caused the change.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Correction Should Preserve the Material Assumption or Model Defect That the Correction Addressed.**

---

## 11. The Triggering Case and the General Principle Are Different Objects

The homelessness case generated principles applicable far beyond homelessness.

Historical should distinguish:

- source problem;
- abstraction process;
- extracted principle;
- later applications.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Origin Context ≠ Historical Scope of a Later Generalised Principle.**

---

## 12. Generalisation Has a Provenance Path

A principle can travel:

`Specific Case → Candidate Generalisation → Cross-Domain Test → Revised Generalisation → Broader Architecture`.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Generalisation Should Preserve the Path by Which Scope Was Expanded.**

This guards against making a later broad principle appear self-evident from the beginning.

---

## 13. Later Generality Must Not Be Projected Backward

A principle discovered in one context may later prove broadly useful.

Candidate principle:

> **Later Demonstrated Generality ≠ Earlier Demonstrated Generality.**

Historical should time-index the evidence supporting scope.

---

## 14. Recursive Return Is Historical Information

The source explicitly requires a new general principle to return to the original concrete case for testing.

This creates a loop rather than a one-way abstraction.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Development Lineage Can Be Recursive Rather Than Merely Forward-Sequential.**

The history is therefore a graph containing return edges.

---

## 15. The Path From Reality to Abstraction and Back Is Part of the Knowledge

The case moves:

`Concrete Problem → Abstraction → Principle → Return to Concrete Problem → Test → Revision`.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Preservation of a Principle Without Its Grounding-and-Return Path Can Lose Evidence About Why the Principle Was Considered Applicable.**

---

## 16. Candidate Principles Must Remain Candidate Principles

The source explicitly warns that generated principles should not silently become constitutional constraints.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Survival of a Candidate Principle ≠ Historical Constitutional Adoption.**

This is familiar but especially important in development archives.

---

## 17. Evidence and Conclusion Must Remain Separable

The Empirical Evidence README states:

> **Evidence is preserved separately from conclusions so that the two can be evaluated independently.**

Historical should preserve that separation.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Evidence Object ≠ Historical Conclusion Object.**

---

## 18. Evidence Status Can Change Without the Underlying Record Changing

The same experiment may later be:

- reinterpreted;
- replicated;
- contradicted;
- found methodologically weak.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Evidence Record Can Remain Fixed While Historical Evidential Weight Changes.**

Therefore evidential weight is time-indexed interpretive state, not intrinsic immutable metadata.

---

## 19. Development Corpus Can Contain Embedded Evidence

The Empirical Evidence README explicitly searches old development history for already-existing methodological evidence.

This introduces a useful distinction.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Evidence Can Be Recognised After the Event Without Having Been Created After the Event.**

The record existed; its evidential significance was identified later.

---

## 20. Later Recognition Must Preserve Discovery Time

Candidate principle:

> **Time of Evidence Production ≠ Time of Recognition as Evidence.**

This parallels Source Resolution 014A's pattern-discovery distinction.

---

## 21. Retrospective Evidence Extraction Has Selection Risk

If later researchers mine old development records for examples supporting a method, they may preferentially select successful-looking cases.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Retrospective Evidence Extraction Requires Provenance of Selection Criteria.**

Otherwise development history can become curated confirmation.

---

## 22. Negative Development Paths Matter

The Empirical Evidence README includes hypotheses being challenged, rejected or transformed.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Development Evidence Includes Abandoned and Rejected Paths, Not Only Productive Paths.**

This is essential to evaluating whether apparent generativity reflects genuine search or retrospective success selection.

---

## 23. The Development Archive Can Reveal Search Behaviour

A sequence of drafts and abandoned branches can show:

- which alternatives were considered;
- how quickly they were discarded;
- what evidence caused revision;
- whether counterexamples were sought.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Search Process Can Be Evidence About the Epistemic Quality of Development Without Proving the Result Correct.**

---

## 24. Messiness Can Be Historical Information

The `SorryForTheMess.md` note records that the repository is living, reorganised, incomplete, and subject to file movement and later upload.

This is not merely housekeeping.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Repository Disorder Can Be Provenance About the Development State of the Project.**

A polished later structure should not be projected backward onto an earlier messy research state.

## 25. Reorganisation Can Hide Developmental Sequence

Files may move to their natural home after their conceptual role becomes clear.

Candidate principle:

> **Later Logical Placement ≠ Original Developmental Placement.**

Historical should preserve enough structural provenance to reconstruct this where material.

This reinforces Source Resolution 010.

## 26. Incomplete Corpus State Is Historical State

## 27. Later Completeness Must Not Be Projected Backward

Candidate principle:

This matters when evaluating what a contemporary contributor could actually have known.

Old drafts, rejected paths and intermediate reasoning can be disproportionately useful for reconstructing knowledge formation.

Candidate principle:

> **Historical Development Retention Should Preserve Material Turning Points, Alternatives, Failures and Scope Changes Rather Than Every Edit by Default.**

## 47. Relationship to ESCP

This pass exposes additional ESCP failures:

- evidence that a method was used can be mistaken for evidence that it works;
- methodological generativity can be mistaken for operational validation;
- successful development paths can obscure abandoned paths;
- final architecture can hide the failed assumptions that generated safeguards;
- later generality can be projected backward;
- later validation can be projected backward;
- a polished current repository can be mistaken for the historical corpus available at the time;
- similarity can be mistaken for independent convergence;
- a final conclusion can be mistaken for the complete reasoning process that produced it.

Therefore:

> **Historical Knowledge Completeness Requires Enough of the formation process to distinguish what was observed, what was inferred, what failed, what changed, and what scope was justified at each stage.**

---

## 48. Source-Resolution Result

This pass does not expose a wholly new Historical domain.

It does, however, strengthen a major emerging conclusion:

> **The history of knowledge is not the history of conclusions.**

The Historical Domain must sometimes preserve how a civilisation learned:

- what problem it thought it was solving;
- what evidence it possessed;
- which methods it used;
- which assumptions failed;
- which edge cases exposed those failures;
- how scope expanded;
- what remained uncertain.

The strongest new element is the explicit distinction between **process evidence**, **outcome evidence**, and **truth claims**.

A development corpus can prove that a method generated revisions without proving those revisions correct.

---

## 49. Strongest Formulation

> **A civilisation that preserves only what it finally believed may lose the evidence of how it learned to believe it.**

And:

> **The failed assumptions, awkward edge cases and abandoned branches of development can be part of the machinery by which later knowledge became possible.**
